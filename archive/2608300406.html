Measles Deaths · RFK Jr.

Two measles deaths in Pennsylvania set off a public clash between RFK Jr. and Gov. Shapiro over whether they were even real

+

The Pennsylvania Department of Health confirmed two measles-associated deaths in unvaccinated Lancaster County residents — the state’s first measles deaths in roughly 35 years and, per multiple Tier-1 outlets, the first reported measles deaths in the U.S. this year. One was a newborn; the state reported the infant was born to a mother with measles and that post-mortem lung tissue tested positive for infection acquired before birth. State Health Secretary Debra Bogen said she could “sadly confirm” the deaths, which fall within a Lancaster-centered outbreak that state figures put near 393 cases across 28 counties, as national 2026 case totals surpassed last year’s tally — the highest since 1991.

Beginning around Aug. 26, Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. publicly questioned the deaths, saying they “may even have been altogether fabricated” by a staffer of the governor and citing the Lancaster County coroner as having “no record” of measles deaths. Gov. Josh Shapiro accused Kennedy of spreading vaccine misinformation. Where the accounts diverge is causation and classification: the county coroner and a commissioner dispute that the infant died of measles rather than with it, which Kennedy amplified into a suggestion of fabrication, while Tier-1 desks agree both people were unvaccinated, both had confirmed measles infection, and the state stands by its “measles-associated” label.

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads this as the predictable, lethal cost of installing an anti-vaccine activist atop HHS: children are dying in a preventable outbreak and the Health Secretary’s instinct is to attack a governor and cast doubt on the death reports rather than urge vaccination. Kennedy’s “fabricated” insinuation, they argue, models the exact misinformation driving MMR rates down, and several voices renew calls for his removal. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

No in-window institutional-right editorial surfaced; the conservative commentary that did publish (RedState, HotAir) is directional and defends Kennedy’s skepticism — framing an ambitious Democratic governor as blurring “died with measles” into “died of measles” for political effect and echoing what these writers call the COVID-era weaponization of disease fear, while pointing to the coroner’s office and disclosure gaps. (Directional; no signed mainstream-right editorial verified.)

Immigration · Milo Deported

ICE arrests and deports Milo Yiannopoulos — a vocal champion of mass deportations — for overstaying his U.S. visa

+

The Department of Homeland Security said Milo Yiannopoulos, the British far-right commentator and former Yeezy executive, was deported to the United Kingdom, one day after ICE arrested him at Louis Armstrong New Orleans International Airport. According to ICE and Tier-1 reporting, Yiannopoulos entered the U.S. legally in May 2019 and subsequently overstayed; an immigration judge issued a final order of removal on July 22, 2026, after he failed to appear at a hearing. He was arrested Aug. 27 and removed around Aug. 28.

Coverage widely noted the irony: Yiannopoulos had publicly championed President Trump’s deportation agenda, including a 2025 post urging that “millions and millions” be deported. Reporting also situated the arrest amid a long-running feud with Trump ally Laura Loomer, who had called for his removal and celebrated it publicly — though outlets present the feud as context rather than cause.

The Left-Leaning Take

Left and liberal commentary treats the episode as pure schadenfreude and a self-indictment of the movement: a man who demanded the mass removal of immigrants was himself an overstayer swept up by the machinery he cheered, illustrating — in this telling — the arbitrary, status-blind cruelty of the enforcement surge and the hypocrisy of its loudest boosters. (Directional.)

It doubles as an intra-MAGA settling of scores, with Loomer taking a visible victory lap and no organized right-of-center defense of Yiannopoulos emerging. (Directional.)

Mail-Voting Order · Courts

Mail-voting whiplash continues: days after the Supreme Court cleared Trump’s order, a Boston judge again freezes the Postal Service’s plan to enforce it

+

President Trump’s March 2026 executive order restructuring mail voting directs DHS to build federal citizenship lists, requires states to submit mail-ballot recipient lists through a new online system, and lets the U.S. Postal Service decline to deliver mail ballots in non-complying states while imposing new envelope-design and tracking rules. A coalition of 23 states and D.C., led by California, challenged it on Elections Clause federalism grounds. On Aug. 24 the Supreme Court, dividing 6-3 along ideological lines, lifted a June injunction against key parts of the order — but the majority expressly declined to rule that any specific implementation measure is lawful, addressing only whether the injunction was premature.

The freshest beats landed Aug. 28. After USPS released a roughly 95-page implementation plan that election officials said sowed confusion, U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani in Boston again blocked the Postal Service from moving forward for at least 14 days while she weighs a longer-term bar, finding states lack the time and money to redesign ballots, retrain officials and upload voter data before the midterms. The administration appealed the same day. The practical stakes are near: some states begin sending mail ballots in September.

The Left-Leaning Take

Progressive and voting-rights commentary frames the order as a naked attempt to suppress mail voting and seize a federal chokehold over elections the Constitution reserves to states — timed to disrupt the very midterms in which it operates. They stress that the Supreme Court pointedly refused to bless the order’s legality, and cast Judge Talwani’s re-block as the guardrail against chaos for the millions who vote by mail. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The directional administration-and-allies framing casts the order as basic election-integrity enforcement — citizenship checks and uniform ballot handling — and treats the stay as a rebuke of activist lower-court judges. Worth flagging for intra-right and libertarian tension: the states’ core objection is a federalism one about Washington commandeering state election machinery, an argument the right usually champions, and even the conservative majority declined to endorse the order’s legality. (Directional; no in-window signed right editorial verified.)

Ukraine · Russia

A Russian drone strike detonates a munitions store west of Kyiv, killing at least 37 — the war’s deadliest attack of 2026

+

Late on Friday, Aug. 28, a Russian drone struck a warehouse in the village of Myla, roughly five kilometers west of Kyiv, igniting a large fire and a chain of ammunition detonations that tore through dozens of nearby homes and buildings. Ukrainian officials said the death toll climbed through Saturday to at least 37, surpassing the 31 killed in a July 2 Kyiv strike to become the war’s deadliest single attack of the year; dozens more were injured and hundreds evacuated. Authorities said 34 of the dead were residents of a care home for elderly and disabled people, and the strike capped roughly 72 hours of near-constant Russian aerial attacks across Ukraine.

Two disputes are worth flagging. Early reports put the toll near 27 before officials revised it to 37, and the sides disagree on the target: Russia’s Defense Ministry said it hit a warehouse storing components and launch boosters for long-range drones, while Ukrainian officials described an ammunition depot. In a related Black Sea flare-up, Turkey summoned Ukraine’s ambassador over Ukrainian drone attacks on two Turkish-operated vessels, one of which was struck while towing the other after an earlier hit. (Casualty figures per Ukrainian authorities, still being updated.)

Nepal · Tibet

A Himalayan glacier collapse unleashes floods that kill more than 750 across Nepal and Tibet, with thousands still missing

+

Flash floods and mudslides that began Aug. 26 along a 72-kilometer stretch of Nepal’s Trishuli River kept driving up casualties through the weekend. Nepali authorities confirmed at least 734 dead and about 2,498 missing in Nepal, plus 16 dead and 546 missing on the Tibetan side of the China–Nepal border. Officials attribute the disaster to a collapsing glacier that dammed a river valley, building up water, mud, ice and debris that then burst downstream through the Himalayas; some victims’ bodies were carried as far as 240 kilometers into India. Rescuers reported more than 7,500 people saved, including 227 foreign nationals.

The disaster carries a heavy international dimension: a number of foreign tourists, including Americans, are among the missing, though the count has shifted across the window — CBS cited 33 Americans missing while earlier NPR reporting referenced as many as 90. The flooding also destroyed the Gyirong Port complex, the main trade-and-tourism gateway between China and Nepal, giving the catastrophe a cross-border and diplomatic footprint beyond the humanitarian toll. (Figures per Nepali and Chinese authorities; the exact number of missing foreigners remains unsettled.)

Venezuela · Oil

Trump’s Venezuela oil deal draws fire from across Caracas’s political spectrum, with critics calling it constitutionally void

+

In a Friday-evening post, President Trump announced what he called “the biggest oil deal in world history,” saying the U.S. had secured majority control of more than 65 billion barrels of Venezuelan reserves “at no cost” to American taxpayers. Under the arrangement, interim president Delcy Rodríguez granted a private joint venture a 100-year concession over 17 strategic fields, with the U.S. government controlling 55% of the venture through an equity stake and rights to buy oil at cost; the framework describes more than $100 billion in investment and $209 billion in projected tax revenue. The deal follows the January 2026 U.S. operation that captured then-president Nicolás Maduro.

The freshest development is the backlash, which spans Venezuela’s political divide. Opposition figures and Chavista hardliners alike attacked Rodríguez for handing Washington exclusive access; former planning minister Ricardo Hausmann, now at Harvard, called it a “shameful” and illegitimate arrangement, arguing she lacks constitutional power to bind the country and that no major U.S. oil company will treat it as durable. Analysts expect legal and constitutional challenges in Caracas, where the state retains control over core oil activities, and note that any actual output would take years. Outlets also vary on the reserve figure — “more than 65 billion” barrels versus “over 60 billion.” (Some reaction attributed to named critics; key terms remain unverified independently.)

Space · NASA

NASA’s Roman Space Telescope is set to launch today to hunt dark energy, dark matter and a census of exoplanets

+

NASA cleared the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope for launch after completing its Launch Readiness Review on Friday, Aug. 28, at Kennedy Space Center. Liftoff is targeted for 7:26 a.m. EDT Sunday, Aug. 30, aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy from Launch Complex 39A, with forecasters predicting roughly a 60% chance of favorable weather. Roman caps a development effort of about 16 years and is named for Nancy Grace Roman, NASA’s first chief astronomer.

Roman carries a primary mirror comparable to Hubble’s but pairs it with a field of view about 100 times larger, enabling wide, high-resolution surveys of the sky. Its core goals are to probe dark energy and dark matter by mapping how cosmic structure and expansion have evolved, to conduct a statistical census of exoplanets — including through gravitational microlensing — and to open a broad guest-observer program across astrophysics. (Launch timing per NASA; subject to weather and range conditions.)

AI · Chips

OpenAI’s first benchmarks for its Broadcom-built ‘Jalapeño’ chip claim it beats Nvidia’s top GPU on performance per watt

+

OpenAI released the first benchmark results for Jalapeño, its debut custom AI accelerator co-designed with Broadcom, on Tuesday, Aug. 25. The inference-focused chip is built on TSMC’s N3P process with a 700-watt design, six HBM4 memory stacks (about 216 GiB) delivering roughly 15.4 TB/s of bandwidth, and about 13.4 petaFLOPs of MXFP4 compute, with each compute slice paired to a local memory slice to minimize data-movement stalls. The chip’s name first surfaced in June, but the performance data is new.

Independent analyst firm SemiAnalysis, which said it verified results in OpenAI’s lab, reported Jalapeño delivering up to about 1.9 times more work per watt than Nvidia’s GB300 at roughly half the power draw; OpenAI’s own figures put it at 1.5–1.9 times across models including GPT-OSS, DeepSeek R1 and Kimi K2.5. OpenAI said the numbers come from early “A0” silicon, that a “B0” stepping already in fabrication should improve efficiency by roughly 25%, and that it plans to deploy the chip across its own infrastructure by the end of 2026 — a move widely read as a threat to Nvidia’s margins as big AI buyers pursue custom silicon. (Vendor and single-firm benchmarks warrant caution until broader independent testing lands.)

Science · Cosmology

A new supernova analysis reaffirms that the universe’s expansion is still accelerating, rebutting claims of a cosmic slowdown

+

The long-running dispute over whether cosmic acceleration is real flared again in late August. A team led by Phil Wiseman of the University of Southampton, using Dark Energy Survey data and publishing in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society, argued that an apparent weakening of dark energy largely disappears once a known correlation between Type Ia supernova brightness and host-galaxy properties (mass and stellar age) is properly accounted for. Their conclusion: the evidence that expansion is still accelerating remains strong, preserving dark energy’s central role.

The claim being rebutted came from a 2025 Yonsei University analysis, which held that after correcting for a supernova-age bias, expansion may have entered a decelerating phase and dark energy may be evolving and weakening over time. The exchange is ongoing rather than settled — a fresh critique argues the defense of acceleration has its own methodological problems — and both camps agree on the deeper mystery: even if acceleration holds, the physical nature of dark energy remains unknown. (An actively contested question, not a closed result.)
