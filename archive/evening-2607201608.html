Trade · New This Afternoon

Trump opens a fresh front with a top ally, slapping 50% tariffs on Canadian autos, dairy and alcohol under a 1930 law

+

President Trump on Monday signed three proclamations imposing additional 50% tariffs on select Canadian goods — motor vehicles, dairy (including milk and cream), and alcohol, plus items such as hockey equipment — while notably exempting energy, potash, fish and critical minerals. The White House said the measures cover roughly $20 billion of Canadian imports and take effect in about 30 days. The administration invoked Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930, a rarely used provision that lets the president penalize countries deemed to “discriminate” against U.S. commerce; trade analysts called it the “nuclear option” for tariffs and among the steepest levies Trump has placed on any nation. U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer framed the action as leveling the field for American cars, alcohol and dairy against Canadian “discriminatory treatment.”

Ottawa reacted with anger. Ontario Premier Doug Ford said on Monday afternoon that Canada “should respond tariff for tariff, dollar for dollar,” and the move revives the trade hostilities that have fueled a running Canadian boycott of U.S. goods. The escalation lands the same day Trump vowed military retaliation against Iran and imposed a fresh diplomatic jab at Britain, capping an unusually combative Monday on the foreign-economic front.

The Left-Leaning Take

The liberal frame is self-inflicted wound: a president picking a trade war with America’s closest ally and second-largest trading partner over cars, cheese and beer, in a way that will raise prices for U.S. consumers, invite retaliation against American exporters, and further corrode a democratic alliance — all on a stretched reading of a nearly century-old statute Congress never meant as a blank check. (No dedicated in-window Mother Jones / Vox / Slate / Guardian column surfaced — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Populist-right outlets (Townhall) cheered the tariffs as an overdue answer to Canadian “discrimination” on autos and dairy. But the sharper conservative reaction is the free-market and libertarian objection: at Reason/the Volokh Conspiracy, Ilya Somin has argued Section 338 is being abused — tariffs are a congressional power, not an executive one — and the Cato Institute’s Scott Lincicome warned the move injects “massive uncertainty” into the global economy and threatens other trading partners. Label this an intra-right free-trade dissent, not the White House line. (No signed in-window National Review / Dispatch / WSJ editorial on the 50% order was retrievable — provisional.)

Elections · New This Weekend

DHS Secretary Mullin threatens state election officials with fines and “prison time” over a voter-roll purge, as two federal judges issue clashing orders on the SAVE citizenship database

+

In the fallout from President Trump’s July 16 primetime address — whose central claim that China compromised “220 million U.S. voter files” was found unsupported by AP, PBS and PolitiFact — the enforcement fight escalated across the weekend. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin sent letters on July 17 to four states — California (about 190,832), New Jersey (35,152), Nevada (15,903) and Pennsylvania (14,576) — alleging roughly 250,000 potential noncitizens on their rolls, drawn from matching public voter data against the federal SAVE database. Mullin then warned election officials in all 50 states that they could face fines, the loss of federal election funding, and possible “prison time” if they refuse to run their rolls through SAVE, without specifying the statute that would authorize prosecution. Because election administration is constitutionally a state function, the threats drew immediate federalism objections.

The legal ground is contested and self-contradicting. U.S. District Judge Sparkle Sooknanan (D.D.C.) issued a 75-page ruling on June 22 blocking DHS’s SAVE overhaul nationwide, finding violations of the Social Security Act, the 1974 Privacy Act and the Administrative Procedure Act — a block since upheld. Yet U.S. District Judge T. Kent Wetherell II (N.D. Fla., a Trump appointee) has ordered DHS to restore SAVE access for Florida, Ohio, Iowa and Indiana, leaving two federal courts pointing in opposite directions. Voting-rights experts and PolitiFact call the noncitizen count overstated, noting SAVE routinely misflags newly naturalized citizens; DHS itself labels the numbers a “preliminary review.” The election fight and the Iran war were the two dominant Sunday-show threads on July 19, with Sen. Chuck Schumer calling the speech an attempt to “change the subject” before the midterms.

The Left-Leaning Take

MSNBC opinion and Democracy Docket read the speech-plus-threats as a single coordinated move: manufacture a fraud pretext out of public data, then use the machinery of DHS to intimidate the nonpartisan state officials who actually run elections and lay groundwork to control the vote from the White House ahead of the midterms. On this view, the “prison time” warning — untethered to any cited statute — is the point: coercion dressed as compliance. (No dedicated in-window Mother Jones / Vox / Slate / Guardian column surfaced — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Mainstream-right coverage (Fox, OANN, the Western Journal) presented Mullin’s action supportively, as an overdue election-integrity push against noncitizen voting rather than as an editorial position. The sharper conservative objection is federalist and libertarian in flavor — the sort Reason presses — that DHS and DOJ simply lack the authority to prosecute state officials over how they administer elections, and that a federal citizenship dragnet is exactly the centralization the right claims to oppose. No signed in-window National Review / Dispatch / WSJ editorial on the threats was retrievable — provisional.

Science · Astronomy

Astronomers find the strongest evidence yet of an atmosphere on a rocky planet in its star’s habitable zone

+

A team led by Collin Cherubim of Harvard reported the strongest evidence to date that a temperate, rocky exoplanet can hold onto an atmosphere — a prerequisite for a potentially habitable surface. Using the 6.5-meter Magellan Clay telescope at Las Campanas Observatory in Chile across observing runs in 2024 and 2025, the researchers detected helium escaping from the outer atmosphere of LHS 1140 b, a rocky world about 48 light-years away that sits in its star’s habitable zone. The escaping helium implies the planet still retains a substantial gas envelope. The work was published this week in Science.

Hard data showing that small, moderate-temperature rocky worlds can keep an atmosphere has been strikingly thin, which is why the result matters: it strengthens the case that Earth-analog conditions — rocky composition, a mild climate and a real atmosphere — can persist elsewhere, and it makes LHS 1140 b a priority target for follow-up characterization with instruments such as JWST.

+

Climate · Extreme Heat

An attribution study calls Europe’s 2026 heat the most severe on record — and “virtually impossible” without human-caused warming

+

Europe’s 2026 heat has intensified through July, and a World Weather Attribution analysis concluded it is the most severe heatwave ever recorded across the studied region — finding that heat of this intensity would have been “virtually impossible” without human-caused warming. The group estimates such heat is now tens to hundreds of times more likely than in 2003 and effectively unheard of half a century ago. Spain peaked at 45.1°C and Germany hit a provisional national record of 41.7°C in late June, with a fresh heat dome pushing the Iberian Peninsula back into the low-to-mid 40s°C in July; the UK Met Office logged its most days above 34°C in a single year.

Preliminary excess-mortality estimates put the Europe-wide death toll above roughly 9,900 — a figure heatwave tallies typically revise upward for months — with Spain reporting more than 100 heat-related deaths and France linking numerous deaths, including drownings, to the conditions. The study is the latest to translate a summer of records into a direct human-attribution and mortality signal rather than a general warming trend.

Sources: World Weather Attribution, CNN, Nature (partial — paywalled)
