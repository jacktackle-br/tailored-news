Executive Power · Free Speech

Trump threatens a $5 billion defamation suit against a liberal think tank over its crime report; the group refuses to retract and calls it intimidation

+

Lawyers for President Trump have threatened a $5 billion defamation lawsuit against the Center for American Progress (CAP) unless it retracts a July 13 analysis concluding that his National Guard deployments to US cities — projected to cost taxpayers more than $1.7 billion by year’s end — had “no measurable effect on violent crime trends.” The letter set an end-of-business Friday, Aug. 21 deadline; CAP declined. Its president, Neera Tanden, said the group stands by the report and will not remove it, calling the threat “a transparent attempt to silence us” and “baseless.” Over the weekend Trump amplified the fight on Truth Social, deriding the study as “another Radical Left SCAM.”

The move extends a pattern of Trump using defamation claims against critics and media — a $10 billion suit against the BBC (which has moved to dismiss it), an earlier ABC News settlement, and pending action against The New York Times. Legal observers note that even a factually mistaken report is generally protected by the First Amendment, and that the suits’ apparent aim is to impose crushing costs and a chilling effect rather than to win at trial. (Facts from The Washington Post, The Hill, NOTUS and CAP.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left calls this censorship-by-litigation: weaponizing a bogus defamation threat to punish inconvenient research is straight out of the authoritarian playbook, and the First Amendment protects publishing facts and analysis that contradict the government — even if they were wrong, which here they aren’t. The tell, they argue, is that Trump is targeting the data undercutting a signature policy rather than rebutting it. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Free-speech and libertarian conservatives (a Reason-style view) are uneasy: a think tank has a constitutional right to publish even flawed analysis, and $5 billion threats over policy research are exactly the government censorship the right claims to oppose. Trump-aligned voices counter that CAP — run by a former Biden official — is a partisan Democratic shop whose “scam” study exists to kneecap a popular anti-crime deployment. The anti-Trump right (The Bulwark) frames the suit as raw intimidation — an intra-right dissent, not the mainstream-right line. (Directional.)

Sources: The Washington Post, The Hill, NOTUS, Center for American Progress
National Security · Iran

On the eve of Bessent’s ‘economic D-Day,’ Iran splits in public — its president defends a US memorandum as no ‘capitulation’ while its new security chief threatens the region

+

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent will hold a Monday, Aug. 24 news conference to unveil what he calls the “toughest sanctions in history” — an “economic D-Day” aimed at third countries that buy Iranian oil, move its money, or conduct seaborne ship-to-ship transfers, warning the Treasury will bring its “full might and force” against violators. The new development this evening is a rare public fracture inside Tehran. President Masoud Pezeshkian defended a US–Iran memorandum of understanding — signed by Trump in June, its 60-day deadline lapsed last week — as the best exit from a “neither war nor peace” limbo, insisting “there is not a single provision… that amounts to capitulation.”

Cutting the other way, the newly installed secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council struck a defiant note, warning of retaliation against any neighbor that joins the US pressure campaign; hardliners in parliament accuse Pezeshkian’s team of conceding to Washington, and a spokesman dismissed the coming sanctions as an “implicit admission of the enemy’s humiliating defeat.” Pakistan’s army chief was reported to be traveling to Tehran as mediators try to revive talks. Note: the MOU’s reopening of the Strait of Hormuz remains the crucial sticking point. (Facts from CNBC, the Irish Times, AP via The Washington Post and Al Jazeera.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a diplomatic off-ramp being drowned out: Iran’s own president is signaling for a deal, yet Washington answers with a blockade and third-party sanctions that function as collective punishment on ordinary Iranians. Doubling down now, they warn, hands the argument to Tehran’s hardliners and risks blowing up the last open channel — an undeclared, Congress-unauthorized economic war in search of a victory it can’t define. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Libertarian skeptics (a Reason-style caution) counter that a blockade is itself an act of war carrying oil-price and escalation risk, but the mainstream-right line is to keep the pressure on until Tehran accepts terms. (Directional.)

Trump readies an ‘economic D-Day’ against Iran, with new sanctions due Monday and a bet that a blockade can do what bombing did not

+

Rather than resume large-scale combat, the Trump administration spent the weekend escalating an economic-warfare campaign against Iran. Tehran responded defiantly. Analysts disagree on efficacy: some report Iran has weathered years of sanctions and retains retaliatory options across the Gulf, while others cite severe damage, with inflation reported variously near 80–88%. Courts · DOJ · Executive Power

A federal appeals court blocks DOJ subpoenas of NY AG Letitia James, finding the Trump-installed prosecutor served unlawfully; Justice Dept. vows a Supreme Court appeal

+

The Second US Circuit Court of Appeals ruled 2–1 that the Justice Department cannot enforce subpoenas issued to New York Attorney General Letitia James’s office, upholding a lower court and finding that John Sarcone — a Trump ally installed as the top federal prosecutor in Albany — was not lawfully serving as lead prosecutor because DOJ’s maneuvers to keep him in the role after his temporary appointment lapsed circumvented the Federal Vacancies Reform Act and the statute governing interim US attorneys. The subpoenas, issued in August 2025, sought records tied to the civil suits James brought against Trump, his family business and the NRA; James has denied wrongdoing and called the probe retaliatory.

A DOJ spokesperson said the department disagrees and intends to take the case to the Supreme Court, which could ultimately decide how much authority temporary US attorneys retain after their initial terms expire. The ruling issued Friday, Aug. 21; the live thread into the weekend is the administration’s vow to seek review at the high court. It is at least the third time a federal appeals court has rejected the administration’s method of installing US attorneys without Senate confirmation. (Facts from NBC News, CBS News, Reuters (via the Detroit News) and The Hill.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads it as a vindication for the rule of law and a rebuke of the administration’s weaponization of DOJ against Trump’s adversaries: the courts caught the government trying to keep a hand-picked loyalist in place through legal sleight-of-hand precisely to pursue a critic. The pattern of installing pliant prosecutors to target enemies, they argue, is the real scandal. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Conservatives split. Executive-power defenders frame the loss as a narrow, technical ruling about appointment procedure — not a finding that James did nothing wrong — and back DOJ’s bid to have the Supreme Court affirm broad presidential authority over interim US attorneys. Rule-of-law conservatives and libertarians are more skeptical of stretching vacancy statutes, seeing the Second Circuit as correctly enforcing statutory limits on the executive. (Directional.)

Sources: NBC News, CBS News, Reuters (via Detroit News), The Hill

DR Congo’s Ebola cases top 5,200 in the country’s deadliest-ever outbreak, and Africa CDC warns the true toll may be far higher

+

The Bundibugyo-strain Ebola outbreak in eastern DR Congo has passed 5,200 confirmed cases and about 2,500 deaths — already the largest and deadliest in the country’s history and among the fastest-growing on record. On Aug. 22, the Africa CDC warned that the official count may capture only 30–40% of actual infections, implying a far larger real caseload. The DRC government has launched a “Congo River Without Ebola” initiative to keep the main river-transport artery from becoming a spread route and to reduce cross-border risk to the Republic of Congo and Central African Republic. The WHO has treated the outbreak as a global emergency since mid-August. (Facts from CGTN, UN News, Al Jazeera and MSF.)

Science · Astronomy & Cosmology

JWST spectra reveal ‘hidden’ small stars in nine ancient galaxies, implying they may be three to four times more massive than thought

+

An international team, including Penn State astronomers, used James Webb Space Telescope spectroscopy of nine massive, “quenched” galaxies — ones that stopped forming stars billions of years ago — and concluded from their light that the galaxies contain far more faint, low-mass stars than standard models assumed. Accounting for that hidden population would make some of these early galaxies roughly three to four times more massive than prior estimates. The result is an observational inference drawn from spectra, not a direct star count or a simulation: the team modeled the galaxies’ combined starlight and found it best fit a “bottom-heavy” stellar mix.

The finding deepens an existing puzzle — how such enormous, mature galaxies assembled so soon after the Big Bang — and the authors note it could also imply that planets around low-mass stars were more common in the early universe. The work was published in Nature Astronomy (coverage dated Aug. 22, 2026). (Facts from Space.com, Universe Today and The Debrief; the primary release page was egress-blocked.)

Science · Biology & Aging

The Greenland shark, Earth’s longest-lived vertebrate, shows almost no age-related decline in its eyes — hinting at DNA-repair-driven longevity

+

Researchers examining the eyes of Greenland sharks — which can live around 400 years — report that the retina shows little of the degeneration normally tied to aging, even in centuries-old animals. Dim-light (rod-based) vision genes remain intact and strongly expressed, suited to the dark, cold Arctic deep, while many bright-light (cone) genes have been lost or silenced. The team found robust expression of DNA-repair genes, including components of the ERCC1–XPF complex, which they propose may help preserve eye tissue over the animal’s extreme lifespan.

They also found that eye parasites long assumed to blind the sharks do not block functionally useful (especially blue) light. These are molecular and tissue findings from dissected specimens — gene-expression and optical measurements — not longevity experiments, and the DNA-repair-to-longevity link is a proposed mechanism rather than proven causation. The work was published in Nature Communications (coverage dated Aug. 21, 2026). (Facts from Nature Communications, Scientific American and Smithsonian Magazine.)

Sources: Nature Communications, Scientific American, Smithsonian Magazine
