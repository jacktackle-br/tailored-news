Iran · Strait of Hormuz

The U.S. resumes “powerful strikes” on Iran and snaps back oil sanctions after tankers are hit in Hormuz

+

The fragile U.S.–Iran truce cracked open on Tuesday, July 7. After projectiles struck commercial vessels transiting the Strait of Hormuz — a liquefied-natural-gas tanker was set ablaze near Limah, Oman, and a second ship took structural damage, with no casualties reported — U.S. Central Command said it had “begun launching a series of powerful strikes against Iran to impose heavy costs” for attacking shipping “crewed by innocent civilians.” CENTCOM said the strikes hit Iranian air-defense, coastal-surveillance, surface-to-air, anti-ship-cruise-missile and drone-launch sites; Iranian state media reported explosions near Bandar Abbas, Qeshm Island and the port of Sirik. (Accounts of the ship attacks varied — the U.K. maritime authority counted two vessels struck, while some U.S. outlets reported three.)

Washington paired the military response with economic pressure: the Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control revoked the license that had temporarily lifted oil sanctions on Tehran, effective July 7 — unwinding a central concession of the memorandum of understanding Trump and President Masoud Pezeshkian signed on June 17 and that had reopened the strait. Iran threatened a “decisive” response. The re-escalation landed as Trump opened the NATO summit in Ankara nursing an explicit grievance that allies had refused to back this year’s U.S.-led Iran campaign. On the home front, the strikes revived the war-powers fight: hawkish Republicans rallied behind the president while Democrats pressed to force a vote reasserting Congress’s role — a resolution that would be largely symbolic absent a veto-proof majority.

The Left-Leaning Take

A president who claimed to have ended a war has restarted it by fiat, with no fresh vote from Congress and no clear off-ramp (Vox, Mother Jones, Slate; Guardian opinion). On this read, torching the June MOU over a shipping incident risks dragging the U.S. back into an open-ended Gulf conflict, and the war-powers dodge is the real scandal: strikes ordered, Congress informed but not asked.

The Right-Leaning Take

Tehran shot first by attacking civilian shipping in an international waterway, and swift, targeted retaliation plus snapback sanctions is exactly the deterrence the moment demands (National Review, WSJ editorial). Reason (libertarian) is the dissent: it warns that reflexive re-entanglement in the Gulf and open-ended strikes without a congressional debate are how a “limited” response becomes another forever-war.

Sources: Washington Post, NPR, CNBC, The Hill, CBS News
The NATO Summit · Day 1

In Ankara, Trump revives the Greenland demand and floats pulling U.S. troops out of Europe

+

As the summit opened Tuesday, Trump turned the alliance meeting into a pressure session. He said he nearly skipped it, berated NATO over what he called allies’ failure to support the Iran campaign (“We’ve invested trillions of dollars in NATO”), and lavished praise on host Recep Tayyip Erdogan — touting their “chemistry” and reiterating he would consider selling Turkey F-35s, reversing the 2019 expulsion. He then resurrected his demand that the United States control Greenland, warning he could withdraw U.S. forces from Europe if Denmark refuses; CNN reported he privately mused about cutting the American troop presence by roughly a third. His NATO ambassador, Matthew Whitaker, framed the goal bluntly: “Europe takes over the conventional defense of the European continent… We’re not going away, we’re just doing less.”

Beneath the theater, the summit’s deliverables were coming together unevenly. Envoys agreed on roughly €70bn ($80bn) in 2026 military aid for Ukraine and a draft “Ankara Declaration” reaffirming an “ironclad” Article 5 and naming Russia a long-term threat, but the text stalled over a clause committing allies to “at least comparable” support in 2027 — Italy resisted locking in the out-year pledge (Rome disputes the “blocking” characterization). Trump’s headline bilateral with Zelensky was set for Wednesday.

The Left-Leaning Take

Threatening to seize an ally’s territory and yank troops mid-war is Putin’s dream scenario, dressed up as burden-sharing (Guardian opinion, Vox, Mother Jones). On this read, the Greenland fixation and the “doing less” doctrine corrode the very deterrence NATO exists to provide, and reward Erdogan’s authoritarianism with F-35s in the bargain.

The Right-Leaning Take

Europeans grumbling about tone while finally funding €70bn for Ukraine and inching toward 5% is proof the pressure works (National Review, WSJ editorial): the U.S. can rationally shift conventional defense to a richer Europe. The Bulwark (anti-Trump right — an intra-right dissent) counters that annexation threats against a NATO member and troop-withdrawal blackmail hand Moscow a propaganda gift no spending chart can offset.

Sources: CNBC, CNN, Stars and Stripes, NPR, RFE/RL

As the first major Western leader in post-Assad Syria, Macron is betting that fast economic engagement can pull Damascus out of Iran’s orbit — and the bombings, staged in the heart of the capital during a state visit, are a pointed reminder of how thin al-Sharaa’s writ still is on security. The trip sets up Trump’s own meeting with al-Sharaa on the summit sidelines.

The NATO Summit

Trump opens the Ankara summit nursing a fresh Iran grievance — and signaling he’ll clear F-35s for Turkey

+

President Trump arrived in Ankara on Tuesday, July 7, as NATO’s two-day summit (July 7–8) opened, carrying new frustration at European allies over their response to this year’s U.S.-led operations against Iran — a fresh front in his long campaign over burden-sharing. Secretary General Mark Rutte framed the agenda around three priorities: raising allied defense investment, boosting defense-industrial production, and supporting Ukraine. Trump’s central demand is enforcement — holding members to last year’s pledge to push spending toward 5% of GDP — and, in a reversal of a first-term ban, he is expected to signal willingness to sell Turkey F-35 fighter jets, according to two U.S. officials.

The money is the flashpoint. Allies are set to pledge roughly €70bn ($80bn) in military aid to Ukraine for 2026, with “at least equivalent” support in 2027 — a package the United States is not contributing to — and, per a draft declaration, to reaffirm an “ironclad” commitment to collective defense and name Russia a threat to Euro-Atlantic security. A bipartisan Senate delegation traveled separately to reassure allies. Trump holds Wednesday bilaterals with Volodymyr Zelensky (see Foreign Affairs) — who arrived saying his top goal is securing more Patriot air defenses after Monday’s deadly Kyiv barrage — and with Syria’s new president Ahmad al-Sharaa.

The Left-Leaning Take

Treating collective defense as a protection-racket ledger — and airing Iran grievances while Kyiv burns — hands Putin the alliance rift he covets (Guardian opinion, Vox, Mother Jones). On this read, dangling F-35s for Erdogan rewards an authoritarian host while Europe is left to bankroll Ukraine alone, all for the sake of a spending scorecard.

The Right-Leaning Take

Burden-sharing hawks (National Review, WSJ editorial) call a €70bn European-funded package and the 5% push a vindication of Trump’s pressure — Europe finally paying its share while the U.S. stops footing the bill — but warn against letting the Iran spat or a Putin call blunt deterrence mid-war. Reason (libertarian) welcomes allies self-funding yet is wary of new F-35 entanglements; The Bulwark (anti-Trump right, an intra-right dissent) argues grievance diplomacy corrodes the alliance even when the spending numbers look like a win.

Sources: NPR, Al Jazeera, CNN, Reuters via U.S. News, Fox News
Guns

The ATF moves to scrap more than three dozen firearms rules in a sweeping deregulation

+

The Justice Department and ATF unveiled a package rolling back more than three dozen federal firearms regulations, with the proposal set for Federal Register publication July 6 and a public comment period running through Oct. 5. The measures rescind the Biden-era pistol-brace rule, narrow the “engaged in the business” rule that had expanded background-check requirements for gun-show and online sellers, abandon the ATF’s “zero-tolerance” dealer-license revocation policy, and ease National Firearms Act paperwork (one fingerprint card instead of two; a standard photo ID). Some provisions would restore gun rights to certain people previously barred over mental-illness adjudications. Officials including Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche cast the rules as unfairly burdening law-abiding owners.

The scope is unusual: the package effectively reverses much of the prior administration’s gun-enforcement agenda in one stroke. Notably, the ATF’s own analysis acknowledged that loosening the mental-illness restriction carries a risk of mass-casualty events. Gun-safety groups warned the changes gut oversight of private and online sales and reopen the gun-show loophole; gun-rights advocates hailed a long-sought restoration of Second Amendment protections.

The Left-Leaning Take

A giveaway to the gun lobby that dismantles hard-won safeguards (Mother Jones, Vox, Slate): reopening the gun-show loophole and restoring firearms access to people once flagged as dangerous — even as the ATF concedes the mass-casualty risk in its own paperwork. On this read, it is deregulation that will get people killed, timed to a base rather than to public safety.

The Right-Leaning Take

Second Amendment advocates (National Review, WSJ editorial) frame it as an overdue correction of Biden-era overreach that criminalized ordinary owners and hobbyist sellers, restoring due process and paperwork sanity. Reason (libertarian) cheers rolling back ATF discretion and the “engaged in the business” dragnet, while cautioning that restoring rights after mental-illness adjudications deserves careful, individualized review rather than a blanket rollback.

Sources: PBS NewsHour, Philadelphia Inquirer, Axios, Newsmax

Gaza

Hamas dissolves its Gaza government to pressure a stalled ceasefire; Israel dismisses the move

+

Hamas announced July 6 that the government running Gaza on its behalf had resigned, offering to hand civilian administration to a Palestinian technocratic committee envisioned under the U.S.-brokered peace plan. Analysts read the step as pressure on Israel amid a stalled ceasefire rather than a clean transfer of power: no timeline has been set to stand up the committee, and the proposed international stabilization force has yet to materialize. Israel dismissed the announcement, arguing Hamas fighters would simply remain in place behind a technocratic facade.

The truce, in place since October 2025, remains largely unfulfilled. Israel has carried out near-daily strikes — killing at least five Palestinians on Monday — and Gaza’s health ministry counts more than 1,072 people killed and over 3,400 wounded in what it calls ceasefire-period violations. Hamas says it will not disarm until Israel halts attacks and withdraws, and accuses Israel of repeatedly breaching the deal; the core bargains of the plan — disarmament, a functioning technocratic authority and an international force — all remain unrealized.

AI Policy

The White House readies a frontier-AI release framework as GPT-5.6 stays locked to a government-vetted few

+

A White House voluntary framework governing how the most capable AI models reach the public is expected as soon as this week, formalizing a pre-release review process that OpenAI and Anthropic have already navigated in practice. It stems from a June executive order directing agencies to build a classified benchmarking process for designating “covered frontier models” and giving the government up to 30 days of pre-release access, with the benchmarks aimed at models carrying cutting-edge cyber capabilities.

The stakes are concrete. OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 “Sol” family (previewed June 26, alongside a mid-range Terra and a lighter Luna) remains limited to roughly 20 government-vetted partner organizations, with broad access expected mid-to-late July. Google’s Gemini 3.5 Pro, cleared for a July launch, is so far the only top-tier model without such restrictions — leaving Google positioned to ship a frontier model without the pre-release gating now shaping its rivals. A formal framework would make those release timelines more predictable across labs.

Astrophysics

The largest-ever gravitational-wave catalog pushes the tally to 390 — and hints at black holes built from black holes

+

The LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA collaboration released GWTC-5, the largest gravitational-wave catalog to date, adding 161 detections from a single roughly 10-month run (April 2024–January 2025) and pushing the all-time total to about 390. Highlights include GW250114, described as the clearest black-hole-merger signal ever recorded — a sharp new tool for testing Einstein’s general relativity — and the most precisely localized merger yet observed.

Among the finds is growing evidence for “hierarchical” mergers: at least two events look like collisions of black holes that were themselves products of earlier mergers, pointing to dense environments where black holes repeatedly combine and grow. Researchers likened the trove to uncovering a hidden population — “the astronomical equivalent of uncovering an ancient civilization” — that is reshaping estimates of how black holes form.

Sources: ScienceDaily, Space.com, Simons Foundation
Energy & Climate

Google’s electricity use jumped 37% in a year as it admits AI is “outrunning” grid decarbonization

+

In its 2026 Environmental Report, Google disclosed that its electricity consumption rose 37% year-over-year in 2025 — the steepest annual jump in its history, and more than 250% cumulative growth since 2019 — as it built out AI and cloud infrastructure. Its data centers drew roughly 42 million MWh, comparable to New Zealand’s entire annual electricity use. Greenhouse-gas emissions climbed 18%, the largest annual rise Google has reported, driven largely by manufacturing AI hardware; supply-chain (Scope 3) emissions grew about 25%.

The company conceded its AI buildout is “currently accelerating faster than the grid is decarbonizing,” even as it signed an unprecedented 12 GW of net-new clean-energy deals. The disclosure crystallizes the defining tension of the AI era: surging compute demand is colliding with climate targets, and — as U.S. grid operators warn of record, data-center-driven peaks — renewable additions and clean-power contracts are straining to keep pace with the load.

Space

Japan’s Hayabusa2 buzzes asteroid Torifune at 5 km/s, revealing a two-lobed “contact binary”

+

On July 5, Japan’s Hayabusa2 spacecraft made an ultra-close, high-speed flyby of the near-Earth asteroid 98943 Torifune (formerly designated 2001 CC21), passing as close as about 1 km at roughly 5 km/s. JAXA released images showing an elongated, roughly 450-meter S-type asteroid that turns out to be a contact binary — a two-lobed, “two-headed” rock — some 62 million miles from Earth.

The pass is part of Hayabusa2’s extended mission after its 2020 sample return from asteroid Ryugu, and was designed chiefly as an engineering rehearsal: proving the precise, high-speed guidance a probe needs to reach and image a small, fast-moving body. The data feeds toward its next target, the tiny, fast-rotating asteroid 1998 KY26, which Hayabusa2 is due to reach in 2031.
