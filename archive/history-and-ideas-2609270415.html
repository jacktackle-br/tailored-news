Philosophy & Ideas · Political Philosophy

The philosopher of ‘reasoning together’ proved his own theory by never being afraid to change his mind — a personal portrait of Jürgen Habermas

+

In a new Aeon essay, “Reasoning together” (published September 25), the philosopher Emilie Prattico — who studied with Jürgen Habermas and later edited the volume Habermas and the Crisis of Democracy (Routledge) — argues that the coherence of Habermas’s long life lies in a single unfashionable virtue: his willingness to revise his views under the pressure of a better argument. For Habermas, truth is not something an individual seizes alone but something that emerges when everyone who could be affected by a norm has a voice, can object, and speaks sincerely. Prattico traces this “discourse ethics” to an unexpected source — the Quaker meetings Habermas encountered on visits to the United States in the 1960s and 70s, whose practice of seeking unity with everyone present, he said, he “heard an echo of” when describing the ideal conditions for deliberation.

The essay is a reflective, partly memoiristic reappraisal of one of the last living giants of twentieth-century thought — not a new study — and its provocation is quietly pointed. In an era of hardened positions and performative certainty, it reframes changing your mind as the highest expression of intellectual integrity rather than a weakness or a defeat. That so austere a theory of rational consensus should turn out to rest, biographically, on a Protestant devotional practice is the kind of irony that makes the piece linger: the twentieth century’s great secular theorist of public reason learned the shape of the ideal conversation in a room full of silent worshippers. (An interpretive, partly personal essay rather than empirical research.)

Sources: Aeon, Aeon (author)
Religion · Sociology of Religion

Pew’s biggest look at American Mormons in years finds a faith losing the young it raises — even as those who stay remain among the nation’s most devout

+

The Pew Research Center released a detailed report, “Tradition and Change Among Latter-day Saints in the U.S.,” on September 22, drawing chiefly on its 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study and comparing it with Pew data from 2007, 2011 and 2014. The headline finding is a steady erosion of retention: only about 54% of Americans raised Latter-day Saint still identify with the church as adults, down from 70% in 2007. Yet the same report underscores a paradox — those who remain are exceptionally religious and report unusually high rates of happy family life, and lifelong members are markedly more observant than both converts and former members.

Coverage from Religion News Service (“9 ways US Mormons are changing”) and the Deseret News framed the study as a portrait of a tradition simultaneously shrinking at the edges and intensifying at its core, with generational and political shifts running underneath. What makes it a “History & Ideas” item rather than a mere data dump is the pattern it captures: a distinctive, high-commitment American faith being reshaped by the same secularizing currents pulling at other traditions, while resisting them in other ways — a small-scale test of whether intensity at the center can offset losses at the margins. (Caveat: survey data are correlational and self-reported, and the LDS subsample is relatively small — 515 current and 316 former members — so fine-grained breakdowns carry wider margins of error.)

Sources: Pew Research Center, Pew (companion), Religion News Service, Deseret News
History · Tudor England & the Reformation

Henry VIII’s own enforcer of the Reformation secretly rescued a forbidden Catholic book he was sent to destroy — and six lost pages just resurfaced in a Brighton archive

+

Researchers at the universities of Cambridge and Sussex, writing in the Oxford journal Notes and Queries (reported September 22), argue that Sir John Gage — the prominent Tudor courtier tasked by Henry VIII from 1536 with overseeing the dissolution of the monasteries across Surrey, Kent and Sussex — quietly preserved a manuscript he was supposed to help destroy. The text is Nicholas Love’s early-fifteenth-century The Mirror of the Blessed Life of Jesus Christ, a devotional work that turned dangerous during the Reformation because it insisted on the real, physical presence of Christ in the Eucharist.

The scholars matched a fragment of six pages held at The Keep archive in Brighton to a manuscript of Love’s text now at Yale, and infer that Gage — who remained Catholic throughout his career as a royal servant — shielded the book from the flames. It is a vivid piece of archival detective work about the private conscience of a man publicly dismantling the old faith, and a reminder that the Reformation was lived not only as top-down decree but as a set of quiet personal compromises. (Caveat: the attribution rests on scholarly inference linking the fragment, the Yale manuscript and Gage’s biography, so it is an argued reconstruction rather than a documented confession.)

Sources: Smithsonian Magazine, Archaeology News
