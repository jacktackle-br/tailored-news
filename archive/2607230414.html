War & the Purse · Escalating

The U.S.–Iran war widens overnight — Iran hits Gulf bases, the Houthis strike Saudi tankers, and Rubio says the price “will continue to get higher every single night”

+

The renewed U.S.–Iran war escalated sharply in the last 24 hours. The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said it targeted U.S. bases in Kuwait and Jordan, and both governments confirmed new attacks Thursday, July 23; Kuwait’s armed forces said air defenses were responding to “hostile drone threats.” Yemen’s Houthis said early Thursday they struck two Saudi oil tankers — hours after President Trump warned the U.S. would “destroy one bridge or power plant” every time Iran attacks a ship transiting the Strait of Hormuz. U.S. Central Command said late Wednesday it had completed a 12th consecutive night of strikes, hitting “maritime capabilities, missile and drone storage facilities, coastal surveillance sites and air defense assets,” with targeting expanding in recent days to roads, tunnels, railways and port infrastructure. Brent crude jumped roughly 4–5% Thursday to about $98 a barrel, its highest since late May.

Speaking on the sidelines of the ASEAN foreign ministers’ meeting in Manila, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said “the price will continue to get higher every single night until they come to their senses,” adding that the president “doesn’t see a lot of utility in responding to their overtures at this point.” Rubio also denied reports that Russia is helping Iran target U.S. bases. The escalation follows the collapse of an earlier ceasefire; polling cited across coverage shows the war is historically unpopular, with a CNN survey putting 59% of Americans against it and 77% of independents calling it “not worth it.”

The Left-Leaning Take

The war is a self-made quagmire: a conflict Trump promised would last “weeks” is now in its fifth month, killing U.S. service members, spiking gas prices and dragging in Gulf allies with no defined endpoint. The left frames the nightly “bridge or power plant” retaliation as escalation for its own sake, untethered from any achievable objective, and points to lopsided independent opposition as proof the country never signed up for a third Gulf war. Congress · Party-Line Vote

House Republicans muscle through a $95 billion Iran-war blueprint fused to Trump’s SAVE America voting overhaul, 216–214

+

On Wednesday, July 22, the House adopted a Republican-only budget resolution 216–214, with all Democrats, two Republicans and one GOP-caucusing independent opposed. The blueprint unlocks up to $95 billion via party-line reconciliation: roughly $73 billion tied to the Iran war (Pentagon and intelligence operations), about $12 billion in aid for farmers hit by Trump’s tariffs, and roughly $10 billion to implement the SAVE America Act, Trump’s election overhaul requiring proof of citizenship to register and photo ID to vote. (Component figures vary across outlets; CBS and PBS cite roughly $60 billion for the Pentagon plus about $13 billion in other national-security needs.) Budget Committee Chairman Jodey Arrington (R-Texas) framed the ask as “just bullets and bombs to finish the job.” Separately, the House also passed a roughly $1.15 trillion annual defense spending bill.

The measure is only the first step; it instructs committees to draft the reconciliation package and heads to the Senate, where the SAVE America Act has stalled for months and where the voting-law provisions may not survive the reconciliation “Byrd” rules barring non-budgetary policy. It follows a non-binding War Powers resolution the Senate passed 50–48 in late June directing Trump to remove forces from Iran. House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries said on the floor: “Republican extremists apparently have money for wars, but can’t feed the poor.”

The Left-Leaning Take

The synthesized left frame is guns-over-butter married to voter suppression: Republicans funding an unpopular war and bailouts for Trump’s own tariff damage while, in Jeffries’ words, “ripping food from the mouths of hungry children,” and smuggling in citizenship-proof voting rules ahead of the midterms. The party-line, offset-free reconciliation route is read as an admission the war can’t command bipartisan or public support. The Right-Leaning Take

Fiscal conservatives are the friction point: several House Republicans balked at a package with no offsetting cuts, and members like Josh Brecheen demanded either offsets or the SAVE Act as the price of their vote. The pro-Trump case is that funding is non-negotiable while troops are in combat and that pairing it with election-integrity measures forces Democrats to choose. But the deficit-hawk wing’s unease — and the razor-thin 216–214 margin — signals a strained coalition. Nonproliferation · Policy Shift

Trump signs a landmark U.S.–Saudi nuclear deal that cracks the door to uranium enrichment — even as the U.S. wages war to end Iran’s program

+

On Wednesday, July 22, the U.S. and Saudi Arabia signed a “123 agreement” for peaceful nuclear cooperation — executed by Energy Secretary Chris Wright and Saudi Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman — alongside a bilateral safeguards agreement. President Trump approved the deal, described as lasting about 30 years and worth tens of billions of dollars, which would let U.S. companies build out the kingdom’s civilian nuclear infrastructure, potentially including a U.S.-built uranium enrichment facility on Saudi soil. Crucially, the framework omits the “gold standard” language that barred prior partners (such as the UAE) from enriching uranium or reprocessing spent fuel — both potential weapons pathways — and does not require enhanced IAEA oversight. The deal now goes to Congress for a mandatory review.

Nonproliferation experts reacted sharply. Carnegie Endowment’s James Acton called it “tantamount to the United States giving up on nonproliferation,” and the Arms Control Association’s Kelsey Davenport called it “reckless and irresponsible.” Senator Ed Markey (D-Mass.) said it risks “enabling nuclear proliferation in Saudi Arabia and igniting an arms race in the region.” The timing is pointed: Washington is simultaneously bombing Iran in part to eliminate Tehran’s enrichment capability.

The Left-Leaning Take

The synthesized left frame is a glaring contradiction and a giveaway to an autocratic partner: Washington wages a costly, unpopular war to deny Iran enrichment while handing Saudi Arabia a path to the very same capability without gold-standard safeguards, inviting a regional arms race. The transactional, commerce-first structure — U.S. reactor contracts, tens of billions in deals — is read as Trump prioritizing business and MBS over decades of nonproliferation policy. (No dedicated in-window Mother Jones / Vox / Slate / Guardian column surfaced — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The pro-deal argument, echoed in administration framing and among Gulf-security hawks, is that locking the Saudis into U.S. technology, fuel and contracts beats ceding the kingdom to Russian or Chinese vendors with weaker safeguards, and anchors Riyadh to Washington amid the Iran war. Skeptics on the right worry about proliferation and the precedent of enrichment-on-soil. (No dedicated in-window NR / Dispatch / WSJ editorial on the Saudi deal surfaced — provisional.)

Middle East · Regional War

The Gulf war becomes an energy crisis: the Houthis strike two Saudi tankers, Hormuz traffic all but halts, and Brent tops $98

+

The conflict escalated into a Gulf-wide energy shock overnight into July 23. U.S. Central Command carried out a 12th consecutive night of strikes on Iran, saying the aim was “to further degrade Iran’s ability to threaten civilian mariners and commercial vessels transiting regional waters.” President Trump warned in a written statement that any time Iran fires at a ship in the Strait of Hormuz, “the United States will bomb and destroy ONE BRIDGE OR POWER PLANT,” while Iran warned that “no infrastructure will be safe” absent security guarantees. Yemen’s Houthis said they had hit two Saudi oil tankers — identified as the Encelia and Layla — with missiles and drones in the Red Sea, days after declaring a naval blockade on Saudi shipping. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Iran was “not ready to make a deal,” dampening ceasefire hopes tied to the collapsed June 14 U.S.–Iran memorandum.

The market reaction was severe: Brent crude jumped roughly 4.6% to about $98.44 a barrel, its highest since late May, with WTI near $90.14 — putting Brent on course for a monthly gain of about 35%, one of its biggest in a decade. Shipping through the Strait of Hormuz has nearly stopped, with Kpler data showing only three commercial vessels transiting on Tuesday. On the legal front, analysts note that UN Security Council Resolution 2817, adopted March 11, 2026, condemned Iran’s attacks as breaches of international law and reaffirmed freedom-of-navigation rights, though the doctrinal fight over whether the full transit-passage regime binds Iran remains unresolved.

Europe · Defense

Greece moves to buy a €3.5 billion Israeli air-defense package — an “Achilles Shield” of David’s Sling, Barak and SPYDER

+

Greece’s government security council, KYSEA, was expected to give final approval on Thursday, July 23, for the purchase of a multi-layer air-defense system from Israel worth up to €3.5 billion (about $4 billion), along with several types of drones, according to Reuters. Athens wants to build a multi-layer anti-ballistic, anti-aircraft and anti-drone architecture dubbed “Achilles Shield,” built around Israeli radars and missiles — the David’s Sling and SPYDER systems from Rafael and the Barak MX air-and-missile-defense system from Israel Aerospace Industries.

The deal reflects a rapid Greek military build-up: defense spending is estimated at about $8.2 billion in 2026 (roughly 3.1% of GDP), and the country aims to allocate some €28 billion by 2036 to modernize its forces, including F-35 procurement and naval upgrades. It also deepens the Athens–Jerusalem defense relationship at a moment of acute regional tension, extending a partnership that began with an earlier air-force training deal.

Technology · AI Chips

AMD unveils its Nvidia-challenging “Helios” AI rack and a 2nm EPYC “Venice” CPU at Advancing AI 2026

+

At its Advancing AI 2026 event in San Francisco (July 22–23), AMD CEO Lisa Su launched Helios, a double-wide, rack-scale AI system that packs 72 Instinct MI455X accelerators alongside EPYC “Venice” CPUs and Pensando “Vulcano” 800 Gbps NICs — delivering roughly 3 AI exaflops of compute and 31 terabytes of HBM4 memory per rack. Helios is priced around $5–5.5 million per rack and, with EPYC Venice and the MI400 GPU family, is slated to ship in the second half of 2026. Venice is the first x86 server CPU built on TSMC’s 2-nanometer process, with AMD claiming up to 1.7× the performance of the prior EPYC generation. The launch is a direct shot at Nvidia’s Grace Blackwell and Vera Rubin rack systems.

The event doubled as a customer showcase: AMD confirmed Microsoft Azure as a Helios buyer, joining Meta, Oracle and OpenAI, and said OpenAI and Meta together account for roughly 12 gigawatts of committed AMD accelerator capacity — anchored by OpenAI’s multi-generation deal to deploy 6 GW of AMD GPUs starting with a 1 GW tranche of MI450-class parts in the second half of 2026. OpenAI president and co-founder Greg Brockman appeared onstage with Su, and AMD teased a future MI500 generation it claims will deliver up to a 1,000× AI-performance increase over current parts.

Sources: CNBC, DataCenterDynamics, Tech Times, Yahoo Finance

Biomedicine · Antibiotic Resistance

Researchers “revive” vancomycin against a resistant superbug by disabling a single bacterial enzyme

+

A study on vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium (VREfm) — a common and dangerous hospital-acquired infection — featured July 22 by ScienceDaily, shows that the antibiotic can be made effective again by knocking out a bacterial enzyme called SagA (secreted antigen A), which remodels the microbe’s peptidoglycan cell wall. When researchers genetically deleted SagA, the bacteria became markedly more susceptible to vancomycin; pairing the drug with a small-molecule inhibitor of the enzyme reproduced the effect pharmacologically, restoring vancomycin’s ability to kill the resistant strain.

Because SagA is conserved across a wide range of VRE strains, the team frames it as an attractive broad target for “resistance-breaker” therapies, and suggests the same strategy — a chemical helper that re-sensitizes bacteria — could rescue other failing antibiotics. (The underlying peer-reviewed paper appeared in Nature Communications in mid-2026; the July 22 ScienceDaily write-up is the news hook rather than the original publication date.)

Sources: ScienceDaily, Nature Communications, EurekAlert
Astronomy · High-Energy Astrophysics

Astronomers pin down a Milky Way “PeVatron” near Altair — a natural particle accelerator that dwarfs anything built on Earth

+

A Hiroshima University–led team reports it has conclusively identified the source powering some of the galaxy’s highest-energy cosmic-ray protons: an object cataloged as LHAASO J1912+1014u, sitting in the constellation Aquila near the bright star Altair. By combining gamma-ray, radio and X-ray data from three separate observatories, the researchers ruled out competing explanations and confirmed the object as a genuine “PeVatron” — a source capable of accelerating protons to peta-electronvolt energies, far beyond human-built colliders.

The object was first flagged in 2024; the confirming analysis was published in The Astrophysical Journal on July 16, 2026, and drew a wave of coverage this week (slightly outside the strict 24-hour window, flagged for transparency). The result gives astronomers a template for hunting similar accelerators across the galaxy, helping explain how the Milky Way sustains its most energetic particles.

Sources: Phys.org, EurekAlert, Universe Today
