The Supreme Court · Voting Rights

Split 6-3, the Supreme Court clears the administration to use its expanded citizenship database to screen voter rolls before the midterms

+

In an emergency order on Friday, Sept. 25, the Supreme Court stayed a June 22 district-court injunction and allowed the Department of Homeland Security to let state and local officials use its overhauled SAVE (Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements) system to check registered voters’ citizenship ahead of the Nov. 3 elections. The vote was 6-3 along ideological lines, with Justices Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson dissenting. The unsigned majority said the injunction impeded federal help to states, and stressed the system may be used for “individualized” checks while federal law still bars systematic voter purges within 90 days of an election.

SAVE, created by Congress in 1986 to verify immigration status for public benefits, was expanded after a March 2025 executive order and newly linked to Social Security records. The stay is not a merits ruling. In dissent, Justice Jackson wrote that “the harm caused by burdening or disenfranchising even a few lawful voters outweighs the nonexistent harm” to a government barred from an action it likely lacks authority to take. Election-administration reporters note the practical 2026 impact is limited by the 90-day window already in effect. (Facts drawn from the order and courts reporting; the stay rests on procedural grounds, not the merits.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads it as a green light for error-prone voter purges: the SAVE database has repeatedly misflagged naturalized and eligible citizens as noncitizens, and they point to states running millions of registrations through such checks to catch a literal handful of cases — evidence, they argue, that the “fraud” problem is vanishingly small while the disenfranchisement risk is real. The recurring complaint: the conservative majority is again using the shadow docket to hand the administration an election-machinery edge weeks before votes are cast. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right frames it as a common-sense election-integrity win: it should not require an emergency Supreme Court petition for the government to use its own data to keep noncitizens off the rolls, and the Court’s insistence on individualized checks answers the accuracy worries. DHS called the ruling a “victory for election integrity,” and the Solicitor General had argued the injunction “threatens the integrity of upcoming elections.” (Directional.)

Press Freedom · The White House

Days after a judge restored its access, the White House cuts CNN from the Air Force One pool — and rival networks refuse to fill the seat

+

On Saturday, Sept. 26, the White House left CNN off the Air Force One press pool for President Trump’s trip to the Tennessee–Texas football game in Knoxville, listing the conservative outlet Real America’s Voice in its place. CNN is a rotating member of the network video pool; the other television members — CBS, ABC, NBC and Fox News — declined to step in as a substitute, leaving no pool video of the trip.

The move came less than 48 hours after U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly issued an overnight order early Thursday, Sept. 25 blocking Trump’s ban on CNN, MS NOW and Politico from White House grounds, finding the government’s national-security rationale “lacked factual support” and that press badges had been revoked without “constitutionally adequate due process.” The three outlets, barred roughly a week earlier, had sued Sept. 21 on First and Fifth Amendment grounds. By excluding CNN from a pool assignment rather than from the grounds, the administration appeared to route around the court order; the DOJ had defended the original ban by citing national security and calling access a “privilege.” (Facts drawn from Tier-1 media and press-freedom reporting and Judge Kelly’s order.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left calls it a petty and likely unconstitutional workaround: having lost in court, the White House simply found another lever to shut out a network it dislikes. This is viewpoint discrimination and retaliation for coverage, they argue, and the rival networks’ refusal to scab shows even competitors see the precedent as dangerous. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Here the mainstream right largely breaks with the White House: National Review ran “Trump Should Rescind the White House Press Ban,” opposing the exclusion on First Amendment grounds, and libertarians (Reason) treat punishing outlets for their coverage as exactly the government retaliation the Constitution forbids. It is a case where much of the right aligns with the left on the legal principle, even as some pro-Trump media applaud the exclusion. (Directional.)

Iran · United States

Trump rejects Iran’s seven-day plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz — as Tehran says it is still awaiting an official U.S. answer

+

President Trump on Saturday, Sept. 26 publicly confirmed he had rejected Iran’s latest proposal to wind down the war. Tehran’s offer — conveyed through Qatari mediators and surfaced at the U.N. General Assembly earlier in the week — would have reopened the Strait of Hormuz and restarted nuclear and peace talks within seven days in exchange for the U.S. lifting its naval blockade of Iranian ports, waiving oil sanctions and releasing frozen Iranian funds, plus a regional ceasefire. “They made a proposal but I rejected it,” Trump told reporters, adding that Iran wants to reopen the strait immediately “because they’re losing so badly.” The Wall Street Journal reported Friday, citing U.S. officials, that Trump has told aides he expects to resume the bombing campaign after the Nov. 3 midterms.

Accounts diverge on where things stand: Trump and U.S. outlets (WaPo, Bloomberg, NPR) describe a flat rejection, while Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Saturday that Tehran had “yet to receive an official US position” and that only a negotiated solution can break the deadlock. The plan closely resembles a June 2026 U.S.–Iran memorandum that collapsed over vague wording on who controls the strait; the war itself began with joint U.S.–Israeli strikes on Feb. 28, 2026, and an April ceasefire and the June memorandum both failed before fighting resumed over the summer. With Hormuz still effectively closed, the standoff keeps global shipping and oil markets exposed, with crude trading near the $90s and pump prices elevated. (Framing disagreement flagged: WSJ/CNBC call the offer a “conditional ceasefire proposal”; AP/WaPo/Al Jazeera frame it as a Hormuz-reopening roadmap.)

Science · Planetary Science

Where did Mars hide its carbon? New models say percolating groundwater buried the planet’s ‘missing’ carbonates underground

+

A geochemical modeling study led by Chang-Chin Wang, with Professor Tomohiro Usui (JAXA / University of Tokyo) and Associate Professor Mohit Melwani Daswani (Earth-Life Science Institute, Institute of Science Tokyo), published Sept. 26, 2026, offers an answer to a long-standing Martian puzzle: why the surface shows far fewer carbonate rocks than expected for a planet once thought to have abundant liquid water and a thick CO2-rich atmosphere. Using one-dimensional thermochemical models of water percolating through columns of mafic versus feldspar-rich rock under cold, CO2-rich early-Mars conditions, the team found that the type of rock the water reacts with — not just the presence of water — governs whether calcium/iron-rich or magnesium-rich carbonates form.

Feldspar-rich rocks, increasingly detected on the Martian surface, readily produce the calcium/iron-rich carbonates seen at many sites, and the simulations suggest groundwater could quietly bury carbonates underground, explaining their apparent surface scarcity. If correct, a substantial reservoir of the planet’s early carbon may simply be out of sight rather than lost. (This is a model-supported interpretation: the buried reservoir is inferred from simulations, not yet directly observed.)

Energy · Nuclear

Google will pay to squeeze more power from Georgia’s nuclear plants, adding about 96 MW as AI demand climbs

+

Announced Sept. 25, 2026 (with a press release Sept. 21), Georgia Power and Google struck a deal in which Google will finance capacity “uprates” on Georgia Power’s owned portions of the Plant Vogtle (near Augusta) and Plant Hatch (near Baxley) nuclear units, adding approximately 96 megawatts of new capacity to the grid. Google’s subscription is projected to deliver roughly $900 million in benefits to customers over the life of the units.

Uprates expand a reactor’s output through equipment upgrades rather than new construction. The arrangement uses a new tariff/subscription program that lets large customers like Google pay for the work, and it remains subject to Georgia Public Service Commission approval. It is another instance of a hyperscaler directly funding firm, around-the-clock nuclear generation to meet surging data-center and AI electricity demand. (Terms per Georgia Power and energy-policy coverage; the deal awaits regulatory sign-off.)

Energy · EVs & Batteries

Geely says its AI ‘pulse restoration’ can reverse fast-charge battery wear — and add up to 20% more cycle life

+

Geely Auto Group introduced its next-generation AI-powered “Geely Smart Charging” system (announced Sept. 23; covered by Electrek Sept. 25). The headline claim is a lithium-ion “pulse restoration” technique that sends micro-pulse currents through cells to reactivate lithium ions that accumulate at the negative electrode during repeated fast charging — which Geely says can reverse some fast-charge damage and add up to 20% more battery cycle life. The system’s AI aligns charging power with the battery’s real-time condition.

In company tests, the Lynk & Co 10 and Zeekr 001 charged 10%–70% in 4 min 30 sec and 10%–97% in 8 min 40 sec. Geely’s fifth-generation charging station supports peak power up to 2,250 kW; its Shendun “Golden Battery” supports a peak 12C charge rate, and a next-generation Ultra Short Blade battery cuts charging heat about 10% while raising the peak rate to 6C. (The cycle-life and charging figures are manufacturer claims from Geely’s own testing, not independently verified.)
