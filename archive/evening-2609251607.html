Congress · War Powers

The Senate defeats a bid to end the Iran war by a single vote — four Republicans break with Trump, and Fetterman breaks with his party

+

The Senate on Thursday, Sept. 24 narrowly rejected a war-powers resolution that would have directed President Trump to pull U.S. forces out of hostilities with Iran, failing 49–50. Four Republicans — Rand Paul (Ky.), Susan Collins (Maine), Lisa Murkowski (Alaska) and Thom Tillis (N.C.) — crossed over to support it, while Pennsylvania’s John Fetterman, a staunch backer of Israel, was the lone Democrat to vote no. The resolution was non-binding, but under the War Powers Resolution it forced senators on record over a war Congress never voted to authorize.

It was not the chamber’s first pass at the question: both the House and Senate approved a war-powers measure in June, which the White House ignored, arguing it infringed on the president’s authority as commander-in-chief. The vote lands amid U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran that began Feb. 28 and a war that has grown politically costly — rising gas prices are unsettling the midterm map — even as most Republicans stuck with Trump. (Vote tally and floor details per Tier-1 wire and courts/national-security reporting; the resolution’s legal force is contested by the White House.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a Congress abdicating its constitutional power to declare war, letting an unauthorized and unpopular conflict grind on while casualties and pump prices mount. Fetterman’s defection, in this reading, handed Trump cover for a war voters never sanctioned — and the one-vote margin shows how close the guardrail came to holding. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right frames the resolution as a symbolic Democratic maneuver that would tie the commander-in-chief’s hands mid-conflict. But the fault line runs through the coalition: libertarians and constitutionalists (Reason; Rand Paul) argue Congress must reclaim the war power, and anti-Trump conservatives (The Bulwark — an intra-right dissent) call the party’s deference a failure of nerve, while hawks insist the strikes are working. (Directional.)

Sources: NPR, NBC News, CNBC, Washington Post
Press Freedom · The Courts

A judge orders the White House to let CNN, Politico and MS NOW back in — on due-process grounds, not the First Amendment

+

U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly in the early hours of Thursday, Sept. 24 ordered the Trump administration to temporarily restore White House access for reporters from CNN, MS NOW and Politico, whose hard passes were revoked Sept. 19 after the president, in a Truth Social post, accused them of peddling “FICTION and LIES.” Kelly’s 14-day restraining order found the outlets likely to win their suit — but rested chiefly on the Fifth Amendment’s due-process guarantee rather than the First: the journalists, he wrote, got no notice and no chance to be heard before losing their credentials. The reporters were back on the grounds by around noon.

The fight is far from over. The Justice Department defended the ban, calling White House access a “privilege” and invoking national security, and Kelly signaled a further hearing on whether to make his order permanent. Even after the ruling, the administration kept the shared TV pool that supplies video of White House events suspended, prompting networks to threaten a boycott of pool duty until CNN’s slot is restored. (Legal framing weighted toward courts reporting; ruling rested on due process, a nuance several outlets flagged.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left casts the ban as a naked attempt to punish critical coverage and intimidate the press — and notes that even a courtroom defeat has not stopped the White House from keeping the TV pool dark. That the outlets prevailed on due process rather than free-speech grounds, they warn, leaves the deeper First Amendment threat unresolved and invites a do-over. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Parts of the mainstream right defend the move as executive discretion over who gets a coveted press pass, echoing the DOJ’s “privilege” framing. But libertarians (Reason) and legal conservatives (The Dispatch) are wary of government retaliating against speech it dislikes, and anti-Trump voices (The Bulwark — an intra-right dissent) call it an authoritarian tell. Even sympathetic editorialists concede the process was sloppy. (Directional.)

Sources: CNN, NPR, NBC News, Washington Post

Iran · United States

A war fought from the podium: Trump vows he could ‘annihilate’ Iran, and Pezeshkian answers that Tehran will not surrender

+

The U.S.–Iran war spilled onto the UN stage this week. In his Sept. 22 General Assembly address, Trump issued one of his sharpest threats yet, warning that if negotiations fail he could “annihilate the Islamic Republic” — and do it quickly. A day later, Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian stepped to the same rostrum, an all-but-unprecedented sight: no leader is known to have addressed the UN in person while his country was actively at war with the United States. He accused Trump of a “bullying mentality,” declared Iran would not surrender and would fight “until our last breath,” yet insisted Tehran still believes in diplomacy to end the conflict.

The exchange framed the central uncertainty of a war now more than half a year old: whether the two governments are edging toward talks or toward a wider escalation. Pezeshkian’s blend of defiance and openness to negotiation mirrors Tehran’s bind — battered by U.S. and Israeli strikes yet unwilling to be seen capitulating — while Trump’s maximalist rhetoric leaves little visible daylight for a face-saving off-ramp. US–China · The White House

Trump and Xi close a summit heavy on pomp and light on substance — a two-month truce, warm words on AI, and no movement on Taiwan or chips

+

President Trump and Xi Jinping wrapped their Washington summit on Friday, Sept. 25 with a tea at the White House and a joint tour of the National Archives, capping a three-day state visit — Xi’s first to the United States in 11 years. Thursday’s roughly three-hour bilateral meeting produced modest, mostly symbolic results: an extension of the U.S.–China trade truce by two months, through Jan. 10, a halving of U.S. “fentanyl tariffs” to 10%, a Chinese commitment to resume U.S. soybean purchases, and a postponement of Beijing’s planned rare-earth export controls. Both leaders singled out artificial intelligence as an area for cooperation, and Trump touted increased market access for American farmers.

The harder questions went unresolved. Xi pressed Washington to “handle the Taiwan question with prudence” and sought assurances against Taiwanese independence that did not materialize; there was no new deal on advanced semiconductors, and Xi again raised the Iran war, urging a return to U.S.–Tehran talks. Multiple outlets characterized the summit as strong on pageantry — including a state dinner studded with U.S. tech titans — but thin on breakthroughs, an uneasy truce that steadied a volatile relationship without settling any of its central disputes. (Truce terms per Treasury and official read-outs; summit assessment synthesized from Tier-1 wire and news coverage.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads the lavish welcome as flattery for an authoritarian who conceded little, and the two-month truce as evidence that Trump’s tariff brinkmanship has left the U.S. bargaining from weakness — a placeholder to hold prices down through the midterms rather than a strategic win, with the tech-mogul dinner underscoring who really sets the agenda. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Free-traders and libertarians (Reason) welcome any de-escalation of a consumer-taxing tariff war. Trump’s populist base cheers a president dealing “from strength,” while never-Trump voices (The Dispatch, an intra-right dissent) note that a two-month punt is not the “great deal” promised. (Directional.)

Immigration · The Supreme Court

The administration asks the Supreme Court to restart fast ‘third-country’ deportations after an appeals court grounds a flight of 70

+

The Trump administration on Thursday, Sept. 24 asked the Supreme Court to clear the way for continued rapid deportations of migrants to “third countries” — nations other than their own, where they may have no ties. The emergency application followed a 1st U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals ruling issued Wednesday just before midnight, which the government says forced the cancellation of a flight carrying 70 people bound for three countries. Lower courts have held that migrants must get notice and a chance to object before being sent to a third country, on the ground that they could face persecution or torture there.

At the center of the fight is the Convention Against Torture, ratified by the Senate in 1994, which bars removal to a country where a person is likely to be tortured — but is vague about what process is owed. The administration argues the injunctions hamstring its enforcement; challengers say fast removals to unfamiliar countries risk exactly the harm the treaty forbids. Reporting indicates roughly 25,000 migrants have been removed under the policy, most to Mexico but thousands to more than two dozen nations, from Liberia to Guyana. (Facts drawn from the filings and courts coverage; legal framing weighted toward courts and national-security reporting.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees an end-run around due process: shipping people who have never set foot in a country to places where they may be jailed or tortured, then racing to the Supreme Court to strip away the modest hearing lower judges required. The Convention Against Torture, they argue, exists precisely to stop this, and the secrecy around the destination deals compounds the danger. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right frames removals as core executive authority over immigration that unelected district judges keep obstructing, and welcomes the administration testing it at the high court. Libertarians (Reason) are uneasy, warning that outsourcing deportations to abusive regimes without a real hearing invites grave error. Legal conservatives (The Dispatch) counter that the process due is narrow, not nonexistent. (Directional.)

Sources: Washington Post, CNN, U.S. News / AP

Ethiopia · Horn of Africa

War returns to Ethiopia’s Tigray as an offensive shatters the 2022 peace deal and spreads into Afar and Amhara

+

Fighting has erupted anew in northern Ethiopia, threatening a return to the full-scale war that killed hundreds of thousands before a 2022 truce. Beginning around Sept. 23, forces aligned with the Tigray People’s Liberation Front and allied militias — including the Fano — launched an offensive, reportedly seizing airports in Mekelle, Shire and Axum and pushing into the neighboring Afar and Amhara regions; Ethiopian Airlines suspended flights to the three cities. The African Union commission chief called for “immediate de-escalation” and a return to talks. Analysts warn the mix of internal Tigrayan splits, Amhara grievances and cross-border dynamics with Eritrea makes this flare-up especially combustible. United Nations · Succession

The race to succeed Guterres tightens: Costa Rica’s Grynspan leads the straw polls, but no candidate has cleared the veto threat

+

With António Guterres due to complete his second term on Dec. 31, 2026, the contest to lead the UN has moved to the front burner during General Assembly week. In the Security Council’s third informal straw poll, Costa Rica’s Rebeca Grynspan — the veteran head of the UN trade-and-development body UNCTAD and a former vice president — led the field with nine “encourage” votes to five “discourage,” just ahead of Guyana’s Carolyn Rodrigues-Birkett (eight to six). The math is daunting: a winner needs at least nine “encourage” votes and no veto from the five permanent members — a bar none of the eight declared candidates has yet cleared. There is broad appetite for the UN’s first woman Secretary-General and, by regional rotation, a Latin American, and the field is female-heavy (Grynspan, Rodrigues-Birkett, Chile’s Bachelet, Ecuador’s Espinosa). The jockeying unfolds against a backdrop of budget cuts, Security Council gridlock and open doubts about the institution’s relevance. Technology · AI & Biology

An AI model claims a first: Anthropic says Claude autonomously found a CRISPR-like enzyme system in phage DNA

+

Anthropic on Sept. 23 unveiled a new life-sciences research group and disclosed an early result: its Claude model, running autonomously, flagged a previously uncharacterized enzyme system in bacteriophage (virus) DNA after sifting a database of roughly 1.9 billion protein clusters. The company calls it array-associated reverse transcriptases (ART) — a reverse-transcriptase enzyme, a partner gene, and a long run of evenly spaced DNA repeats whose layout echoes the guide-RNA arrays that make CRISPR programmable. The autonomous campaign spanned about 21.5 hours, 949 agent sessions and 215 million tokens.

The caveats are large: Anthropic says it has not yet determined what ART does, and wet-lab experiments to characterize it are ongoing. Independent scientists will need to reproduce and probe the finding before any claim about a new gene-editing toolkit can stand. Still, the announcement is a notable data point in a fast-moving debate over whether AI systems can generate genuine, testable biological hypotheses at scale rather than merely summarize known literature. (Claims and metrics are the company’s own; results are not yet peer-reviewed.)

Energy · Solar & Storage

Solar and batteries made up 70% of new US power capacity in the first half of 2026 — and storage is heading for a record year

+

The U.S. grid keeps tilting toward clean power: solar and battery storage together accounted for about 70% of all new generating capacity added in the first half of 2026, according to industry data. Developers installed 11.4 GW of new solar in the second quarter alone — up roughly 45% year-over-year — and the country is on track to add a record amount of battery capacity this year, with one industry report projecting some 71 GWh of new storage in 2026. California and Texas continue to lead on utility-scale batteries.

The batteries matter as much as the panels: cheaper storage lets solar power be shifted into the evening peak, turning “daytime solar” into round-the-clock supply and improving its economics against gas. The persistent caveat is demand — electricity use is climbing fast on the back of AI data centers, electric vehicles and electrified heating — so much of the clean build-out is racing to meet new load rather than retire existing fossil plants. Technology · Robotics

MIT’s paper-clip-weight flying robot learns insect agility — 10 somersaults in 11 seconds and 447% faster flight

+

Engineers at MIT report that their tiny aerial microrobot — about 4 centimeters across and lighter than a paper clip — can now fly with something close to insect agility. A new two-part AI controller, built around a “tube” model-predictive controller devised by aeronautics engineer Jonathan How, let the flapping-wing bot fly about 447% faster with a 255% jump in acceleration and reel off 10 continuous somersaults in 11 seconds while holding to within 4–5 centimeters of its planned path.

The trick is splitting the problem: one layer plans aggressive, gymnastic trajectories while a second, computationally lean layer keeps the flight stable in real time on limited onboard hardware. The researchers frame the advance as a step toward swarms of insect-scale robots that could one day pollinate crops in vertical farms or thread through earthquake rubble and other tight, hazardous spaces where conventional drones cannot go. (Performance figures are from the research team’s reported experiments.)

Science · Planetary Science

Uranus’s moon Ariel may once have hidden an ocean 100 miles deep — more than half the little moon’s volume

+

A new analysis suggests the small Uranian moon Ariel once harbored a subsurface ocean more than 100 miles (170 km) deep beneath its cracked icy shell. By mapping craters, ridges and trench-like grooves on the surface and modeling the tidal stresses that would have fractured the crust, planetary scientists reconstructed how thick the shell was before it broke — and concluded the buried ocean may once have made up roughly 55% of the whole moon’s volume, sitting atop a core about 740 km across.

The work, published in the journal Icarus, adds Ariel to a growing list of candidate “ocean worlds” and, combined with similar hints from the neighboring moon Miranda, strengthens the case that the distant Uranus system could host multiple hidden (or once-hidden) seas. Researchers say it sharpens the scientific rationale for a dedicated Uranus flagship mission — a long-standing priority for planetary scientists — to go back and look directly. (Ocean depth and volume are model-derived estimates from surface geology and tidal modeling, not direct measurement.)
