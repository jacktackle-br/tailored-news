Courts · Mail Voting

The Supreme Court could rule any day on Trump’s mail-ballot rule as challengers’ response falls due and ballots go out

+

The Trump administration has asked the Supreme Court — for a third time — to let the U.S. Postal Service begin enforcing new restrictions on how mail ballots are distributed before the Nov. 3 midterms. The rules stem from Trump’s March 2026 executive order on election “integrity” and a late-August USPS “Final Rule” that would require states to hand the Postal Service lists of voters eligible to receive mail ballots, use federally reviewed, uniquely barcoded envelopes, and direct postal workers to deliver ballots only to voters on the state-provided lists. A coalition of 23 states plus the District of Columbia (with Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro) sued, arguing USPS lacks statutory authority, that the rule violates the Voting Rights Act, and that it intrudes on states’ constitutional power to run elections. U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani (D. Mass.) blocked the rule around Sept. 4–5, finding the plaintiffs likely to succeed and warning that a rule issued fewer than 70 days before the election “threatens disenfranchisement of millions.”

The Justice Department filed its emergency application over the weekend, and the Court directed the challenger states to respond by 10 a.m. EDT on Tuesday, Sept. 8; Talwani’s temporary restraining order was set to expire Sept. 10, so a ruling could come at any time. The bid follows a late-August order in which the Court, 6–3, lifted an earlier injunction and let parts of the underlying executive order proceed. North Carolina became the first state to begin mailing November ballots, on Sept. 4, sharpening the timing. Legal analysts at Lawfare stress that the delivery rule is designed to work alongside a parallel DHS/Social Security data-matching component that would cross-reference the state lists to decide whose ballots may be mailed. (Legal/elections story weighted to Tier 1 wire desks, court reporting and Lawfare for the facts; as of Tuesday morning the justices had not ruled, and the order’s ultimate merits remain undecided.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads this as engineered “mail-ballot chaos” — an unprecedented federal grab over a domain the Constitution assigns to the states, timed so close to Election Day that the confusion itself suppresses turnout. They lean on Talwani’s finding that the executive branch has no authority to regulate elections and argue the aim is disenfranchisement and pre-emptively delegitimizing the result. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The administration lane frames the rule as routine election-integrity — making sure mail ballots reach only verified, eligible voters — and says a single district judge shouldn’t override postal and executive authority on the eve of a vote. But a notable countercurrent on the right, labeled as such, warns that years of attacks on mail voting are backfiring by depressing Republicans’ own mail-ballot use; a Reason-style libertarian strand adds that federalizing ballot handling by fiat concentrates power whoever wields it. (Directional; intra-right worry labeled.)

Sources: NBC News, CNN, The Hill, NPR, Lawfare
Pentagon · Civil-Military

Hegseth polygraphs officials to hunt leakers and installs ‘military influencers’ as a Republican senator’s call to fire him hangs over the building

+

The Pentagon remains in sustained turmoil as Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth consolidates control. Army Secretary Dan Driscoll — at 38 the youngest-ever Army secretary and a close ally of Vice President JD Vance — resigned this week after months of clashes with Hegseth over modernization and readiness, telling the administration his efforts were being blocked by Hegseth’s firing of the generals responsible for them. By multiple counts, at least 25 senior military and civilian leaders have been fired, forced out or retired early since Hegseth took office in January 2025 — among them the chairman of the Joint Chiefs, the Navy’s top admiral and the Army chief of staff — and he has removed at least 45 officers from promotion lists in 2026. The Army is now without a Senate-confirmed civilian secretary, a confirmed top general, or a confirmed commander of its forces in Europe, all during the six-month-old war with Iran. (Outlets differ on the Driscoll timing: CNN and Axios date the announcement to Aug. 31, NPR and The Hill to Monday, Sept. 1.)

The story advanced over the last day or two on several fronts. Fresh reporting (The Hill, Sept. 7) says the department administered polygraph tests to high-ranking officials to find press leakers, and that at least three retired officers — Cols. Rob Maness, Kurt Schlichter and Thomas Anderson — were handed undisclosed civilian roles to amplify Hegseth’s agenda on social media. Republican Sen. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.) has publicly urged Trump to replace Hegseth, citing “inept management,” a “leadership void,” and a secretary “intimidated by competence.” Betting markets cited by Newsweek still put a near-term departure as unlikely (roughly 9% before Oct. 1). The White House has not moved to replace him, and Trump continues to back his secretary. (Facts corroborated across The Hill, Axios, NPR and TIME.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads a loyalty-driven hollowing-out of an apolitical officer corps — dangerous precisely because it is happening during a shooting war — and stresses the disproportionate removal of Black, Hispanic and women officers. The polygraphs and paid “military influencer” hires read as authoritarian consolidation substituting for accountability. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream-right split is unusually open. Administration defenders (Fox, Sens. Rick Scott and Mike Lee) praise Hegseth for clearing out “woke” bureaucracy and restoring a “warrior ethos.” But The Dispatch is sharply critical, arguing he substitutes ruthlessness for strategy. Further out, the anti-Trump right — The Bulwark and Atlantic writers — calls him “singularly unqualified”; that is intra-party dissent, labeled as such, not the mainstream-right position, which has largely defended the housecleaning. (Directional; intra-right disagreement labeled.)

Elections · 2026 Midterms

The 2026 midterm season opens with both chambers in play — and a GOP fretting its own base is flagging

+

With Labor Day past, NPR and affiliated public-radio desks marked Tuesday, Sept. 8 as the unofficial start of the general-election season, two months from the Nov. 3 vote. Republicans hold narrow majorities in both chambers; the party in power typically loses ground in a midterm, and 61 House members plus two non-voting delegates (26 Democrats, 37 Republicans) have announced retirements. The decisive contests run through Republican-leaning terrain — Iowa, Texas, Ohio and Alaska — and control of the Senate is described as no longer a given for either party. Republicans are staging an unusual midterm convention in Dallas this week to energize a base that reporting characterizes as flagging, and New Hampshire held state and federal primaries on Sept. 8.

Coverage identifies the issues shaping the environment: the war in Iran, immigration, tariffs, gas prices and overall affordability. Analysts describe a national mood tilting toward Democrats — driven by Trump’s second-term unpopularity and Democratic enthusiasm — even as strong GOP-state incumbency and candidate quality complicate any wave narrative. Texas is flagged as a wildcard, with Republicans uneasy about the Senate race and hoping Trump’s fundraising can lift nominee Ken Paxton. (This is a softer framing story pegged to an NPR wire piece; the specific races and the national environment will move as autumn progresses.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads the map as a referendum on an unpopular second term — the Iran war, tariffs, affordability — and argues Democratic enthusiasm plus a wave of GOP retirements put both chambers genuinely within reach, with the mail-ballot fight seen as an attempt to blunt that advantage. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream-right counters that structural advantages — a Senate map running through red states, incumbency and Trump’s war chest — can hold the line, while warning that self-inflicted wounds (the Hegseth chaos, depressed GOP mail-ballot use) and convention theatrics risk demoralizing rather than mobilizing the base. (Directional.)

Europe · Russia–Ukraine War

Trump works the phones after the Witkoff–Kushner shuttle, but Zelensky warns ‘the war will continue’

+

U.S. special envoy Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner wrapped a weekend of shuttle diplomacy that produced no ceasefire. They met Vladimir Putin at the Kremlin on Saturday, Sept. 5 — more than three hours of closed-door talks, Witkoff’s eighth trip to Russia since February 2025 — then made their first wartime visit to Kyiv on Sunday, Sept. 6 to brief President Volodymyr Zelensky. Putin ordered a 72-hour pause on Russian strikes against Kyiv to coincide with the delegation’s visit, and Ukraine separately ordered its own temporary halt in fighting, but both sides stressed no wider ceasefire was reached. Witkoff publicly touted “substantive progress” and said Washington hopes to return to trilateral U.S.–Ukraine–Russia negotiations.

Trump was to be briefed by his envoys and then call Putin first, followed by Zelensky — the calls expected Monday, Sept. 7. Zelensky struck a more downbeat note: “We expect the war will continue,” while adding that Ukraine is counting on U.S. energy and air-defense support through another wartime winter. This is the first real re-engagement of the Witkoff/Kushner channel after roughly a six-month lull, but the readouts point to process, not substance — no territorial framework, prisoner deal or ceasefire terms surfaced publicly, and the dueling unilateral pauses functioned as goodwill gestures around the visit rather than a durable truce. (Corroborated across CNN, NBC, Al Jazeera, The Hill and the Kyiv Independent; no official readout of the Trump–Putin and Trump–Zelensky calls had surfaced as of Tuesday morning — treat their outcome as pending.)

Middle East · US–Iran War

The US–Iran ‘tanker war’ widens in the Strait of Hormuz as oil jumps and Iran signals a new exclusion zone

+

The U.S. and Iran are trading tit-for-tat strikes on shipping around the Strait of Hormuz, and oil prices jumped on Monday, Sept. 7. U.S. Central Command said American forces struck three Iranian oil tankers on Saturday, Sept. 5 — including one near Kharg Island, Iran’s main export hub — after IRGC forces fired ballistic missiles toward a U.S. aircraft carrier and destroyer; CENTCOM said it “permanently disabled” two tankers and “completely destroyed” a third. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said they in turn attacked three oil tankers and three U.S.-linked vessels. U.S. officials describe a new “tanker-for-tanker” policy approved by Trump — the first time the U.S. has struck Iranian tankers in retaliation rather than to enforce a blockade. Iran’s security-council secretary Mohsen Rezaei said a restricted zone outside the strait would be announced within days.

Markets moved on it: Brent rose to roughly $96–$97 a barrel (up about 8% on the week and nearly 19% over the month), with WTI near $92. Bloomberg separately reported Iran saying an Oman-brokered Hormuz de-escalation deal is “days away” — the key countervailing signal against further exchanges. The shift from blockade-enforcement to offensive retaliation marks a real escalation, and with roughly a fifth of the world’s seaborne oil normally transiting Hormuz, the risk premium stays elevated. (Material dispute: the U.S. insists the strait is effectively open and under “total control” with about 30 escorted ships a day; Al Jazeera and CNN analyses of ship-tracking data suggest real throughput is meaningfully lower — a live factual disagreement over how much oil is actually moving.)

Germany · State Election

After the AfD’s landslide in Saxony-Anhalt, Merz reaffirms the ‘firewall’ — but the coalition math is now the problem

+

The far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) won Sunday’s Landtag election in Saxony-Anhalt with 43.8% of the vote per preliminary official results — more than double the 17.2% of Chancellor Friedrich Merz’s center-right CDU, led in the state by incumbent premier Sven Schulze. It is the party’s strongest state-election result to date. But the AfD took 39 of the chamber’s 83 seats, short of an outright majority, leaving government formation unresolved. Schulze conceded the result “hit us hard”; an AfD leader called it “a signal for the whole of Germany.”

A commanding plurality does not by itself deliver power. Over Sunday and Monday, Merz and senior CDU officials reaffirmed the “firewall” (Brandmauer) barring cooperation with the AfD, and analysts flag the left-populist BSW as a possible kingmaker while questioning whether the firewall holds at the state level. The result is a fresh blow to Merz, whose coalition is struggling to revive Europe’s largest economy, and the central question is now arithmetic: whether mainstream parties can assemble a viable government without the AfD — and whether the postwar firewall survives its sternest state-level test. (Figures are preliminary official results and may shift on final certification.)

Sources: NPR, Al Jazeera, Fortune, TIME, CNN

Technology · AI / M&A

Nvidia moves to buy Hugging Face for about $13 billion, reaching for control of open-model distribution

+

Nvidia has agreed to acquire Hugging Face, the open-source hub for AI models and datasets, in a deal valued at roughly $13 billion — one of the largest AI acquisitions to date and a move into a key distribution channel for open-weight models. Per Nvidia’s announcement and an SEC 8-K filing dated Sept. 2, 2026, the structure is about $11.9 billion payable to Hugging Face stockholders plus an equity-based retention program of up to roughly $1.0 billion for employees joining Nvidia. Hugging Face hosts a community it puts at more than 18 million developers sharing over 3 million models, 500,000 datasets and 1 million applications.

Nvidia says it will keep the platform open and continue supporting rival silicon vendors — a pledge that matters given the company’s dominance of AI training hardware and the antitrust scrutiny a deal this size invites. (Caveat: the agreement was announced Sept. 2–3 and is a deal, not a completed acquisition; it is subject to regulatory approvals and is expected to close in the first half of 2027.)

Biomedicine · Aging

A GLP-1 drug extended the lifespan of older mice — a first hint that the Ozempic class may touch aging itself

+

A study published in Nature on Sept. 2, 2026 reports that late-life treatment with semaglutide — the GLP-1 agonist sold as Ozempic and Wegovy — extended lifespan and slowed markers of aging in older female mice. Treatment began at 20 months of age, roughly equivalent to a 60-year-old human. Treated animals reached a median lifespan of 834 days versus 742 days for saline controls, about a 12% increase, and also showed improved muscle and cognitive function, better glucose control and exercise endurance, and reductions in aging hallmarks such as chronic inflammation and impaired regenerative capacity — benefits that on some measures exceeded calorie restriction alone.

The work, covered by the NIH, C&EN and UC Berkeley, adds to evidence that a blockbuster metabolic drug class may have effects reaching beyond weight and blood sugar into the biology of aging. (Caveats: this is an animal study in female mice, not humans; the longevity and healthspan claims have not been demonstrated in people, and human lifespan trials of this kind are impractical to run directly.)

Sources: Nature (paper), Nature (news), NIH, C&EN
Energy · Grid & Storage

Spain leads Europe on wind and solar — and is now hitting the wall for want of batteries and grid

+

Reporting published Sept. 6–8 (Bloomberg, syndicated by The Japan Times and Energy Connects) details how Spain — a European leader in wind and solar generation — is running into the limits of clean power without enough storage and grid capacity to absorb it. Spain had roughly 400 megawatts of installed battery storage in 2025, less than 2% of the ~22.5 gigawatts it targets by 2030. BloombergNEF expects installed capacity to more than triple in 2026, but investors have been deterred by delayed regulation and the absence of a capacity market that would pay batteries to be available.

To unblock it, the government has announced €13.6 billion (about $15.8 billion) of grid investment through the end of the decade, intended to raise high-voltage network capacity roughly 14-fold and unlock an estimated €7 billion of currently stalled industrial projects. The bottleneck is a preview of a broader European problem: renewables can be built faster than the wires and batteries needed to use them. (Caveat: this is analysis built on BloombergNEF data rather than a single hard announcement, and the storage-growth figures are projections.)

Sources: Bloomberg, The Japan Times, Energy Connects
Space · Planetary Science

After eight years in transit, BepiColombo begins its final approach to Mercury

+

The joint ESA–JAXA BepiColombo mission has entered its “arrival phase” after eight years in transit. Mission controllers confirmed that the Mercury Transfer Module — the ion-propulsion stage that has hauled the spacecraft across the inner solar system — separated from the stack on Sept. 3, 2026, with confirmation via ESA deep-space antennas in Spain and Argentina. The remaining stack comprises Europe’s Mercury Planetary Orbiter and Japan’s Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter (Mio).

Those two orbiters are now on a months-long final approach: orbit insertion around Mercury is planned for Nov. 21, 2026, the orbiters separate around Dec. 9–10, and science operations are slated to begin in April 2027. Launched in 2018, BepiColombo has performed a long series of gravity-assist flybys of Earth, Venus and Mercury to bleed off speed and fall into the Sun’s gravity well. (Caveat: this is a milestone — module separation and the start of the arrival phase — not orbital arrival, which is still months away.)

Sources: Sci.News, EarthSky, Starlust
