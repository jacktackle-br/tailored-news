Courts · Mail Voting

The Supreme Court could rule any day on Trump’s blocked USPS mail-ballot rule as the third emergency appeal sits fully briefed

+

The Trump administration has returned to the Supreme Court for a third time to try to enforce new limits on how mail ballots are distributed before the Nov. 3 midterms. The fight traces to a March 31, 2026 executive order directing the Department of Homeland Security to build federal “citizenship lists” and the U.S. Postal Service to police which voters may receive mail ballots; an Aug. 26 USPS “Final Rule” then required states to submit mail-voter names and addresses to a federal USPS portal and to adopt USPS-approved ballot-envelope designs and barcodes. A coalition of 23–25 states led by California sued. On Friday night, U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani (D. Mass.) issued a preliminary injunction barring the envelope and portal provisions, finding that implementing the rule fewer than 70 days before the election “threatens disenfranchisement of millions.” The Justice Department’s emergency application (docket 26A297) went to Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson as Circuit Justice for the First Circuit, who set a 10 a.m. ET Tuesday, Sept. 8 deadline for the states to respond. As of Wednesday morning the Court had not ruled on that third application, and Talwani’s injunction leaves the USPS rule blocked for now.

The posture matters. In late August the Court, 6–3 along ideological lines (Justices Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson dissenting), let parts of the underlying executive order proceed — the DHS citizenship list, the Attorney General’s prosecution priorities and USPS rulemaking on envelope design — but expressly declined to decide the most contested piece: whether USPS may withhold ballots from anyone not on a government-approved list. Sotomayor wrote that the order “merely postpones adjudication.” The timing is acute because North Carolina, Alabama and other states have already begun mailing November ballots, and challengers say the portal and envelope mandates are impossible to meet in time. Legal analysts at Lawfare note the delivery rule is designed to work alongside a parallel DHS/Social-Security data-matching component that would cross-reference the state lists. (Legal/elections story weighted to Tier 1 wire desks, SCOTUS reporting and Lawfare for the facts; as of Wednesday morning the justices had not ruled, and this USPS-rule fight is distinct from a separate argued case over counting late-arriving ballots.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads this as engineered “mail-ballot chaos” — an unprecedented federal grab over a domain the Constitution assigns to the states, timed so close to Election Day that the confusion itself suppresses turnout. Writers at Mother Jones and Slate lean on Talwani’s disenfranchisement finding and argue the 6–3 Court enabled the power grab by ducking the merits while the clock runs out. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The right is genuinely split. A libertarian strand (Reason’s Volokh Conspiracy, Ilya Somin) calls the order “an assault on federalism and separation of powers,” stressing that election administration belongs to states and Congress, not the president; a Washington Examiner op-ed argues any court “win” is a trap only Congress can fix through legislation like the SAVE Act. MAGA-aligned commentators counter that mail voting invites fraud and the Court was right to let the administration proceed. (Directional; intra-right federalism worry labeled.)

Sources: SCOTUSblog, The Hill, NPR, Lawfare, Reason (Volokh)
2026 Midterms · Party Politics

Republicans open a first-of-its-kind midterm convention in Dallas as Trump headlines a party fighting brutal poll numbers

+

Republicans open the first-ever midterm national convention on Wednesday, Sept. 9, at the American Airlines Center in Dallas — a two-night event (Sept. 9–10) built entirely around speeches, fundraising and rallying rather than any delegate business, nominations or votes. No party has staged a midterm convention in the modern era. President Trump delivers a keynote on Night 1 (after reworking the schedule to speak both nights) and closes the convention Thursday; Vice President JD Vance gives Thursday’s main address. The RNC lists more than 100 speakers and projects roughly 20,000 attendees per night (organizer Ray Washburne), under a “Common Sense Revolution” banner. Access is steep: ticket-plus-lodging packages have run about $25,000, and a donor invitation first reported by the Wall Street Journal sought upwards of $88,000 for a photo with the president at a private roundtable.

The venue is pointed. Texas hosts one of the cycle’s marquee Senate races — GOP AG Ken Paxton against Democratic Rep. James Talarico — and Dallas, once the heart of Bush-era “Republican country” (Reagan was nominated there in 1984), underscores how thoroughly the party has been remade. The gathering lands amid difficult signals: an August Reuters/Ipsos poll put Trump’s approval at 33%, described as the lowest of his second term, with 64% disapproving. The explicit aim in Dallas is to manufacture momentum and cash to offset midterm headwinds, with GOP messaging casting November as a contest against “communism.” (Softer framing story pegged to the convention’s opening; the specific races and the national environment will keep moving through the fall.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left frames the Dallas gathering as a personality-cult rally that ties every vulnerable Republican tighter to a president underwater at 33% — a liability, not a lift. The six-figure access prices and delegate-free format, they argue, mark it as a fundraising and loyalty spectacle rather than a governing agenda, staged in Bush’s Dallas to show how completely Trump has hollowed out the old GOP. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Mainstream-right voices (Fox, RNC-aligned) present the convention as a disciplined show of force to nationalize the midterms, energize the base and out-raise Democrats, casting the choice as capitalism versus “communism” and rallying behind candidates like Paxton. Skeptics on the right worry a hasty, Trump-centric spectacle risks amplifying his low approval and crowding out local candidates’ own brands. (Directional.)

Sources: PBS NewsHour, Texas Tribune, Axios, Fox News, NPR
National Security · Pentagon

Hegseth’s Pentagon deepens into crisis — a polygraph leak hunt, purged brass and a GOP senator’s call to fire him — six months into the Iran war

+

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is presiding over the deepest leadership crisis of Trump’s second term at the Pentagon, playing out as the U.S. is roughly six months into a war with Iran. Army Secretary Dan Driscoll — a close Vance ally whom Trump had tapped for sensitive Ukraine diplomacy — resigned around Sept. 1 after months of clashes, reportedly warning Trump directly that Hegseth’s purges were sowing chaos and harming modernization. His exit followed the firing of Army Chief of Staff Gen. Randy George (April) and the ouster of Gen. Christopher Donahue, the top Army commander in Europe (July), and leaves the Army without Senate-confirmed civilian or uniformed leadership mid-war. By multiple counts, Hegseth has removed, sidelined or blocked promotions for two dozen-plus senior officers — roughly half of all four-star generals. On Sept. 2, Sen. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.) publicly urged Trump to fire him, writing that Hegseth is “intimidated by competence” and that he had “never witnessed more inept management.”

The leak crackdown is the other flashpoint. Building on a Pentagon–DOJ joint leak task force announced in July to “identify and prosecute” leakers, investigators in August administered polygraphs to roughly 50 members of the Joint Staff, probing leaks to journalists about the Iran war and declining U.S. stockpiles of long-range missiles and Patriot air-defense interceptors; reporting suggests the sweep could ultimately touch thousands. On Sept. 8 the Washington Post ran an opinion package consolidating the criticism (“the chaos in Pete Hegseth’s Defense Department”), and a NATO/Europe troop review is due to hand Hegseth options by Nov. 6 amid allied fears of deep cuts to the roughly 80,000 U.S. forces in Europe. (Facts corroborated across the Washington Post, TIME, CBS News, Semafor and The Hill; opinion cited only for the takes below.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left casts Hegseth as a culture-warrior hollowing out an apolitical officer corps in the middle of a shooting war — dangerous precisely for that reason. The polygraph program reads as a “culture of fear” and loyalty hunt substituting for accountability, with fewer senior leaders and fewer critical weapons than at any prior point in the war. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

This is a live intra-right fight. Hegseth’s MAGA defenders praise him for cutting bloated bureaucracy, purging “woke” brass and treating wartime leaks as the genuine danger. But the center-right Dispatch and, strikingly, Tillis’s on-record call for his firing amount to an unusual Republican rebuke — intra-party dissent, labeled as such, not the MAGA mainstream, which still backs the secretary. (Directional; intra-right disagreement labeled.)

Middle East · US–Iran War

Brent tops $100 for the first time since July as the US–Iran tanker war escalates in the Strait of Hormuz

+

The seven-month U.S.–Iran maritime conflict sharply escalated over the last day. On Tuesday, Sept. 8, U.S. forces destroyed Iranian crude tankers in retaliation for an attempted Iranian attack on an American warship near the Strait of Hormuz; on Wednesday, Sept. 9, Iran’s Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) said it had struck two American vessels and eight oil tankers in the Gulf. Outlets differ on the U.S. strike count: CNBC reported American forces “destroyed five Iranian crude tankers Tuesday,” while earlier Al Jazeera and CNN reporting described three tankers hit, including a carrier near Kharg Island, Iran’s main export hub. Iran is signaling a new maritime “exclusion zone” extending from the U.S. blockade line into the Persian Gulf, to be lodged with the UN’s International Maritime Organization — even as Tehran says a separate Oman-brokered deal for a temporary “safe corridor” through Hormuz is “just days away.” Traffic through the strait has fallen to its lowest since May, roughly 10 ships per day (Kpler data cited by Reuters).

The market impact was the day’s headline: Brent crude crossed $100 a barrel for the first time since July, trading about 2.5% higher near $100.44 on Wednesday morning after ranging in the $96–$98 band earlier in the week; analysts warn of a path toward $120-plus if shipping attacks intensify. The simultaneous escalation (strikes) and de-escalation talk (the Oman corridor) suggests both sides are fighting for leverage over the terms of managed passage rather than a full closure — Tehran trying to convert military pressure into recognized control of a corridor while Washington keeps escorting traffic. The $100 print is the global story, transmitting the war into energy prices and inflation worldwide. (Corroborated across CNBC, CBS News, the Irish Times and Al Jazeera; exact tanker counts — three vs. five U.S. strikes, Iran’s “two vessels plus eight tankers” claim — remain contested between outlets and were still developing Wednesday.)

Sources: CNBC (oil), CNBC (strikes), Irish Times, CBS News, Al Jazeera
Europe · Russia–Ukraine War

Trump and Putin talk for an hour with no ceasefire, as a face-to-face Trump–Zelensky meeting is tentatively set for late September

+

Following the Witkoff–Kushner shuttle to Moscow and — for the first time — Kyiv over the weekend, President Trump held an hourlong phone call with Vladimir Putin on Tuesday, Sept. 8. Kremlin foreign-policy adviser Yuri Ushakov described it as “constructive and quite frank,” said Putin laid out his “vision for the peace process,” and — notably — that Putin assured Trump Russia “has no aggressive plans regarding Europe.” Ushakov said Trump pitched ending the war as a way to reopen large-scale U.S.–Russia trade, while also relaying that Putin intends to press Russia’s battlefield gains; spokesman Dmitry Peskov has said Moscow believes it is close to winning. Trump was then to speak with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky — the calls sequential, not a three-way.

Zelensky said the U.S. envoys brought “very good, worthwhile ideas,” but stressed “We expect the war will continue,” and is pressing for a “winter package” of additional Patriot interceptors and energy support, including U.S. LNG. Ukrainian foreign-ministry spokesman Georgiy Tykhyi said Sept. 8 that a face-to-face Trump–Zelensky meeting has been “tentatively agreed” for September, with Kyiv pointing to the second half of the month (around Sept. 20). No ceasefire emerged. One credibility gap stands out: Putin’s “no plans against Europe” assurance lands as European capitals press concrete sabotage accusations — Germany last week blamed Russia for an attempted August drone incident at a Leipzig-area airport. This remains process, not substance: warm atmospherics and trade inducements, no movement on actual terms, and continued fighting away from the symbolic capitals pauses. (Corroborated across AP via PBS, The Moscow Times, the Kyiv Independent and Al Jazeera; treat the meeting date as tentative and the readouts as divergent between Washington, Moscow and Kyiv.)

Europe · Germany Politics

‘Deeply shocked’ Merz vows to push his reforms through as the AfD’s Saxony-Anhalt landslide reopens coalition rifts

+

The far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD) won the Sept. 6 Saxony-Anhalt state election with roughly 43.8% of the vote (lead candidate Ulrich Siegmund), finishing well ahead of Chancellor Friedrich Merz’s CDU at about 17.2% — though the AfD fell about three seats short of the absolute majority it would have needed to govern alone and break the mainstream “firewall.” At a Sept. 8 press conference Merz said he was “deeply shocked” by the result but insisted his federal reform package — including raising the pension age and clamping down on sick pay — must proceed, conceding only that he needs to “better explain” the reforms to voters. His CDU again ruled out any cooperation with the AfD, reaffirming the firewall (Brandmauer).

But the defeat has reopened divisions inside Merz’s federal coalition. The SPD, his center-left partner, is now demanding talks on possible changes to the pension and care-reform proposals and reopening debates over the 2027 budget and inheritance/wealth tax — reporting framed the win as “clouding Merz’s reform agenda as an ally seeks changes.” The story is now less a fresh electoral shock than an unfolding governance fight: a single eastern-state result is being wielded in Berlin to force renegotiation of an already-fragile federal package. The question to watch is whether the coalition softens the pension and sick-pay measures — which would be read as the AfD indirectly shaping federal policy despite the firewall. (Figures are preliminary official results and may shift on final certification.)

Sources: Reuters / U.S. News, Reuters / Japan Times, TIME, Washington Times / AP, Al Jazeera

Technology · Semiconductors

Qualcomm signs Amazon as its first big cloud customer for AI chips, a direct challenge to Nvidia

+

Qualcomm on Sept. 8, 2026 announced a multi-year, multi-generation agreement with Amazon Web Services to co-develop custom AI-inference silicon and high-bandwidth optical interconnects for AWS data centers — the company’s first Western hyperscaler win and a direct move into territory Nvidia dominates. Amazon’s purchases and other commercial commitments could total as much as $60 billion in payments running through September 2036, though that figure is a ceiling on potential commitments, not booked revenue. As part of the deal, Qualcomm issued Amazon a warrant to acquire 25 million Qualcomm shares at $161.26 each (roughly $4 billion; some outlets cited a higher headline value), of which about 3.75 million shares have already vested against Amazon’s initial commitments. Qualcomm shares jumped about 10% on the news.

The partnership centers on inference workloads — running trained AI models rather than training them — and builds on Qualcomm’s recently unveiled data-center “Dragonfly” platform (a C1000 CPU, an AI300 accelerator and networking). The two also plan optical connectivity for Amazon’s data-center networks starting at up to 1.6 terabits per second, with faster future generations. Qualcomm said revenue would begin in its fiscal first quarter of 2027 (the December 2026 quarter), with chips already in production. (Caveat: the $60 billion is a decade-long potential total tied to warrant and commitment terms, not a firm order.)

Sources: CNBC, The Register, Quartz, TipRanks
Climate · Global Temperatures

August 2026 was the warmest on record, capping the hottest boreal summer as El Niño builds

+

The EU’s Copernicus Climate Change Service reported that August 2026 was the warmest August on record in its ERA5 dataset (which runs back to 1940), at about 1.65°C above the pre-industrial baseline — beating the previous August record, set in 2024, by a wide ~0.14°C margin. The June–August period was also the hottest such Northern Hemisphere summer measured. The heat reflects the long-term, human-caused warming trend layered on top of a rapidly strengthening El Niño; the underlying warming rate has climbed from roughly 0.18°C per decade in the early 2000s to around 0.27°C now. Ocean heat was extreme too: the daily global average sea-surface temperature hit a record 21.1°C on Aug. 22.

Copernicus now puts the odds that 2026 becomes the warmest calendar year on record at roughly 75%, with El Niño expected to persist; Berkeley Earth’s Zeke Hausfather independently highlighted the same 1.65°C figure. (Caveats: the 1.65°C is a single-month anomaly, not a sustained breach of the Paris 1.5°C long-term goal; the figures come from the ERA5 reanalysis and are preliminary until other datasets — NASA GISS, NOAA and Berkeley Earth — confirm them.)

Sources: Copernicus (C3S), WMO, Gizmodo
Technology · AI

Mistral raises €3 billion in a Samsung-led round — the biggest ever for a European tech company

+

The French AI developer Mistral AI announced on Sept. 8, 2026 that it had closed a €3 billion (about $3.5 billion) Series D led by Samsung Electronics, lifting its post-money valuation above €21 billion — described as the largest equity fundraising round ever completed by a European technology company. Co-leads included the EQT-managed Scaleup Europe Fund and existing investor PSG Equity; new investors included Advent, funds managed by BlackRock and the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg. Existing backers a16z, ASML, General Catalyst, Lightspeed, Nvidia and Salesforce Ventures also took part — notably chip-tool maker ASML, which had earlier taken a large strategic stake.

CEO Arthur Mensch said the capital would go toward building and owning data centers while also renting additional compute, and that Mistral is on track to surpass $1 billion in annual recurring revenue before year-end. The round is being read as evidence that “sovereign AI” — nationally or regionally controlled models and infrastructure — has become a serious commercial category, as European and Gulf governments grow wary of dependence on U.S. model providers. (Caveat: the ARR target is a company projection, and headline valuation and round figures vary slightly across outlets because of euro–dollar conversion.)

Health · Cardiovascular

Novo Nordisk halts two big heart-failure trials of its anti-inflammatory drug, another blow to its non-obesity pipeline

+

Novo Nordisk has stopped two late-stage cardiovascular trials of ziltivekimab, an experimental anti-inflammatory drug (an IL-6 inhibitor) that aimed to protect the heart by lowering inflammation rather than cholesterol. The company told trial investigators on Sept. 4, 2026 that it was ending the Phase 3 HERMES and ATHENA studies early after an independent data-monitoring committee judged them unlikely to succeed; the news was first reported Sept. 7 and covered widely (including by STAT) on Sept. 7–8. Both trials tested ziltivekimab against placebo in heart-failure patients on outcomes such as cardiovascular death and heart-failure hospitalizations. The decision follows the drug’s earlier stumble in the larger ZEUS trial, which missed its primary endpoint in July.

The setback matters because it is part of Novo Nordisk’s push to diversify beyond its blockbuster GLP-1 obesity and diabetes franchise (Ozempic and Wegovy), and it lands amid broader pressure on the company’s pipeline and share price. It came the same week as a second Phase 3 failure at Novartis — its del-desiran drug for the muscle disorder myotonic dystrophy type 1 (the HARBOR trial). (Caveat: the ziltivekimab trials were halted for futility — deemed unlikely to hit their endpoints — not for a safety signal, and final data have not been published.)

Tennis · US Open

An American surge at the US Open: Shelton stuns Alcaraz in a record-late epic to set up an all-American semifinal with Tiafoe

+

At Flushing Meadows the tournament is at the semifinal stage — the finals fall on the weekend of ~Sept. 12–13, so there is no champion yet. The dominant story of the last 24 hours is an American surge in the men’s draw. Ben Shelton beat world No. 1 Carlos Alcaraz 6-7(5), 6-1, 6-3, 1-6, 7-6(10-7) in a quarterfinal that finished at 3:33 a.m. — the latest-ending match in US Open history, 4 hours 28 minutes, and Shelton’s first career win over Alcaraz. Frances Tiafoe then rallied from two sets down to beat Alex Michelsen 5-7, 3-6, 7-5, 6-3, 7-6(10-6) on Tuesday. That sets up an all-American semifinal, Shelton vs. Tiafoe, on Friday — the first Open-era Grand Slam meeting at the semifinal stage or later between two Black American men — guaranteeing an American man in the final and a shot at ending the 23-year U.S. men’s Slam drought (Andy Roddick, 2003).

In the women’s draw, two-time defending champion and world No. 1 Aryna Sabalenka edged Linda Nosková 7-6(1), 3-6, 7-6(10-7) in the quarterfinals to stay alive for a three-peat, and will meet No. 3 Jessica Pegula (who beat Emma Navarro) in a semifinal on Thursday. Elena Rybakina, who ended Naomi Osaka’s run, and Zheng Qinwen, who earlier stunned Iga Świątek, are the other names into the closing rounds of the bottom half. (Scorelines corroborated across ESPN and CBS Sports; the exact Rybakina–Zheng semifinal pairing is treated as likely-but-not-fully-confirmed, so the anchor here is the confirmed Sabalenka–Pegula semifinal.)
