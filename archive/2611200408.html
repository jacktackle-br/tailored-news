Senate · Energy · Permitting

Senate passes the bipartisan permitting overhaul 62–33 in a late-night vote, sending it to an uncertain House

+

The Senate gave final approval on Thursday night to the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA), 62–33. The bill is meant to shorten federal permitting timelines for energy, transmission, infrastructure and housing projects. Nearly all Republicans voted yes, joined by about 15 members of the Democratic caucus, including independent Angus King. Republicans Josh Hawley and Mike Rounds voted no. Outlets differ slightly on the Democratic split: NPR counts 15 of 47 Democrats in favor, while Native News Online counts 14 for and 28 against. This settles the question left open after Monday’s procedural vote, which some outlets had reported as final passage.

The bill changes how reviews are carried out under the National Environmental Policy Act and other environmental laws, and it limits court challenges to permits. It also requires data centers to pay for their own grid upgrades. Industry groups, the American Petroleum Institute, the AFL-CIO and President Trump support it. The Sierra Club, the NRDC, the National Wildlife Federation, tribal leaders and a coalition of 17 Democratic-led states oppose it. The House passed a different bill, the SPEED Act, in December 2025, so it must now take up the Senate text. Majority Leader John Thune said he did not know whether the House would pass it as written, and a spokesperson for House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said it “falls short in several areas.”

White House · Shenzhen summit · Aftermath

Back from Shenzhen, Trump gives the summit a “12” out of 10 while the White House says little about what was agreed, ahead of a Friday news conference

+

President Trump returned to Washington after Thursday’s three-way meeting with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen. The White House has released little about what happened there. The Washington Post noted that Trump did not hold the closing news conference usually given at the end of a foreign trip, and that the White House statement confirmed only that the meeting took place. On the South Lawn, Trump called the meeting historic, told reporters that “on a scale of zero to 10, I think it was a 12,” and said he would hold a news conference at midday Friday.

Trump told Fox News that he kept talking with Putin after reporters left the trilateral session, and that Putin wants a Ukraine deal. The Kremlin’s account was flatter. Aide Yuri Ushakov said the two leaders discussed Ukraine for about 40 minutes but that “neither side made any particularly important statements,” and Putin said Russia was “not yet agreeing to compromises.” CNN’s takeaways found no diplomatic breakthrough beyond the four-point declaration the leaders signed.

A Philadelphia Inquirer column accused Trump of trading away U.S. security for “a photo op and business deals” with two autocrats, and the White House’s silence has fed complaints that the public still does not know what was said.

Courts · First Amendment · USDA

Judge bars Agriculture Secretary Rollins from sending faith-laden emails to USDA staff, finding they likely violated the Establishment Clause

+

U.S. District Judge Trina Thompson in San Francisco ruled on Thursday that Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins’ religious messages to the department’s roughly 100,000 employees, including a December 2025 Christmas email and an April 2026 Easter email, likely violated the First Amendment’s Establishment Clause. She issued a preliminary injunction. She pointed to Rollins’ authority over the workforce, her use of official email, and wording that presented Christianity as the shared faith of USDA staff, and wrote that “the buck stops with Secretary Rollins.” The AP describes the emails as unconstitutional, while The Hill says the judge found they likely were. The difference reflects the preliminary stage of the case.

Under the order, Rollins must stop religious messaging to a colleague who asks her to, and may not use her official authority to pressure subordinates. She may still talk about her faith with colleagues who are interested and invite co-workers to religious events. The judge said she expressed no view on Rollins’ conduct in her personal capacity. The employees were represented by Americans United for Separation of Church and State and Democracy Forward. A USDA spokesperson called the ruling “a completely unprecedented attempt to muzzle a Cabinet Secretary” and said the department is considering an appeal.

The Left-Leaning Take

Search turned up no in-window piece on the ruling from Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion, so none is summarized here. The plaintiffs’ side and their allies welcomed it. The federal employees’ union said workers are “public servants, not an audience for their bosses’ personal religious beliefs,” and Raw Story framed the decision as a reckoning for an official using a government inbox to preach.

The Right-Leaning Take

Search turned up no in-window piece on the ruling from National Review, The Dispatch, the WSJ editorial page or Reason, so none is summarized here. The administration’s position, stated by the USDA, is that the ruling muzzles a Cabinet officer’s speech. That fits a long-running conservative argument that courts treat public expressions of faith by officials with too much suspicion.

Ukraine · Berlin · Security guarantees

In Berlin, Kushner says Russia is “comfortable” with Article 5-style guarantees for Ukraine, but Zelenskyy says no common understanding emerged

+

U.S. envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff met Volodymyr Zelenskyy and European leaders in Berlin on Thursday, the same day as the Shenzhen summit, at a Coalition of the Willing meeting hosted by Chancellor Friedrich Merz. Kushner told reporters: “I think we’ve gotten the Russians comfortable with that concept where any security guarantee for Ukraine would be based on Article 5.” That would be a mutual-defense commitment without NATO membership. He also said Trump would not impose a peace deal and wants Europe and Ukraine involved. Kyiv has pushed for a multilateral round of talks that includes Europe, after the U.S.-brokered talks in Abu Dhabi ended without a ceasefire.

The claim is not yet confirmed by Moscow. Zelenskyy said there was no common understanding in Berlin of what Article 5-style guarantees would involve, Moscow had not responded to Kushner’s remarks, and Al Jazeera notes that Russia has never publicly accepted the guarantees that Kyiv’s allies have in mind. The leaders backed a European-led multinational force, supported by the U.S., after a ceasefire. Merz said he is open to talks with Russia on neutral ground. Territory remains the main unresolved issue. Meanwhile Ukraine’s strikes on Russia go on: it hit the Crimean Bridge for the third time in a week on Thursday.

Saudi Arabia · Houthis · Gulf war

Houthi strike damages Riyadh’s tallest tower, the headquarters of Saudi Arabia’s sovereign wealth fund

+

A Houthi missile attack on Thursday damaged the Public Investment Fund Tower in Riyadh’s King Abdullah Financial District. The tower is the city’s tallest building and the headquarters of the kingdom’s roughly trillion-dollar wealth fund. Reuters reported that the blast blew out windows across seven or eight floors and bent parts of the metal facade. Saudi civil defense said several floors and nearby buildings were hit, no one was injured, and authorities called it a “terrorist” attack. Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree said the group had fired cruise missiles at King Khalid International Airport and the financial district.

It is the second strike on the financial district this month, after a November 8 attack hit an Aramco building there. It comes a day after the Saudi-led coalition said it shot down Houthi cruise missiles fired toward the capital. The strike shows that the Iran-aligned group can still reach symbolic economic targets in the Saudi capital as the wider Iran war continues around the Strait of Hormuz.

Science, Tech, Energy & Climate
Climate · COP31 · Antalya

COP31’s second draft drops every mention of fossil fuels but keeps a 35%-by-2035 electrification goal, as talks reach their scheduled final day

+

Co-hosts Turkey and Australia released a revised nine-page “cover decision” at the UN climate talks in Antalya on Thursday that contains no reference to fossil fuels. Monday’s first draft had called for “the immediate transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems”; the new text speaks only of a “transition towards low greenhouse gas emissions and climate-resilient development.” It also drops a quantified adaptation-finance goal that developing countries want. It keeps a proposal to raise electricity’s share of global final energy use to 35% by 2035, which Saudi Arabia and other Arab states are reportedly trying to remove. As CNN notes, the target does not specify that the electricity must be renewable.

Fiji said the conference will have failed without fossil-fuel transition language. Panama said it would formally object in the closing plenary if its demands are not met, and Australia’s Chris Bowen defended cutting the adaptation-finance wording. Friday is the scheduled final day, but no closing time had been confirmed and the talks may run over. No final text had been adopted as of this writing.

Tech · Chips · Export controls

House panel releases records showing the U.S. licensed nearly 880,000 Nvidia and AMD AI chips for Chinese buyers in six months

+

House Foreign Affairs Committee Chairman Brian Mast on Thursday released Commerce Department export-license records. They show that the Bureau of Industry and Security approved 879,868 advanced AI chips, Nvidia H200s and AMD MI325Xs, for Chinese buyers between December 2025 and May 2026. Chinese buyers had requested about 3.4 million. The approvals came through 21 license applications and included about 150,000 chips for Tencent, 75,000 for Alibaba, 43,000 for Baidu and 4,000 for BYD. Mast used a provision of the Export Control Reform Act that allows disclosure when withholding it would be contrary to the national interest.

Some outlets report that more than 30% of the approved chips went to companies the Pentagon lists as tied to China’s military. The records do not show how many chips were actually shipped. Ranking Democrat Gregory Meeks said BIS appeared to approve advanced chips for China “with little scrutiny” even as other license reviews were badly delayed. Reuters reports that this week’s run of U.S. measures on China’s chip access, including a new rule on remote access to advanced chips, pushed Chinese tech stocks lower.

Science · Ageing · Memory

Later-born generations reach old age with better memory and decline more slowly, a 219,000-person analysis finds

+

A study in Nature Medicine, covered by Nature’s news desk on Thursday, pooled about 783,000 episodic-memory tests from roughly 219,000 adults aged 50 and over. The participants were born between 1910 and 1964 and came from five long-running studies in Western countries. Being born ten years later was associated with a memory score about 10% higher at age 60. The authors describe a “double dividend” for later generations: they enter old age with better memory and then decline more slowly.

The authors suggest the gains could explain much of the fall in dementia incidence, about 13% per decade, reported in Europe and the U.S. The study is observational, however. It does not identify which changes in education, health, nutrition or environment are responsible, and it compares birth cohorts rather than actual parents and children.

Arsenal

Zubimendi out with a hamstring injury and Saka a month away, as Arsenal Women are beaten 2–0 by Lyon at a foggy Meadow Park

+

Arseblog News reports from Mikel Arteta’s pre-Newcastle press conference that Martin Zubimendi will miss Saturday’s trip to St James’ Park. Arsenal drop to eighth, three points behind fourth-placed Chelsea, with one league-phase round left.
