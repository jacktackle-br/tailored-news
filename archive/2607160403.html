Elections · Primetime Address

Trump plans a primetime White House address reviving 2020 fraud claims and asserting voting-machine “vulnerabilities” ahead of the midterms

+

President Trump is scheduled to deliver a primetime address from the White House on Thursday, July 16, at 9 p.m. Eastern (6 p.m. Pacific), billed as focused on “election integrity” and what the administration calls vulnerabilities in voting machines. Officials say Trump will cite newly declassified intelligence purporting to show irregularities in the 2020 contest and alleged foreign-interference risks. The declassification push is being driven by acting DNI Bill Pulte and John Solomon, a former journalist who has amplified fraud claims; CIA Director John Ratcliffe, FBI Director Kash Patel and DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin are expected to appear alongside the president. The speech dovetails with a Republican effort to attach the SAVE (Safeguard American Voter Eligibility) Act to State Department funding. As of this brief the address had not yet been delivered — treat it as scheduled, not given.

The premise conflicts with the established record. Trump lost 2020 by more than seven million votes; numerous courts, Republican-run audits and his own first-term DOJ found no significant fraud or voting-machine rigging, and CISA called the 2020 vote “the most secure in American history.” A 2021 Justice/Homeland Security review found “no evidence that any foreign government-affiliated actor prevented voting, changed votes, or disrupted the ability to tally votes.” Reporting also notes the litigation backdrop: Fox News paid $787.5 million to settle Dominion’s defamation suit, and Newsmax and OANN also settled voting-machine claims.

The Left-Leaning Take

In-window progressive framing (Democracy Docket, the MS NOW/Maddow blog) casts the address as election denialism operationalized from the White House — an attempt to pre-emptively delegitimize the 2026 midterms and manufacture a pretext for federal intervention in how states run their elections, which legal experts quoted in wire coverage call unconstitutional. Slate ties the voting-machine narrative back to the Trump-allied operatives who actually breached election systems in Georgia, Michigan and Pennsylvania after 2020, arguing the real security threat came from Trump’s own side. (The Guardian, Vox and Mother Jones opinion were not retrievable this session — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

No full-throated in-window mainstream-right editorial defense of the speech’s factual premise surfaced (provisional). Mainstream conservative outlets have stayed wary of machine-rigging claims given the Dominion/Smartmatic exposure; the affirmative right case is pitched at the adjacent policy — the SAVE Act’s proof-of-citizenship requirement — rather than at machine fraud. Intra-right disagreement: The Dispatch’s in-window posture (podcast: “Will Trump Interfere with the 2026 Midterms?”) reflects Trump-skeptical conservative anxiety, and the anti-Trump Bulwark treats the address as authoritarian theater — positions well to the left of the elected GOP. (WSJ editorial and NR were not retrievable in-window.)

Immigration · ICE Use of Force

Trump overrules his own DHS and reinstates ICE vehicle stops a day after the agency paused them following two fatal shootings of bystanders

+

On Wednesday, July 15, President Trump overturned a Department of Homeland Security order pausing most ICE vehicle stops — roughly 24 hours after agents had been told of it — posting that traffic stops are one of ICE’s “most important and effective Crime Fighting tools” and that halting them would be “playing right into the criminal’s hands.” The pause had been ordered by DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin after federal agents fatally shot two men who were not the targets of enforcement operations: Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, a 52-year-old Mexican national who had lived in the U.S. about 35 years, in Houston on July 7, and Joan Sebastian Durán Guerrero, a 26-year-old Colombian man, in Biddeford, Maine, on Monday, July 13. In both shootings the agents were not wearing body cameras, despite Congress having appropriated roughly $20 million for that purpose; Mullin’s order had also required at least one body-camera-equipped officer per arrest team.

Reporting (Washington Post, NBC, Time, CNN) indicates senior ICE leaders were surprised by the reversal, and that internal arrest-quota pressure has been blamed for the shootings. The Post framed the sequence as Trump’s “deportation push falters after fatal ICE encounters,” emphasizing operational disarray; the administration cast the reversal as protecting an essential tool. Democratic congressional leaders responded with renewed calls to overhaul or abolish ICE. Outlets agree on the core facts — two shootings, the pause, the reversal, the missing body cameras.

The Left-Leaning Take

Progressive and civil-liberties framing centers on impunity and racialized enforcement: critics quoted across coverage argue ICE is “stalking Latinos,” and that an internal pause and another body-camera pledge are “entirely insufficient” without statutory limits — a point sharpened by Trump reversing even that modest pause within a day. Democratic lawmakers are pushing to overhaul or abolish the agency. (Guardian, Vox and Mother Jones opinion were inaccessible this session — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream-right and administration position, as reported, is that traffic stops are indispensable to interior enforcement and that halting them rewards criminals; National Review covered the original pause straight as news without endorsing a pullback. A libertarian angle is distinct and critical: Reason (“ICE keeps shooting people. Here’s a way Congress can rein it in,” July 15) argues the real fix is legislative — reviving a Bivens-style cause of action and ending qualified immunity so agents who use excessive force can be sued. Intra-right disagreement: the anti-Trump Bulwark ran the bluntest in-window pieces, arguing the administration is indifferent to civilian deaths.

Sources: Washington Post, Time, CNN, NBC News, National Review, Reason, The Bulwark
DOJ · Attorney General Confirmation

Blanche’s attorney-general nomination is in doubt as Republicans Cornyn and Tillis balk over a $1.8B “anti-weaponization” fund and a one-vote committee margin

+

A day after acting Attorney General Todd Blanche — Trump’s former personal defense lawyer, nominated to replace fired AG Pam Bondi — testified before the Senate Judiciary Committee, his path to confirmation looks genuinely uncertain. In Tuesday’s hearing Blanche acknowledged “mistakes that were made” in the release of the Jeffrey Epstein files and apologized to survivors whose personal information was left unredacted, and repeatedly said the roughly $1.78 billion DOJ “anti-weaponization” fund — created by settling Trump’s suit against the IRS over his leaked tax returns, to pay people who claim they were wrongfully investigated — is “dead” and “not moving forward,” though the settlement language reportedly has not yet been amended in court. Sen. John Cornyn (R-TX) said Wednesday he is undecided, citing Blanche’s inability to call the fund statutorily unenforceable, and Sen. Thom Tillis (R-NC) says the fund must be fully scrapped for his support.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. After Sen. Lindsey Graham (R-S.C.) died July 11–12 of an aortic dissection at 71 — Gov. Henry McMaster appointed Graham’s sister, Darline Graham Nordone, to the seat on Trump’s recommendation — Judiciary now sits 11 Republicans to 10 Democrats, so a single GOP defection would stall the nomination in committee. The same day Blanche testified, DNI nominee Jay Clayton appeared in a separate hearing and, like other Trump nominees, declined to say plainly that Biden won in 2020 — a thread that links directly to tonight’s scheduled address above.

The Iran War · Escalation

The U.S. presses a fifth straight day of strikes on Iran, reaching near Tehran and disabling a blockade-running tanker, as Iran claims retaliatory attacks on bases in Kuwait, Jordan and Bahrain

+

U.S. Central Command carried out a fresh wave of strikes on Wednesday, July 15 — a fifth consecutive day — which the Pentagon framed as degrading Iran’s ability to threaten shipping in the Strait of Hormuz. Strikes concentrated in Iran’s south but also reached deep into the country, hitting areas near Tehran for the first time in this round, targeting command centers, air-defense sites, missile and drone capabilities and coastal surveillance. With the U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports back in effect since Tuesday, CENTCOM said a U.S. aircraft disabled an empty, Curaçao-flagged tanker, the M/T Belma, with Hellfire missiles fired into its smokestack as it headed toward Kharg Island in defiance of blockade warnings — the first vessel disabled since the blockade resumed. Iran’s military called the strait an “unbreakable red line” and threatened a “superior” response after Trump warned the U.S. could hit Iranian bridges and power plants next week absent a return to talks. On Thursday, July 16, Iran’s army claimed retaliatory strikes on U.S. assets in the Gulf — the Ali al-Salem airbase in Kuwait, the al-Azraq airbase in Jordan, and facilities in Juffair, Bahrain; Jordan’s military earlier said it intercepted four missiles fired from Iranian territory.

Casualty and battle-damage figures are contested and largely unverified: Iran’s Health Ministry says at least 35 people were killed and more than 300 wounded in the latest wave — figures Iranian officials describe as civilian and that are not independently confirmed — while Iran’s claims of destroyed U.S. systems (including HIMARS launchers) are uncorroborated by Washington. The diplomacy is muddled: Tehran says the memorandum underpinning the earlier ceasefire is “voided” and that it has no plans to negotiate, while Vice President JD Vance said the U.S. and Iran held technical talks in Doha on a 14-point framework and would not resume military action “unless necessary” — hard to square with five days of active strikes. Separately, Trump announced July 15 that Iran had released a wrongfully detained American, dual citizen Dena Karari, held since December 2024, calling it a “gesture of goodwill”; Vance ruled out sending U.S. ground troops to topple Iran’s government.

Sources: CNN, NPR, Al Jazeera, CNBC, Axios, NBC News
Europe · Ukraine

Ukraine’s parliament confirms Naftogaz chief Serhii Koretskyi as prime minister amid a wartime reshuffle, as protesters rally over the defense minister’s ouster and Russian missiles again hit Kyiv

+

On Thursday, July 16, Ukraine’s Verkhovna Rada approved Serhii Koretskyi — head of the state energy firm Naftogaz — as prime minister, reported with 289 votes in favor. He replaces Yulia Svyrydenko, who resigned as part of a broad reshuffle President Volodymyr Zelensky announced July 12, the fourth major government overhaul since the 2022 full-scale invasion. Zelensky cited Koretskyi’s energy-sector record and the need to prepare Ukraine for another wartime winter. The move is contentious: hundreds of protesters gathered outside parliament over Zelensky’s decision to oust Defense Minister Mykhailo Fedorov after roughly six months, with critics warning it could disrupt military reforms mid-war; Interior Minister Ihor Klymenko has been floated as the next defense chief. Overnight, Russia again struck Kyiv with ballistic missiles as part of a wider barrage — recent nightly totals cited around 146 drones and 13 missiles — while Ukraine continued hitting Russian tankers, oil depots and bridges.

On the European support side, the EU and Ukraine signed a drone-cooperation agreement announced by Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, tied to a broader defense-industrial partnership and a reported €10 billion plan for drones, missiles and aircraft. Outlets vary on the precise choreography of the reshuffle — who ultimately fills defense — and on whether the story is primarily foreign-policy realignment or domestic political turbulence; the 289-vote figure comes from wire copy and should be treated as the reported count.

Asia-Pacific · China

Fallout from China’s rare Pacific submarine-missile test widens as Taiwanese intelligence disputes Beijing’s account, calling it a surface launch

+

The strategic aftershocks of China’s July 6 launch of a submarine-launched ballistic missile (SLBM) into the open Pacific continued through the July 15 news cycle. The missile — assessed by analysts as likely a JL-3 with a range over 10,000 km, carrying a dummy warhead — flew south from near the Guangdong coast, past Guam and near several Pacific island states, drawing formal protests from the United States, Japan, Australia, New Zealand, the Philippines and the Solomon Islands, whose prime minister said it was “not the behavior of a friend.” Beijing called it routine annual training “not aimed at any specific country.” In fresh July 15 reporting, a senior Taiwanese defense-intelligence official disputed that framing, saying the missile was not fired from a submerged submarine but launched from the surface near the coast — a technical distinction that matters for judging the maturity of China’s undersea nuclear deterrent.

Analysts and Defense News framed the test, together with China’s new coast-guard patrols east of Taiwan (which Beijing calls “routine law-enforcement”), as part of a deliberate, longer-term effort to hold off regional rivals; Taiwan’s Mainland Affairs Council called the patrols a violation of international law that damages regional stability. The window also coincided with the tenth anniversary (July 12) of the Permanent Court of Arbitration ruling against China’s South China Sea “nine-dash line” claims. Note the “surface-launched” characterization is a single-source Taiwanese-intelligence assessment, not independently confirmed.

Sources: Defense News, Washington Times, CNN, NPR, The Diplomat, Foreign Policy

Tech · Semiconductors

A Shanghai startup unveils an HBM-free 14nm AI chip, claiming higher memory bandwidth than Nvidia’s H200 while sidestepping U.S. export curbs

+

Chinese startup Dongfang Suanxin (founded 2024, based in Shanghai’s Zhangjiang hi-tech zone) exited stealth and launched its flagship DF1000 processor at an event on Monday, July 13, ahead of the World Artificial Intelligence Conference in Shanghai. The chip is built on a mature 14nm process, yet the company claims 520 teraflops of BF16 performance and 6.4 TB/s of memory bandwidth — roughly a third higher than Nvidia’s H200 — achieved without high-bandwidth memory (HBM) or advanced nodes, via a “software-defined computing” framework plus 3D-stacked near-memory computing. The company says the DF1000 is ready for mass production, with shipments by the end of 2026.

The roadmap is aggressive: a DF2000 in Q4 2026 (claimed to surpass the H200) and a DF3000 in late 2027 aimed at Nvidia’s B300. Dongfang Suanxin closed a Series A+ in late April at a roughly 12.3 billion yuan (~$1.7 billion) post-money valuation, backed by the National AI Industry Investment Fund and the venture arms of Xiaomi and JD.com, and is led by Tsinghua semiconductor veteran Wei Shaojun. One caveat worth keeping front of mind: every performance figure here is vendor-stated, not independently benchmarked.

Sources: TrendForce, South China Morning Post, Interesting Engineering, Crypto Briefing
Energy · Electrification

The EU prepares a first-ever 2040 electrification target aimed at halving oil use and cutting gas demand by two-thirds

+

Per a draft that leaked and was widely reported this week, the European Commission is set to publish an “electrification action plan” on July 17 that sets a (still-to-be-quantified) 2040 target for electricity’s share of final energy consumption, to be written into law via the post-2030 energy framework due in Q4 2026. The Commission estimates faster electrification could replace roughly two-thirds of EU gas demand and halve oil consumption by 2040, cutting the bloc’s fossil-fuel import bill by about €200 billion. The plan targets the three biggest fossil consumers — industry, transport and buildings — and is framed partly as an energy-security response to Middle East supply shocks. Timing caveat: the formal unveiling is Friday, July 17, so as of this brief it is an imminent, leaked policy rather than adopted text.

The move lands alongside hard news from Beijing: China’s State Council released its carbon-peaking action plan for the 15th Five-Year Plan (2026–2030) on July 9, targeting a 17% cut in carbon intensity versus 2025, non-fossil energy at 25% of consumption, and new-energy vehicles at roughly 30% of the on-road fleet by 2030. Together the two set the near-term electrification agenda for the world’s two largest trading blocs.

Space · Astronomy

Hubble reveals four white dwarfs hiding in plain sight within 65 light-years, concealed by brighter red-dwarf companions

+

A team from the University of Warwick and the University of Colorado Boulder (LASP) directly detected four white dwarfs in nearby binary systems for the first time, all within 65 light-years — one of them the ninth-closest white dwarf to the Sun. The systems (G 203-47, GJ 207.1, LHS 1817 and Wolf 1130) had each appeared to be single stars because a larger, brighter red dwarf outshone the compact stellar remnant. Using Hubble’s ultraviolet spectrograph plus custom calibration to separate the white-dwarf UV signal from red-dwarf flaring, the team confirmed all four; one system, G 203-47 at just 25 light-years, took 27 years after its initial radial-velocity wobble to pin down.

The work was published and circulated July 13–14, 2026. White dwarfs so close to Earth are prized natural laboratories — for studying stellar aging and for calibrating the cooling-age clocks astronomers use to date stellar populations across the galaxy.

Sources: ScienceDaily, Phys.org, Space.com, LASP (CU Boulder)
Science · Zoology

Researchers formally describe a new monkey with orange lips and a froglike roar from the DR Congo — only the fifth new African monkey in 75 years

+

Scientists confirmed a new colobus monkey, Colobus congoensis (known locally as “likweli”), in the region of the Democratic Republic of Congo’s Lomami National Park, in findings published July 15, 2026. The roughly 15-pound (7 kg) monkey has conspicuous orange lips, slate-grey cheekbones framing black-rimmed eyes, silky black fur, and distinctive roaring and snorting vocalizations. The trail began with a 2008 expedition photo of an unidentified monkey, a second sighting in 2018, and then a dedicated search to describe it.

It is described as only the fifth new African monkey species named in the past 75 years. Given its restricted range, small population, and pressure from hunting and habitat loss, the authors recommend an IUCN Red List status of Endangered.

Sources: Phys.org, Scientific American, Live Science, Discover Wildlife

World Cup 2026 · Final Set

Messi-inspired Argentina stun England with two late goals to set up a first-ever World Cup final against Spain at MetLife Stadium on Sunday

+

Defending champions Argentina came from behind to beat England 2–1 in the semifinal on Wednesday, July 15, reaching the final of the first 48-team World Cup. England looked set to reach their first final since 1966 after Anthony Gordon fired them ahead around the 55th minute, but Lionel Messi turned the game late: he set up Enzo Fernández for an 85th-minute equalizer, then crossed for substitute Lautaro Martínez to head the winner in the second minute of stoppage time. The result sends England out and books Argentina’s place in the title match.

The final is set for Sunday, July 19, kicking off at 3 p.m. ET (noon PT) at MetLife Stadium in East Rutherford, New Jersey. It pits Argentina against Spain, who beat France 2–0 in the other semifinal on July 14 — the first World Cup final ever between the two nations. Argentina bid to become the first back-to-back winner since Brazil in 1958 and 1962; Spain seek a second world title. A notable subplot: 18-year-old Lamine Yamal faces Messi on the biggest stage for the first time.
