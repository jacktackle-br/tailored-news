Justice Department · Alaska

Trump says “I’m the one that said ‘do it’” after the Justice Department sued to block certification of Alaska’s Senate result if Sen. Dan Sullivan loses

+

The Justice Department sued Alaska election officials in federal court on Thursday. The complaint, signed by Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward, asks a judge to bar certification of “any result that may be impacted by voter confusion … pending an investigation to determine whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan were meant to be cast for Senator Sullivan.” The suit concerns a second candidate, Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., who appears on the ballot alongside the Republican incumbent. Alaska’s elections office had tried to remove him over the summer, finding his candidacy was not in good faith, but the state courts reversed that, and the Alaska Supreme Court ordered him onto the ballot in June. A DOJ spokesperson said the candidacy was meant to mislead voters. Sullivan faces Democrat Mary Peltola on Nov. 3.

Asked about the suit on Friday, Trump said, “Well, I’m the one that said ‘do it,’” and called the other Sullivan’s candidacy “a disgrace.” Peltola called the suit a “very desperate attempt to cling to power.” Sen. Lisa Murkowski, a Republican who has endorsed Sullivan, wrote that the department “should never have filed this lawsuit,” and reports say Sullivan’s campaign did not ask for it. Legal scholars were skeptical. Notre Dame’s Derek Muller called the theory “strange and unusual” and said “the statute simply doesn’t let the Department of Justice enjoin certification.” Florida State’s Michael Morley called the suit meritless, though he said the concern about voter confusion is valid. Muller expects the case to matter only if Sullivan loses narrowly.

The Left-Leaning Take

Critics on the left see the federal government preparing, before any votes are counted, to overturn a result it doesn’t like, with the president saying openly that he ordered it. They argue that ballot disputes belong to the states, which have already settled this one, and that the suit fits a pattern of using the Justice Department for electoral ends this fall. The Right-Leaning Take

Supporters of the suit say a same-name candidate is an obvious attempt to siphon votes from the incumbent and that protecting voters from confusion is legitimate. Some conservative legal voices accept that the problem is real but doubt that federal court is the remedy. Murkowski’s rebuke shows the move is contested inside the GOP. Trade · Campaign 2026

The White House drops the extra 50% tariff on nine kinds of Canadian goods Maine growers rely on, as Sen. Susan Collins announces it, and adds new items to the list within the hour

+

On Friday the administration exempted nine categories of packaging and machinery from the 50% duties it put on about $20 billion of Canadian imports after trade talks collapsed in August. The list includes corrugated cartons and boxes, plastic crates, refrigeration and freezing equipment, and seaweed products, all used by Maine’s blueberry, potato and apple growers and seaweed harvesters. The changes take effect Oct. 30, four days before the election. The White House called them adjustments that “further refine” the tariffs’ scope. It also added items, including certain valves, carpets and waste products, citing Canada’s “continued discrimination” and retaliation. CNBC reported that the additions were announced less than an hour after Collins publicized the exemptions. Trump said Canada is working on a deal.

The Washington Post and others described the move as helping Republicans in states closely tied to Canada’s economy, especially Maine and Michigan, which the GOP likely needs to hold the Senate. Collins said she had repeatedly asked trade officials for relief and would keep pressing for a broader agreement. Her Democratic challenger, Troy Jackson, said she deserves blame rather than credit for “bringing a cup of water to put out the fire.”

The Left-Leaning Take

Democrats see election-season favors: relief for a targeted list of goods in a swing state, timed to a vulnerable incumbent’s press release, while the broader tariffs that raised costs stay in place and new ones are added. (No in-window Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion piece found; summary drawn from the Jackson campaign and Democratic reaction as reported.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Collins and the White House say the tariffs are leverage over a trading partner that keeps retaliating, and that adjusting them to protect American farmers and manufacturers while talks continue is responsive governance. Free-market conservatives would point out that every carve-out concedes the tariffs hurt the people they were meant to protect. (No in-window National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason piece found; summary reflects White House and Collins statements as reported.)

Iran · War

“We may do another little Epic Fury, or they’ll agree,” Trump tells a Florida rally, after a U.S. envoy says a strike could “absolutely” come this weekend

+

Speaking in Fort Myers on Friday at a rally for Byron Donalds, the Republican candidate for Florida governor, Trump said the U.S. may carry out another military operation against Iran. He linked it to whether Tehran reaches a deal but did not say what terms it would have to meet. Reuters noted the war is nearing its eight-month mark as fuel prices weigh on the midterms. Earlier, Tom Barrack, the U.S. ambassador to Turkey, was asked on a podcast whether a strike could come this weekend and answered “Absolutely.” He said regime change is not the goal but “there will be consequences” if Iran keeps threatening the Gulf. Barrack has no direct role on Iran, and Trump said he knew nothing about the remark. Several outlets noted that it conflicted with a White House statement on Thursday that attacks would not resume before Nov. 3.

The rhetoric is rising on all sides. Prime Minister Netanyahu said Iran’s government must be overthrown “once and for all.” Iran’s armed forces chief of staff, Ali Abdollahi, said Tehran could bar U.S. warships from the Gulf, and the Revolutionary Guard warned that the fighting would extend to U.S. bases if American escorts through Hormuz continue. Brent reached a five-month high on Friday, and U.S. crude topped $100 a barrel.

Ukraine · Satellites

Trump says Musk has “put it back,” but Ukrainian commanders say Starshield military satellite service is still being throttled

+

Trump said Friday that Elon Musk had restored Ukraine’s access to Starshield, the military version of Starlink. The Wall Street Journal had reported that he called Musk on Oct. 21 to ask for the restrictions to be eased. Ukrainian officials say that hasn’t happened. Andriy Klymenko, commander of the 414th Unmanned Systems Brigade, told the Washington Post that the service was still running “with constant interruptions,” though faster than on Thursday. Ukraine’s chargé d’affaires in Washington, Denys Sienik, said speed limits remained in place, and a Zelensky adviser, Serhii Beskrestnov, said there was no sign they had been lifted.

No one has publicly taken responsibility for the throttling. Musk wrote that any speed or usage limits are set by the U.S. military and “ultimately POTUS.” Trump denies ordering them, and Secretary of State Rubio called Starlink a private company. Fortune counted conflicting messages from Rubio, Hegseth and Vance. Ukraine’s drone units, which depend on the network, are the ones affected while it stays unresolved.

Russia · Energy

The first cargo of Russian diesel under the Trump–Putin deal is on its way to New York, Reuters reports, the first Russian fuel import since the 2022 ban

+

According to LSEG shipping data cited in a Reuters exclusive, the Malta-flagged tanker Agioi Fanendes loaded 40,000 metric tons of ultra-low-sulphur diesel at the Baltic port of Primorsk on Oct. 22 and is heading for New York. Reuters expects it to arrive around Nov. 7, and NBC says around Nov. 10, either way after the election. It is the first U.S. import of Russian petroleum products since the 2022 ban. Trump announced the purchase deal on Oct. 9 as a way to lower fuel prices before Nov. 3. Reuters said the buyer and seller were not immediately known.

The cargo is small next to U.S. diesel demand, but the political symbolism is large. Kyiv, European allies and senators from both parties have criticized the deal for paying into Russia’s war budget, and some have called it a “betrayal.” Russia has partly eased its own diesel export restrictions to make the shipments possible.

Sources: Reuters via U.S. News, Oct 23, NBC News, The National, Oct 23, Kyiv Independent

Climate · Health

Heat killed an estimated 50,000 people in Europe’s cities this summer, a record, and two-thirds of the deaths are attributed to climate change

+

A rapid analysis of 854 cities in 30 European countries, home to about 173 million people, estimates 50,391 heat-related deaths in summer 2026. That is 71% more than the previous worst summer in the series, 29,480 in 2022. The researchers modeled a world without warming and compared it with this summer’s deaths, and on that basis attribute 32,619 of the deaths to climate change. About a third of the deaths fell in a single two-week stretch, June 17 to July 1. The work is due to appear in Nature Health.

The authors call the figure an underestimate. It leaves out a deadly heat wave in May and everything outside the 854 cities, and because death certificates rarely list heat as a cause, it relies on statistical modeling. Last year the same group’s method gave about 24,400 deaths, so this summer roughly doubled it.

Sources: RTÉ, Oct 23, CBC, NBC News, Phys.org
Tech · AI

OpenAI lowers its fundraising target to $1.25 trillion after investor pushback, in a week when a supply warning from Lam Research sent chip stocks to their worst day since June

+

OpenAI is seeking up to $30 billion at a $1.25 trillion pre-money valuation, down from the $1.4 trillion it first sought. The round is reportedly led by Coatue and UAE investors. Reports point to “capital fatigue” among institutions that bought into earlier rounds, including March’s $122 billion raise at an $852 billion valuation. Investors have until Oct. 30 to commit, the round could fall short, and the company’s IPO is now expected in 2027.

The cut comes during a broad pullback from AI stocks. On Thursday the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 6.7%, with all 30 members lower, after Lam Research said near-term supply constraints had trimmed its guidance. CEO Tim Archer said clean-room space and component shortages could limit chipmakers into 2027. Lam fell about 12%, AMD and Marvell about 8%, and Nvidia 4.4% after Jensen Huang said neocloud customers need far more capital than is available. Bloomberg reported that investors moved into Treasuries, pushing the 10-year yield below 5% for the first time in a month. Chip stocks recovered part of the loss on Friday, and the Nasdaq slipped 0.2%.

Electrification · China

Electric vehicles passed two-thirds of China’s car sales for the first time in September, as gasoline prices from the Hormuz crisis hit combustion cars

+

Carbon Brief’s analysis of China Passenger Car Association data, published Friday, shows new-energy vehicles reached a record 67.0% of passenger-car retail sales in September, up from August’s record of 65.2%. The share rose because the overall market fell sharply. Total retail sales were 1.618 million, down 28% from a year earlier, and NEV sales themselves fell 16.7% to 1.084 million. The CPCA blamed the collapse in combustion-car sales on fuel prices rising since the Hormuz crisis began.

Exports partly made up for weak domestic demand, with passenger-car exports up 73.7% to 917,000. The figures suggest the oil shock is pushing China’s car market toward EVs faster than policy alone had, a pattern that could also appear in other oil-importing markets if the Gulf stays closed.

MLB · World Series

Brewers hold off the Rays 3–2 in Game 1 after a replay overturns a game-ending double play, and Misiorowski opens with a 103.5 mph pitch

+

Milwaukee took a 1–0 series lead Friday night at American Family Field. Jacob Misiorowski’s first pitch, a 103.5 mph fastball to Yandy Díaz, was the fastest World Series pitch in the pitch-tracking era. He went five innings, allowing one run on four hits, and got the win. Christian Yelich’s RBI triple in a two-run first put the Brewers ahead for good, and Tampa Bay went 0-for-6 with runners in scoring position.

The ending was tense. What looked like a game-ending double play was overturned when replay showed shortstop Cooper Pratt’s foot was slightly off the bag. That brought in a run and left the tying and go-ahead runners at second and third. Closer Trevor Megill then struck out Jonny DeLuca on a 2–2 pitch. Neither franchise has won a World Series. Game 2 is Saturday night in Milwaukee.

Sources: NBC News/AP, Fox Sports, CBS Sports, ESPN
