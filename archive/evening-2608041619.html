2026 Midterms · Michigan Senate Primary

Michigan votes: progressive Abdul El-Sayed goes into the night as the heavy favorite over Schumer-backed Haley Stevens as polls close and returns begin

+

Polls across most of Michigan closed at 8 p.m. ET Tuesday (Aug. 4) and returns began flowing in the marquee Democratic Senate primary previewed this morning — the open-seat contest to replace retiring Sen. Gary Peters. As this brief published, no winner had been projected, but Abdul El-Sayed — the former Wayne County health director backed by Bernie Sanders and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez — entered election night as the clear favorite over four-term Rep. Haley Stevens, the establishment choice backed by Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer and much of the state party. Final independent polling had pointed the same way: an Emerson College survey gave El-Sayed a 16-point edge (57–41), and regulated prediction markets Kalshi and Polymarket both put his odds around 97%.

The result, once called, will be read as a verdict on the party’s direction — and on Israel and Gaza, which became the defining fault line after more than $50 million in outside spending, much of it from pro-Israel groups including AIPAC, flowed against El-Sayed, who would be the first Muslim U.S. senator. On the Republican side former Rep. Mike Rogers is effectively unopposed and awaits the Democratic winner in a genuine swing-state general election on Nov. 3.

The Left-Leaning Take

An El-Sayed win in a must-win purple state would be the clearest sign yet that the base has moved: unconditional military aid to Israel has become a liability rather than an asset, and AIPAC’s record spending failed to stop him. The progressive read is that Democratic voters, not consultants, are choosing the party’s future — and choosing rupture with a cautious establishment. (Synthesized from Slate and NOTUS reporting.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Conservatives frame an El-Sayed nomination as a gift to Mike Rogers: a fierce Israel critic comfortable alongside figures like streamer Hasan Piker would be far easier to nationalize and beat in November. In this telling Democrats are trading a winnable swing seat — and their Senate majority hopes — to satisfy their activist wing. (Synthesized from conservative campaign coverage, incl. Fox News.)

Sources: Washington Post (live results), NBC News (live results), CNN (live), Emerson College Polling, The Hill
Justice Department · Confirmation Fight

Blanche clears the Judiciary Committee on a 12–10 party-line vote, sending his attorney general nomination to the full Senate after the GOP holdouts folded

+

The development this morning’s brief flagged came to pass: the Senate Judiciary Committee voted Tuesday (Aug. 4) to advance acting Attorney General Todd Blanche — President Trump’s former personal defense lawyer — on a 12–10 party-line vote, with every Republican in favor and every Democrat opposed. The vote follows Blanche’s weekend order formally rescinding the roughly $1.8 billion “Anti-Weaponization Fund,” which brought GOP holdouts John Cornyn (Texas) and Thom Tillis (N.C.) on board; Tillis said his concerns about what he called a “payout pot for punks” had been addressed now that the fund was gone. The nomination now heads to the full Senate for a final confirmation vote, where the Republican majority is expected to carry it.

Democrats and some outlets continue to flag a caveat: Blanche’s order rescinds the May implementing order but, by CNN’s reading, not the underlying settlement provision requiring an attorney general to establish such a fund — an obligation that could arguably be revived. Cornyn and Tillis, both leaving the Senate, also secured an acknowledgment that a related tax-audit immunity arrangement applies only to specific named plaintiffs.

The Left-Leaning Take

The concession is cosmetic and the vote confirms it: two departing senators took a paper win and handed the Justice Department to a Trump loyalist who has argued the president has a “right” and “duty” to steer investigations of political enemies. If the settlement obligation survives the rescission, even the substantive win may be illusory. (Synthesized from Slate and Washington Post / Philadelphia Inquirer editorial opinion.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The party-line advance masks a real split on the right: National Review’s editorial board (“No to Todd Blanche”) and Reason urged rejection, Reason arguing he “blessed corruption” by facilitating a taxpayer-funded payout to Trump’s circle. Countervailing pro-Trump right: The Federalist dismisses that as Never-Trump droning and says the Senate should confirm a nominee committed to ending Biden-era “weaponization.”

Sources: ABC News, NBC News, Bloomberg, Washington Post, NPR
Foreign Policy · US–Iran War

Trump hardens the ultimatum — talks are Iran’s “last chance before decapitation” — as an emerging Iran–Oman deal would reopen Hormuz for a reported $1 million-per-ship “service fee”

+

President Trump escalated his rhetoric Monday into Tuesday, calling the negotiations he says are under way Iran’s “last chance” to avoid an escalation of U.S. strikes — adding, in France 24 and Euronews accounts, a threat that Tehran faces “decapitation” if it walks away. He again framed a deal around reopening the Strait of Hormuz “literally by tomorrow” and addressing the U.S.’s nuclear concerns, and said he pursued talks at the request of Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Qatar. Tehran continues to insist it is not in direct talks with Washington, negotiating only with Oman over safe passage through Hormuz.

The substantive new development is the shape of that Oman track. Per New York Times reporting, Iran and Oman are near a deal that would give Iran more control of the strait than before the war: inbound ships would transit a channel controlled by Iran, outbound ships a channel controlled by Oman, with a “service fee” — reportedly floated by Tehran at around $1 million per ship — split evenly and pitched as covering security, staffing and environmental costs. A U.S. official called Iran’s description “inaccurate” and insisted there would be no transit charges — a genuine dispute over whether any emerging deal cements Iranian leverage or removes it.

The Left-Leaning Take

Threatening “decapitation” one day and a deal the next is reckless improvisation with no strategy and no congressional authorization — and the escalating rhetoric undercuts the very diplomacy Trump claims to be pursuing while a war grinds on. (Synthesized from liberal / anti-war opinion.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The right hits from the opposite side: the Wall Street Journal editorial board has mocked the on-again, off-again threats as Trump’s “chaotic way of war,” and conservatives warn that a Hormuz arrangement letting Iran charge tolls to reopen a waterway it closed would reward the aggressor and hand Tehran a permanent chokehold. The libertarian read (Reason) casts the whole cycle as more bluster than plan.

Sources: France 24, Euronews, Al Jazeera, Al Jazeera (Oman track), IER (on NYT reporting)

Europe · Russia’s War on Ukraine

Ukraine warns Russia is turning the Black Sea into a “second Strait of Hormuz” as strikes on merchant shipping threaten to choke off its economic lifeline

+

The maritime front flagged this morning sharpened into an explicit economic-warfare threat. Ukraine’s acting foreign minister warned Tuesday (Aug. 4) that Russia is trying to make the Black Sea a “second Strait of Hormuz” — using missiles and drones to render Ukraine’s main export corridor so dangerous, costly and unpredictable that shipowners and insurers pull back on their own. In the latest incident a Russian drone struck a vessel carrying some 55,000 tons of high-grade iron-ore pellets in Ukrainian Black Sea waters, killing one crew member; Russia’s Defense Ministry said it continued hitting ports and ships carrying military cargo, and reported strikes on an automotive-equipment plant and a port terminal in the Odesa region.

Ukraine is answering in kind. An SBU source said Ukrainian forces struck three Russian ships at a Black Sea port, killing three sailors — the follow-on to the Aug. 1 sinking of the Rosatom-linked container ship Yanina near Novorossiysk. The mutual targeting of merchant vessels raises international-law questions around attacks on civilian shipping and freedom of navigation, and the blockade dynamic — making a route uninsurable rather than physically impassable — is precisely the tactic Kyiv is warning could strangle its grain and metals exports without a single ship being formally turned back.

Sources: The Washington Times, Kyiv Independent via Yahoo (SBU source), Ukrinform, The Moscow Times
Africa · Sudan Civil War

A Sudanese army drone strike on a village court in Darfur kills at least 35 as civilians gathered for hearings, a rights group says

+

The Sudanese army struck a traditional community court with drones in Garra al-Zawaya, North Darfur, on Sunday (Aug. 3), killing at least 35 people and wounding others, the Emergency Lawyers rights group said. The strike hit around 1 p.m. local time as civilians gathered to hear local cases and disputes — a session a witness said was being held under a tree with community leaders present. Among the dead were four tribal leaders and two commanders of the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF), which controls the village and has been locked in a devastating war with the army since April 2023.

Garra al-Zawaya lies near Kabkabiya, roughly 150 km west of El-Fasher — the army’s last Darfur stronghold before the RSF overran it in a bloody offensive last October. The attack underscores how heavily both sides now lean on drones against targets in populated areas: the United Nations says more than 1,000 civilians were killed in drone strikes in Sudan in the first five months of this year alone. It is a fresh reminder of a conflict that, despite its scale, remains far from the front of Western attention.

Sources: Al Jazeera, France 24, Democracy Now!, The New Arab

Middle East · Israel–Lebanon Diplomacy

US-brokered Israel–Lebanon talks open in Rome to implement a July framework — even as Hezbollah’s leader calls the negotiations “nothing but shame”

+

Israeli and Lebanese delegations arrived at the U.S. Embassy in Rome on Tuesday morning (Aug. 4) for a fresh round of direct, U.S.-mediated talks scheduled to run through Aug. 6. The technical teams — the Lebanese side including a military delegation, the Israeli side including IDF officials — are working to implement the framework the two signed last month, which envisions ending the war that erupted March 2, disarming Hezbollah, deploying the Lebanese Armed Forces (LAF) in the south, and a phased Israeli withdrawal via two “pilot zones” (Israel recently pulled out of Zawtar al-Gharbiyeh as the LAF moved in). On the agenda: expanding the pilot-zone process, resolving outstanding border points, and building toward a comprehensive deal. Just before the session, the LAF publicized weapons it had intercepted near the Syrian border — a signal it is taking disarmament seriously.

The talks open under strain. In a televised address marking the Shia Arbaeen commemoration, Hezbollah Secretary-General Sheikh Naim Qassem said direct negotiations with Israel would bring Lebanon “nothing but shame, humiliation, and successive compromises,” and accused President Joseph Aoun of having “become biased and divisive.” The core standoff persists: Beirut wants Israel to complete withdrawals from remaining occupied border points first, while Israel conditions further pullback on the LAF eliminating Hezbollah’s presence at the frontier — leaving the round at risk of stalling. (Outlets variously describe this as the sixth or seventh round.)

Artificial Intelligence

Alibaba releases Qwen3.8-Max, a 2.4-trillion-parameter model it says rivals or beats top US frontier systems — on its own, unverified benchmarks

+

Alibaba’s Qwen team launched Qwen3.8-Max on Aug. 2–3, its largest model yet: a mixture-of-experts design with roughly 2.4 trillion total parameters but only about 95 billion activated per request, a structure meant to hold down inference cost and latency. The company published benchmark tables positioning it against OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 “Sol” and Anthropic’s Claude models, reporting, for example, 93.0 on PaperBench and 82.8 on IFBench, and 86.6 on the Terminal-Bench 2.1 agentic-coding test — while trailing on some coding suites (67.7 on SWE-bench Pro).

The model is available now via QwenCloud, and Alibaba said it will open-source the weights on Hugging Face and ModelScope within about a week. An important caveat: the figures are Alibaba’s self-reported results and have not been independently verified. Coverage framed the release as the latest sign of Chinese labs closing the gap with U.S. frontier developers.

Sources: Bloomberg, SiliconANGLE, MarkTechPost, Neowin
Energy · Solar

The world crosses roughly 3 terawatts of installed solar capacity — a threshold reached far faster than the two before it

+

In an Aug. 3 analysis, Bloomberg reported that global cumulative installed solar PV passed the 3-terawatt (3,000 GW) mark in 2026, with the exact crossing date debated among analysts. The trajectory underscores the acceleration: after passing 100 GW in 2012, it took about a decade to reach the first terawatt (~2022); the second took under three years; and the third came less than two years after that. The IEA’s Photovoltaic Power Systems Programme pegged end-2025 cumulative capacity at roughly 2,974 GW, meaning the world entered 2026 right at the threshold.

The milestone lands amid shifts flagged by BloombergNEF’s New Energy Outlook 2026, which projects solar to become the single largest source of electricity globally by 2032 and cumulative solar to exceed 9 TW by 2036; BNEF has also noted that about 90% of new power-generating capacity added to grids last year came from solar, wind and batteries. (This is distinct from yesterday’s China solar-vs-coal item — it concerns total global installed PV.)

Sources: Bloomberg, Energy Connects, pv magazine, BloombergNEF
Space · Human Spaceflight

NASA sets Sept. 12 as the target launch for SpaceX’s Crew-13 mission to the ISS, with a three-agency crew

+

At two briefings at Johnson Space Center on Aug. 3, NASA and SpaceX said they are targeting no earlier than Sept. 12 for the Crew-13 launch to the International Space Station. The four-person crew will fly a Crew Dragon atop a Falcon 9 from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station for a long-duration science expedition.

The crew spans three agencies: NASA’s Jessica Watkins (spacecraft commander) and Luke Delaney (pilot), Canadian Space Agency astronaut Joshua Kutryk and Roscosmos cosmonaut Sergey Teteryatnikov, both mission specialists. Watkins is set to become the first NASA astronaut to fly on a Crew Dragon twice, while the flight will be Delaney’s first trip to space.
