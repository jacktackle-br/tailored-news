History

A Stoic voice speaks again from the ash of Vesuvius

+

On June 25, researchers from the Vesuvius Challenge gathered at the Biblioteca Nazionale di Napoli to announce something without precedent: the complete recovery of the surviving text of an entire carbonized Herculaneum scroll, read without ever physically unrolling it. Scroll PHerc. 1667 was one of hundreds turned to lumps of charcoal when Vesuvius buried the seaside town in 79 CE. Using high-resolution CT imaging and machine-learning models trained to detect the faint density signature of ink on scorched papyrus, the team virtually “unwrapped” roughly 1.4 metres of writing surface — about twenty-two columns of Greek. The content turns out to be a philosophical treatise on ethics: human nature, impulse, and moral progress. The final preserved column names Aristocreon, nephew and disciple of the great early Stoic Chrysippus, strongly suggesting the work belongs to the Stoic tradition, and perhaps to Chrysippus's own circle.

What makes this more than a technical stunt is that it reopens the only intact library to survive from classical antiquity. The treatise argues, in effect, that our “own nature” — human rationality and an innate inclination toward the good — should guide the pursuit of virtue and knowledge, a recognizably Stoic claim now recoverable in an ancient author's actual words rather than through later paraphrase. For two millennia these scrolls were considered destroyed; scholars now anticipate that the same method could restore hundreds more, potentially expanding the surviving corpus of Greek philosophy in a way no discovery has since the Renaissance. It is a rare case of technology not merely preserving the past but resurrecting lost arguments — putting a long-silent philosophical voice back into circulation.

Sources: Smithsonian Magazine, Artnet News, Newswise
Religion

The Renaissance heretics who reimagined original sin

+

In the June 25 issue of The New York Review of Books, historian Erin Maglaque reviews Umberto Grassi's What God Kept for Himself: Atheism, Sodomy, and Radical Dissent in Renaissance Italy, and the essay is a small revelation about how ordinary people quarrelled with the deepest doctrines of their faith. Grassi mines never-before-studied Inquisition trials from the sixteenth through eighteenth centuries in which defendants across the Italian peninsula were accused of the same startling heresy: the claim that the true original sin of Adam and Eve was not disobedience but sodomy — a “celestial” pleasure, they argued, that God had deliberately reserved for himself. Maglaque frames this against the long shadow of Augustine, who in late antiquity first fused sexuality with the story of the Fall and bequeathed the West its enduring association of sex with shame.

The interest lies in who these dissenters were and how their ideas spread — not learned theologians but artisans and shopkeepers' customers, trading heterodox readings of Genesis in streets and stores. Their arguments about sexual freedom, Grassi shows, became a wedge for something far larger: a skeptical, sometimes frankly atheistic critique that denounced clerical corruption, questioned papal authority, and floated the incendiary notion that organized religion was a hoax engineered to preserve elite power. It is a reminder that the intellectual history of unbelief did not descend fully formed from Enlightenment philosophers but bubbled up from below, out of the most intimate and embodied objections to what the Church taught about the body. The review makes a persuasive case that our sense of where “modern” religious doubt came from is due for revision.

Sources: The New York Review of Books, NYRB (June 25, 2026 issue)
Natural Sciences

The cave painters left their DNA on the wall

+

A study published this past week in Nature Communications reports a genuine first: recovering ancient human DNA directly from cave walls and prehistoric rock art. An international team, including paleoanthropologist Genevieve von Petzinger, sampled 54 spots across 24 rock-art panels in 11 caves in Spain and Portugal — from simple painted dots and hand stencils to the famous figurative animals of Altamira. In a red-dot sample from Portugal's Escoural Cave, and in two samples of bare, unpigmented wall (one male, one female), they detected traces of Homo sapiens DNA that had clung to the stone for thousands of years, plausibly reaching back into the Upper Palaeolithic, tens of millennia ago.

The promise here is startling. Rock art has always been anonymous — we admire the images but never meet the hands that made them. If DNA shed by touch or breath can be reliably recovered from the surfaces people worked on, archaeologists may begin to attach individual biological identities to specific paintings: to learn the sex, ancestry, and relatedness of the artists, and perhaps whether particular figures were made by the same person or across generations. Von Petzinger calls it “the start of a new era,” with the potential “to meet the actual artists.” Real caution applies — contamination and dating are formidable challenges, and pigment chemistry can degrade genetic material — but the method reframes decorated caves not merely as galleries of images but as archives of the people who stood, painted, and pressed their palms against the rock.

Sources: Smithsonian Magazine, Nature Communications, Max Planck Society
