Courts · Mail Voting

Trump goes to the Supreme Court a third time — on a Sunday — to force through USPS mail-ballot restrictions before the midterms

+

The Trump administration on Sunday, Sept. 6, filed an emergency application at the Supreme Court seeking to immediately implement new U.S. Postal Service rules restricting mail-in ballots for the November midterms — the third time it has escalated the fight to the justices, and, by SCOTUSblog’s read, “not meaningfully different” from a request the Justice Department already filed on Sept. 3. The move answers an order by U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani in Massachusetts, who on Friday extended her block on the rules indefinitely; her order had been set to expire Sept. 10. Talwani found the regulation — which operationalizes an executive order Trump signed March 31 and would have had USPS refuse to deliver mail ballots to certain voters — “unauthorized by the Elections Clause,” in conflict with Congress’s statutory scheme, and unconstitutional where it intrudes on powers held by Congress and the states.

The suit was brought by a coalition led by the League of Women Voters of Massachusetts alongside Democratic attorneys general from 24 states and the District of Columbia. The timing is the point: general-election ballots are already going out in the first states, so each day the rules stay blocked is a day the midterm vote proceeds under the old regime. The Supreme Court had in late August lifted one of two injunctions against the underlying executive order, leaving the mail-delivery piece as the live battleground now in front of the justices on an emergency posture. (Legal/elections story weighted to Tier 1 and Lawfare-style court reporting; the executive order’s ultimate merits are not yet decided.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left frames this as an open attempt to suppress the vote before an election the administration fears losing — repeatedly running to a friendly Supreme Court to override a district judge who found the scheme flatly unconstitutional, and doing it on a Sunday to beat the ballots into the mail. The through-line: when the law says no, the response is not to comply but to escalate. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The administration lane argues the executive branch has legitimate authority over the Postal Service and a real interest in election integrity, and that a single district judge should not be able to dictate national mail-ballot policy weeks before an election — exactly the kind of sweeping injunction the emergency docket exists to check. A libertarian-leaning strand (Reason-style) is warier: federalizing ballot delivery by executive fiat is itself a concentration of power, whichever party holds it. (Directional.)

2026 Midterms · GOP

Republicans convene their first-ever midterm national convention in Dallas, with Trump and Vance set to headline a ‘Trump-a-palooza’ less than 60 days out

+

Attention turned Sunday to Dallas, where the Republican National Committee is staging an unusual midterm national convention — the party’s first — at the American Airlines Center on Sept. 9–10. President Trump is slated to speak both nights, with a keynote on the first evening and closing remarks on the second; Vice President JD Vance, widely discussed as a 2028 successor, headlines the second night. With fewer than 60 days to the November elections, the RNC is casting the mid-cycle gathering as a way to celebrate nominees, fundraise, and manufacture momentum — what one report dubbed a “Trump-a-palooza.”

The event doubles as a stress test of party discipline: reporting noted a Texas RNC official urging the delegation not to boo former U.N. ambassador Nikki Haley ahead of her speech, a reminder of the lingering fault lines beneath the show of unity. On the Sunday shows, administration officials worked to downplay the consequences of the six-month Iran conflict even as the convention buildup dominated the political calendar. (Forward-looking; the convention formally opens Sept. 9.)

The directional left sees a party fusing itself entirely to Trump at a moment of Pentagon turmoil, an unauthorized foreign war and court losses — a coronation-style spectacle standing in for a governing record, with the Haley-booing anxiety exposing how little dissent the coalition now tolerates. Germany · State Election Result

The result is in: the far-right AfD wins Saxony-Anhalt in a landslide, roughly doubling the CDU and becoming the strongest party in a German state for the first time

+

The Saxony-Anhalt vote that was still in progress this morning has been called. According to ARD exit polls and projections, the Alternative for Germany (AfD) — classified as right-wing extremist by German domestic intelligence — took roughly 44.5%, more than double the center-right CDU’s about 18.5%, with Die Linke near 9.5% and the SPD around 8%. It is the first time the AfD has finished as the strongest party in a German state parliament, a milestone the party’s lead candidate Ulrich Siegmund hailed by saying, “We have achieved exactly what we wanted to achieve.” The CDU had governed the state for some 35 years.

A commanding plurality does not by itself deliver power. Every other party maintains the “firewall” (Brandmauer) refusing to govern with the AfD, so a CDU-led coalition assembled from second place remains the likeliest path to a government — but the arithmetic is now far tighter and the politics more strained than the pre-vote firewall assumed. Chancellor Friedrich Merz reiterated there is “no agreement with this party,” casting the AfD as the CDU’s “main opponent,” even as cracks show from within: the head of the CDU’s own youth wing in the state has said openly the party will eventually have to work with the AfD. The result lands as a national warning shot two weeks before further votes in Berlin and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, and as a direct rebuke to Merz’s wager that a firmer CDU would win voters back from the far right. (Figures are exit-poll/projection numbers; official results follow.)

Diplomacy · Russia–Ukraine War

Trump’s envoys call the Kyiv talks ‘substantive’ and press for three-way negotiations — but leave with no breakthrough

+

Following the morning’s report that Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner had arrived in Kyiv, the two envoys concluded their first wartime talks with President Zelensky on Sunday and described them as “substantive” and “very meaningful,” with Witkoff voicing confidence in a “good resolution.” Both sides said the discussions covered security and economic guarantees for Ukraine, deliveries of urgently needed air-defense missiles, and a support package as another wartime winter approaches. Zelensky called for the resumption of trilateral U.S.–Russia–Ukraine negotiations, and further talks are expected to fold in the national security advisers of France, Germany and the UK.

The gap that has defined the war did not close. No breakthrough was announced, and the substance still runs into the same wall visible after Saturday’s Kremlin meeting: Moscow wants territory and a bar on Ukraine’s NATO membership, while Kyiv will trade the NATO bid for firm guarantees but not cede land. The warmer choreography — “encouraging” language, talk of a trilateral format, European advisers joining — marks process, not resolution. The immediate test is whether the narrow, capital-only strike pause Putin allowed holds long enough to get any three-way table stood up, and whether Washington can convert “substantive” atmospherics into terms both capitals could actually sign. (Facts corroborated across CNN, NPR, ABC and RFE/RL; envoys’ characterizations are their own.)

Pentagon · Leak Investigation

The Pentagon polygraphs about 50 members of the Joint Staff in an ‘unprecedented’ hunt for the source of munitions-shortage leaks

+

The New York Times reported Thursday, Sept. 4 — with the story dominating coverage into the weekend — that the Pentagon subjected roughly 50 military and civilian members of the Joint Staff to polygraph tests in August, hunting for the sources of news reports about dwindling U.S. munitions stockpiles, including long-range missiles and Patriot interceptors expended during the Iran war. The military’s own internal investigators, not the FBI, administered the tests, which followed an earlier round over the summer that swept in some CENTCOM officials. Gen. Dan Caine, chairman of the Joint Chiefs, was not among those tested. A retired Navy judge advocate general called the scope unprecedented: “I’ve never seen polygraphs employed this broadly or at this seniority level.”

The campaign traces to Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who in July announced a joint Pentagon–DOJ task force to identify and prosecute leakers. Reporting describes an atmosphere of “distrust and paranoia,” with Hegseth having pushed out or blocked promotions of at least 80 generals, admirals and other officials. The politics broke into the open when Sen. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.), a member of the Senate Armed Services Committee, called on Trump to replace Hegseth, saying he “is intimidated by competence and retreats to ginning up culture wars instead of soberly attending to the vital work of our national defense.” Trump has stood by his secretary, calling him “fantastic.” (The originating scoop is the NYT; facts here are corroborated across CBS, The Hill and Al Jazeera.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads the polygraph spree as authoritarian paranoia that inadvertently confirms the underlying scandal — that the U.S. is running low on critical munitions in the middle of a war. The frame is of a loyalty-obsessed Hegseth intimidating career officers, chilling whistleblowers, and treating oversight-worthy facts as treason while gutting professional military leadership. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The administration lane counters that leaking munitions data during a shooting war is a genuine national-security breach that warrants aggressive investigation, and that the press is weaponizing the story. But the intra-right split is stark: the anti-Trump right (The Bulwark), with Tillis as an on-the-record Republican voice, treats the campaign as proof Hegseth is unfit — substituting intimidation for stewardship. Civil-libertarian voices (Reason-style) are uneasy about coerced polygraphs of career personnel regardless of the leak. (The Bulwark/Tillis line is intra-right disagreement, not the mainstream-right position; directional.)

Sources: CBS News, The Hill, Al Jazeera, Haaretz, Common Dreams (left take)
Oversight · Cabinet Conduct

A Labor Department watchdog finds ex-Secretary Chavez-DeRemer ran a ‘toxic’ department, drank on the job and misused public funds

+

On Thursday, Sept. 4, the Labor Department’s Office of Inspector General released a report concluding that former Labor Secretary Lori Chavez-DeRemer oversaw a “toxic, intimidating and humiliating” work environment and committed misconduct across six areas. Investigators found she regularly drank alcohol during the workday, maintained an “inappropriately close and unprofessional relationship” with a member of her security detail, failed to properly report expensive gifts, misused public funds on travel that mixed business with personal trips, and directed subordinates to perform personal tasks. The OIG interviewed 53 current and former employees and said it found merit in all the original complaints “and more.”

Chavez-DeRemer was not interviewed — she resigned the day before her scheduled interview. She had served about a year and left in April 2026, the third Trump Cabinet member to step down. Her attorney, Nick Oberheiden, pushed back, telling the Washington Post the report “confirms that Secretary Chavez-DeRemer did not violate any laws” and that while some “may not have liked the way she managed the Department,” she “looks back with … pride” at serving the administration. (Charges are administrative findings, not criminal; she was not interviewed for the report.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left treats this as fresh confirmation of Trump-Cabinet corruption and impunity — a political appointee running a department like a personal fiefdom and escaping accountability by resigning before investigators could question her. A pointed strand uses Chavez-DeRemer, a onetime bipartisan-courted pick, to argue Democrats should never lend cover to Trump nominees. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The institutional right has largely stayed muted — no prominent editorial defense surfaced. The response has centered on Chavez-DeRemer’s own camp reframing the findings as a management-style dispute rather than lawbreaking, while some right-leaning coverage foregrounds the personal-conduct angle as a discrete scandal rather than an indictment of the administration. A good-government/libertarian read simply treats it as a straightforward case for spending and ethics accountability regardless of party. (Directional.)

Europe · Russia–Ukraine War

Trump’s envoys carry a peace push from the Kremlin to Kyiv, as Putin allows a capital-only strike pause but rejects a broader ceasefire

+

President Trump’s envoys Steve Witkoff and adviser Jared Kushner arrived in Kyiv on Sunday, Sept. 6, for their first wartime visit to the Ukrainian capital, a day after roughly three hours of talks with Vladimir Putin at the Kremlin on Saturday. Kremlin foreign-policy aide Yuri Ushakov called the meeting “substantive, constructive, exceptionally candid,” said the U.S. side “presented specific settlement ideas,” and noted the two sides also discussed potential bilateral U.S.–Russia economic projects. Putin confirmed a pause on strikes targeting Kyiv but rejected a broader multi-day frontline ceasefire. Trump has billed the mission as carrying a “proposal to end the war.”

Ahead of the Kyiv leg, President Zelensky said “We are ready for talks … Our proposals are ready,” signaling willingness to drop Ukraine’s NATO bid in exchange for firm Western security guarantees while restating his red lines against ceding territory. The pause is narrow and fragile: Russia had hit Ukraine’s SBU headquarters and Kyiv’s two international airports on Friday and overnight, and reporting indicated strikes elsewhere in Ukraine into Sunday killed at least one person and wounded about 10 — the truce covers capitals only, not the front or other cities. The core gap is unchanged: Moscow wants territory and no NATO; Kyiv will trade NATO for guarantees but not land. Ushakov’s warm language plus talk of economic projects suggests Russia is dangling normalization rather than battlefield concessions. (Facts corroborated across CNN, NBC, NPR and CBS; the Ushakov characterization is the Kremlin’s own.)

Middle East · US–Iran Conflict

Iran says it struck back at tankers and U.S. vessels in the Strait of Hormuz, a day after Washington sank three of its oil carriers ‘three for two’

+

The U.S.–Iran tanker war escalated again over the weekend. On Saturday, Sept. 5, U.S. Central Command said it “permanently disabled” two Iranian crude carriers — the M/T Downy off Kharg Island and the M/T Stark 1 near Jask — and “completely destroyed” a third, the unladen M/T Kylo, in the Gulf of Oman, in retaliation after the IRGC fired ballistic missiles at a U.S. aircraft carrier and a guided-missile destroyer (both evaded the attacks; no U.S. personnel were harmed). It issued a new warning to commercial shipping to “refrain from any suspicious movement … Otherwise, you will be targeted.” The tit-for-tat is now an institutionalized doctrine over control of the Strait, raising the risk of miscalculation each cycle. Germany · State Election

Saxony-Anhalt votes, with the far-right AfD polling around 40% and eyeing its first turn as the strongest party in a German state parliament

+

Voters in the eastern German state of Saxony-Anhalt go to the polls Sunday, Sept. 6, in a Landtag election that could make the Alternative for Germany (AfD) — classified as right-wing extremist by German domestic intelligence — the strongest party in a state parliament for the first time. Final pre-election polling put the AfD around 40–42% (one tracker showed roughly 41.7%), close to double the center-right CDU at about 22–23%, with Die Linke near 12%, the SPD around 8%, and the Greens hovering near the 5% threshold. The incumbent government is a CDU–SPD–FDP coalition under Minister-President Sven Schulze; polls close at 6 p.m. local time (9 a.m. PT), with exit polls to follow immediately.

Even a clear AfD plurality would not automatically translate into power: every other party maintains a “firewall” (Brandmauer) refusing to govern with the AfD, so the CDU could still form a government from second place if the AfD falls short of a majority. The real stakes are whether the AfD comes close enough to an absolute majority that the arithmetic of the firewall becomes fragile, and the national signal such a result would send. Watch the exit polls for the AfD’s actual share versus an outright majority, whether the Greens and FDP clear 5%, and the CDU’s coalition-math statements Sunday evening. (Framed as an election in progress; results were not yet published at press time.)

Science · Synthetic Biology

A natural enzyme accurately transcribes an eight-letter DNA alphabet, doubling the code all known life runs on

+

UC San Diego researchers reported that RNA polymerase — the core enzyme that reads DNA to make RNA, the first step of gene expression — can accurately transcribe an expanded eight-letter genetic alphabet, doubling the four bases (A, C, G, T) used by all known life. The synthetic system, called hachimoji (“eight letter” in Japanese), adds four lab-made bases (P, Z, B, S). The study, led by Dong Wang of the Skaggs School of Pharmacy, was published Sept. 2 in Nature Communications, with coverage breaking Sept. 4. The team provided structural evidence that the cell’s natural machinery can process the synthetic information — a long-sought synthetic-biology goal that could eventually enable engineered organisms producing molecules not found in nature.

(Caveat: this is fundamental, structural biochemistry demonstrated in vitro with bacterial — E. coli — RNA polymerase, not a functioning synthetic organism or therapy. The result concerns the enzyme’s ability to read the expanded code; applications such as engineered biosystems remain years off.)

Climate · Extreme Heat

Spain matches its all-time September heat record at 45.7°C as a late-season heatwave grips the country’s south

+

Spain matched its joint-highest September temperature on record — 45.7°C (114.3°F), recorded Thursday, Sept. 3, in the southwestern town of El Granado, per state meteorological agency AEMET, and reported Sept. 4. The reading ties the previous September national record set at Montoro in 2016 and was the highest recorded anywhere in Spain so far in 2026. It is part of an unusually persistent, late-season pull of 40°C-plus heat across the south and west, following a summer of repeated European heatwaves. In related public-health data, Spanish figures put heat-related deaths in 2026 at 5,232 — the highest since 2015.

Scientists attribute the intensification and prolongation of such episodes to human-driven climate change. (Caveat: a single record-tying reading is a weather event, not by itself a trend; the attribution framing reflects the established scientific consensus on heatwave intensification rather than a new formal study for this specific day. Heat-death tallies are provisional and typically revised.)

Electrification · Autonomy

Federal safety regulators open a probe into Tesla’s no-wheel, no-pedal Cybercab on its Austin launch week

+

NHTSA’s Office of Defects Investigation opened an audit (Open Audit Query AQ26002) into Tesla’s Cybercab, examining how Tesla self-certified a purpose-built vehicle with no steering wheel, no pedals and no mirrors as compliant with Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards. The query, opened Sept. 3, covers an estimated ~1,000 vehicles and landed the same week Tesla began limited commercial deployment of the two-seat robotaxi in Austin (Thursday, Sept. 3), initially offering rides to a small group of shareholders and influencers before wider app access. Tesla told the agency the vehicles comply with all applicable standards, arguing some rules written around human controls do not apply to a car without them; its shares fell Sept. 4 as the update was seen as underwhelming and the probe emerged.

(Caveat: an “audit query” is an information-gathering compliance review, not a recall or a finding of a defect — no violation has been established and outcomes are pending. The regulatory backdrop is in flux, with an administration proposal earlier in 2026 that would relax some autonomous-vehicle control requirements.)
