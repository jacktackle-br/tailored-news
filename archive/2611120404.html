White House · Oversight

White House counsel warns the incoming Oversight chair that Democratic probes “may cross the line into blatant criminality”; Garcia says Congress needs no permission

+

White House counsel Will Scharf has sent a letter, dated Monday and first reported by Punchbowl News, to Rep. Robert Garcia (D-Calif.), who is expected to chair the House Oversight Committee when Democrats take the majority in January. The letter says congressional investigations must serve a “legitimate legislative purpose,” and that failing to show one would be “a clear abuse of power.” It also warns that “threats to executive branch officials designed to deter them from public service, or process designed merely to abuse or instill fear in the recipient may cross the line into blatant criminality.” Scharf cited Garcia’s election-night advice that administration officials should “lawyer up” as evidence of improper motive. Garcia’s planned agenda reportedly covers the handling of the Epstein files, ICE operations, the White House ballroom and other construction, Trump family mineral deals and crypto dealings.

Garcia replied that Congress’s investigative power comes from Article I, “not from the permission of the White House,” and that Democrats “are not going to be threatened or intimidated.” Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next Speaker, called the letter “outrageous.” Rep. Jamie Raskin said Garcia would be within bounds to seek a restraining order. Attorney General Todd Blanche separately said Oversight members should “be very worried.” Politico called the letter a departure from precedent, because the Justice Department usually handles disputes over oversight. Legal experts quoted across outlets said the letter has little practical force. The Supreme Court’s cases, including Trump v. Mazars, require only that a probe concern a subject on which legislation “could be had,” and lawmakers are protected for legislative acts. In a related development, Rep. Thomas Massie’s discharge petition for a second Epstein Files Transparency Act reached 218 signatures on Wednesday, which forces a House vote before this Congress ends.

The Left-Leaning Take

Mother Jones summed up the letter in its headline: “Investigate Trump? The White House Says That Might Be a Crime.” Left-leaning commentators read it as an attempt to intimidate the new majority before it holds any power. Critics quoted in coverage were blunt. NYU’s Ryan Goodman called it “legally repulsive” and suggested the letter itself could touch obstruction-of-Congress law, and Princeton’s Deborah Pearlstein called it a “ham-fisted stab at intimidation.” Several argued it helps Democrats by putting on record an intent to obstruct.

The Right-Leaning Take

No in-window National Review, Dispatch or WSJ editorial piece could be verified. The administration’s case rests on the Court’s holdings that oversight cannot be used for law enforcement or to punish opponents. On that view, Garcia’s “lawyer up” comments and his talk of working with state attorneys general look like targeting, not legislating. Reason, writing from the libertarian side, reported the letter Wednesday and gave prominent space to critics who doubt it has any legal weight.

Senate · Courts

Senate Republicans’ lame-duck rush to confirm about two dozen Trump judges runs into Democratic objections and a Tillis holdout

+

Republicans hand control of the Senate to Democrats on January 3. Majority Leader John Thune has pledged to keep senators in Washington well into December, if needed, to confirm four appeals-court nominees and about 21 district-court nominees. Trump has publicly demanded action on all “161 Nominations, including 25 Federal Judges.” On Tuesday the Judiciary Committee sent four district nominees to the floor. Chairman Chuck Grassley has signaled nomination hearings in back-to-back weeks. Democrats led by Dick Durbin say that breaks the committee’s practice of holding hearings at least two weeks apart and allowing 28 days to review nominee questionnaires.

CNN reports that the push is already hitting speed bumps. Grassley had to postpone a committee vote on Oklahoma nominee Zachary West because of opposition from fellow Republican Thom Tillis. A Fifth Circuit nomination was also reshuffled after home-state objections. Grassley argues that Democrats confirmed a batch of judges in the 2024 lame duck and have signaled they will block Trump nominees in the next Congress. Executive-branch nominees can move in packages, but judicial nominees cannot, so each one needs floor time.

The Left-Leaning Take

No in-window Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion piece could be verified. MS NOW’s Maddow blog called Grassley’s justification an “unserious claim.” It argued that the idea he has “no choice” but to rush nominees because Democrats might be unprecedentedly obstructive ignores Republicans’ own blockade of Obama’s judges in 2015–16 and their slow-walking of Biden’s.

The Right-Leaning Take

Grassley made the case himself in a Wall Street Journal op-ed: “The stakes are far too high for Republicans to take their feet off the gas now.” He pointed to Democrats’ lame-duck confirmations in 2024 and their vow to stonewall. At Reason’s Volokh Conspiracy, a Wednesday post on the Fifth Circuit fight argued that “the President nominates circuit judges, not senators”. It criticized home-state senators for sinking a nominee and noted that this is friction inside the GOP, not only from Democrats.

Ukraine · Diplomacy

Witkoff and Kushner meet Putin at the Kremlin as some 50 European leaders gather in Dublin, where Zelensky awaits the envoys

+

U.S. envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner arrived in Moscow on Thursday and were met at the airport by Kremlin envoy Kirill Dmitriev. Their talks with Vladimir Putin were set for around noon Moscow time. It is Witkoff’s tenth in-person meeting with Putin. The trip follows two days of trilateral talks in Abu Dhabi that agreed another large prisoner exchange but made no public breakthrough on territory. Kyiv still rejects Moscow’s demand for all of Donbas, so U.S. mediators have been steering the talks toward security guarantees and reconstruction. Kushner said they would ask Putin to let European representatives into future rounds. No readout of the Kremlin meeting had been published when this edition went out. British and German press reports of a proposal to redraw the administrative boundaries of Donetsk and Luhansk have not been confirmed by Kyiv or Moscow.

The envoys go on to Dublin to brief Volodymyr Zelensky. He is attending the ninth European Political Community summit, which brings roughly 50 leaders to the Convention Centre Dublin and is co-chaired by Taoiseach Micheál Martin and European Council President António Costa. Zelensky addresses the opening plenary, and an informal European Council follows on Friday. Reports differ on whether the envoys meet him Thursday or Friday.

Gaza · Ceasefire

Israeli strike on Nasser Hospital in Khan Younis kills at least six, including a surgeon, as Israel says it targeted a senior Hamas commander

+

An overnight Israeli strike on Nasser Hospital in Khan Younis, the largest hospital in southern Gaza, killed at least six people and wounded about 20, according to Gaza’s health ministry and a civil-defence official quoted by AFP. Medics said a surgeon was among the dead. The Israeli military said it struck a senior Hamas figure from the Khan Younis brigade, identified in reports as Abu Ismail, and other militants from Hamas’s armed wing. It accused Hamas of operating from the hospital. Some reports say he was killed, but Israel had not clearly confirmed his death.

Hamas spokesman Hazem Qassem called the strike a war crime and blamed the U.S.-backed Board of Peace for failing to stop it. The ceasefire that took effect in October 2025 is formally still in place. Both sides accuse each other of violations, and Israel has recently stepped up strikes it says target senior Hamas members.

Iran · Terrorism

U.S. indicts three alleged Iran-backed operatives over a campaign of nearly 20 attacks, including a foiled assault on RAF Fairford

+

Federal prosecutors in New York unsealed charges against three men, indicted by a grand jury on Tuesday. They are accused of carrying out a campaign of nearly 20 attacks and attempted attacks across Britain, Europe and the United States on behalf of Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps and the Iraqi militia Kata’ib Hezbollah. The most serious alleged plot targeted RAF Fairford, a base in southern England that hosts U.S. aircraft used in the war against Iran. On September 26, three vans headed for the base. At least two were stopped at a security roadblock, and British police seized all three and arrested the people inside early the next morning. Prosecutors say one leader directed the plot from a Brooklyn jail through coded messages in phone calls with relatives.

The indictment also cites plots against synagogues in London and New York, arson and explosives attacks, and a near-fatal stabbing of two Jewish men in London. British authorities had already charged two men over the Fairford incident and say their investigation continues. The charges are allegations and have not been proven in court. The case adds a homeland-security front to a conflict that Washington is currently waging mainly through a naval blockade and sanctions.

Tech · Chips

Nvidia’s board meets on whether to oust Jensen Huang, sending shares down sharply, as Reuters finds a blacklisted PLA-linked university bought H200s

+

Nvidia’s board met Wednesday to discuss whether to remove Jensen Huang as chief executive, CNBC confirmed after the Wall Street Journal first reported that directors were preparing to oust him. Shares fell about 8% after hours and were down around 7% on Thursday, extending a slide of more than 20% over the past month. In a message to employees, Huang admitted he had been unfaithful to his wife, apologized and said he would cooperate with the board’s review. On November 8 the board opened an independent investigation into reports that Huang had been targeted by a foreign intelligence effort. It said it had no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised. No decision on his position had been announced as of this edition. A Reuters analysis said investors fear a new leader might pull back from Nvidia’s practice of financing its own customers’ data centers, which has helped sustain the AI build-out.

Separately, a Reuters exclusive found that Northwestern Polytechnical University, which has close ties to the People’s Liberation Army and has been on the U.S. Entity List since 2001, bought two Nvidia H200 chips in October for 874,700 yuan (about $123,000). Reuters reviewed 26 procurement and corporate records from this year that show Chinese universities and institutes buying, or renting access to, U.S.-restricted Nvidia hardware through domestic suppliers. The findings add to scrutiny of Huang’s lobbying for looser export rules.

Energy · Grid

Josh Hawley becomes the first GOP senator to call for a nationwide moratorium on data-center construction until ratepayers are protected

+

Sen. Josh Hawley (R-Mo.) on Wednesday listed a “MORATORIUM on data centers” among five priorities he wants Senate Republicans to pursue. His office told Politico the pause would last “until Congress passes a bill of rights giving taxpayers guarantees of no rate increases,” with no fixed end date. That makes him the first Senate Republican to back a national pause. Progressive Democrats, including Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, have pushed their own AI Data Center Moratorium Act. In February, Hawley and Sen. Richard Blumenthal (D-Conn.) introduced a bill that would put household electricity first and bar utilities from passing data-center-driven costs on to consumers.

The call follows midterms in which rising power bills were a campaign issue and candidates who wanted tighter limits on data centers did well. Heatmap polling earlier this year found seven in ten Americans would oppose a data center near their home, up from four in ten in 2025. A Senate moratorium bill currently has no cosponsors, so the near-term effect is political: a populist-right voice joins the left in treating AI’s demand for power as a pocketbook issue.

Science · Genomics

All of Us study of nearly 100,000 Americans finds every participant carries at least one variant in genes that shape drug response

+

Researchers analyzing genomes from nearly 100,000 participants in the NIH’s All of Us program report in Science Translational Medicine that every participant carried at least one variant in the 16 pharmacogenes they studied. These are genes that affect how people metabolize or respond to common medicines. The team also catalogued thousands of previously unrecognized variants in those genes, according to STAT.

All of Us deliberately recruited groups underrepresented in genetic research, which is why it turned up variants that earlier, mostly European-ancestry datasets missed. The results strengthen the case for routine pharmacogenomic testing before prescribing. They also show how much of the variation still lacks clinical guidance, because many newly found variants have unknown effects on dosing or side effects.
