Trade · Economy

US–Canada trade talks collapse at the deadline and Trump’s 50% tariffs take effect; Carney vows to match them ‘dollar for dollar’

+

US–Canada trade negotiations broke down late Friday, Aug. 21, minutes before a deadline, and new 50% US tariffs on roughly $20–28 billion of Canadian goods took effect at midnight into Saturday, Aug. 22. The duties hit hundreds of items — lumber and plywood, liquor and wine, electrical and telecom equipment, motor-vehicle-related goods, furniture, cement and building materials, even hockey gear. The White House cast the escalation as a response to Canada’s alleged “discriminatory treatment” of US dairy, alcohol and auto exports; Trump invoked a decades-old tariff statute to impose the levies after Canada retaliated against his March tariffs.

Prime Minister Mark Carney suspended negotiations and said Canada “will match those tariffs dollar for dollar to protect our workers and businesses.” He said “important progress” had been made but blamed “last-minute changes in the U.S. proposed terms” that were “unfair, uneconomic, and called into question the reliability of any deal.” No further talks are scheduled, deepening a US–Canada trade war that has run since 2025 and landing squarely on affordability anxieties ahead of the midterms. (Facts from NBC News, CNBC, Bloomberg and NPR.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left folds this into its affordability message: tariffs are a self-inflicted consumer tax, and refusing to negotiate simply keeps Americans paying. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer said “Americans cannot afford Donald Trump’s price-spiking tariff temper tantrums,” with his office pegging the hit at roughly $1,300 per household across goods from lumber to beef. Polling, they note, shows most Americans and most economists expect consumers — not foreign governments — to eat the cost. (Directional; drawn from Schumer’s statement and Democratic messaging.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The sharpest in-window conservative voice was the free-market right: the Wall Street Journal editorial board called the move “madness,” warning that “the more Mr. Trump keeps swinging recklessly, the more Americans are likely to think there’s only madness in his tariff methods,” and that he “is using the law to punish Canada for retaliating against his tariffs.” The administration’s own case: the levies are leverage to force Ottawa to drop “discriminatory” barriers on US dairy, autos and alcohol. No in-window NR or Dispatch editorial defending the tariffs was isolated. (WSJ editorial is free-market right, not a defense of the policy.)

Sources: NBC News, CNBC, Bloomberg, NPR, WSJ editorial (via reporting)
Economy · The Iran War at Home

Gas tops $4 a gallon as the Iran war grinds on, and Trump dismisses the spike as ‘a little glitch’

+

The US national average gasoline price is at or above $4 a gallon — reported figures around $4.06 to $4.24 — up nearly a dollar from about $3.14 a year ago, roughly a 35% jump since the US–Iran war began, according to AAA. Moody’s estimated the increase has added on the order of $100 billion in household costs over three months. The driver is the war’s squeeze on global oil supply: traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, which normally carries about a fifth of the world’s oil, remains sharply reduced, with Reuters (citing Kpler) reporting just seven commodity ships passed on Thursday. A 60-day deadline for a US–Iran deal expired Monday with no agreement and no talks scheduled.

Trump dismissed the increase as “a little glitch” and “a very small price to pay” for safety, predicting prices will fall “when it’s over”; Vice President JD Vance said driving oil prices back down is now “goal No. 1.” The politics are turning sour for the GOP: an NBC News poll put Trump’s approval on cost of living at 36% (62% disapprove), and a majority disapproved of his handling of Iran, feeding Republican anxiety about the midterms. (Facts from the Associated Press, PBS NewsHour, NBC News and CNBC.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left calls this a self-created crisis for a president who campaigned on cutting costs and avoiding foreign wars — two core promises broken at once. An MSNBC opinion piece framed high gas prices from “Trump’s war on Iran” as his next political reckoning, and the “little glitch” line strikes progressives as tone-deaf while households absorb hundreds to well over a thousand dollars in added costs. (Directional; MSNBC opinion plus Guardian/Vox-style coverage.)

The Right-Leaning Take

On the right the dominant note is anxiety, not defense: Fox News reported GOP strategists warning that Republicans “face a ticking midterm clock” and that Trump must end the conflict fast or pay at the polls. The administration’s affirmative case — from Trump and Vance — is that short-term pain is worth “Safety and Peace” and that lower oil prices are now the top war aim. No in-window NR or Dispatch column making the affirmative “worth-it” argument was isolated. (Directional.)

Courts · DOJ · Rule of Law

The 2nd Circuit rules Trump prosecutor John Sarcone was unlawfully installed, leaving his subpoenas of NY AG Letitia James quashed

+

On Friday, Aug. 21, the US Court of Appeals for the 2nd Circuit ruled 2–1 that John Sarcone — a Trump ally who had been acting US attorney for the Northern District of New York — was not lawfully serving when, in August 2025, he issued subpoenas to Attorney General Letitia James’s office seeking records on her civil suits against Trump, the Trump family business and the NRA. The court affirmed a lower-court decision barring Sarcone from the probe, leaving the subpoenas quashed. The 27-page opinion, by Judge Guido Calabresi, held that the Federal Vacancies Reform Act lets a “first assistant” automatically become acting officer only if already serving when the vacancy arose; Sarcone became first assistant months after the post went vacant, after judges declined to extend his tenure and AG Pam Bondi re-designated him.

Calabresi wrote that the government’s reading “rewards evasion and penalizes compliance” and would let a president “install virtually anyone.” It is at least the third time a federal appeals court has rejected the administration’s method of installing US attorneys without Senate confirmation; one judge dissented, siding with DOJ. A Justice Department spokesperson said the administration disagrees and intends to appeal directly to the Supreme Court, while James’s office is opposing any stay. (Facts from NBC News, CBS News, CNN and the 2nd Circuit opinion, docket 26-156.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads it as a rebuke of politicized prosecution: Sarcone’s subpoenas were an illegitimate, retaliatory probe of a Trump adversary, and the ruling reaffirms that the administration cannot bypass Senate confirmation to weaponize US attorney offices against the president’s enemies. Outlets like Democracy Docket cast it as one more court closing off an end-run around the Constitution. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The operative right-of-center response is the DOJ’s own: the department disagrees and will seek Supreme Court review, and the lone dissenting judge accepted the government’s FVRA reading — the basis for a “this was a lawful appointment; the majority misreads the vacancies statute” argument. No dedicated in-window NR or Dispatch editorial on the ruling was isolated; any Bulwark treatment would be intra-right/anti-Trump rather than the mainstream-right position. (Directional.)

Sources: NBC News, CBS News, 2nd Circuit opinion (26-156), Washington Examiner

Europe · Russia–Ukraine

A Russian ‘double-tap’ drone strike on a Kryvyi Rih shopping mall kills at least 16 in Zelensky’s hometown

+

Russian drones hit the “Sunny Gallery” shopping center in Kryvyi Rih — President Volodymyr Zelensky’s birthplace — on Friday afternoon, Aug. 21. About half an hour after the first strike, a second drone hit the same building while firefighters and medics were already inside, a “double-tap” pattern Ukrainian officials say is designed to kill first responders. Local officials put the toll at 14 to 16 dead (figures rose through the day) and roughly 121–130 wounded, including about 22 children; as of early Saturday, nine people, two of them children, remained missing under the rubble. Zelensky called it a “terrorist” act carried out in two waves and vowed a response.

The strike — one of the deadliest on Kryvyi Rih since April 2025 — lands amid a diplomatic stall. Moscow said this week it is “ready for new ideas” to end the war but only ones matching Putin’s goals, with Deputy Foreign Minister Sergey Ryabkov inviting a revived US mediation role aligned with “realities on the ground.” The continued targeting of civilian sites even as the Kremlin signals openness to talks hardens Ukraine’s argument that Russia is not negotiating in good faith. Death-toll figures varied by source and time of day. (Facts from the Associated Press, CBS News, Al Jazeera and the Kyiv Post.)

Africa · Global Health

DR Congo’s Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak becomes the deadliest in the country’s history, passing 2,300 deaths

+

The Democratic Republic of the Congo’s Ebola outbreak has become the deadliest the country has ever recorded. Local authorities reported roughly 2,325 deaths, surpassing the 2,299 of the 2018–2020 epidemic that had been the previous record; the WHO’s latest figures (as of Aug. 20) count about 5,290 confirmed cases and 2,516 related deaths overall, making this the largest Ebola outbreak ever in the DRC and the fastest-growing on record. What began in Ituri Province’s Mongbwalu health zone has spread to 54 health zones across six provinces and over the border into Uganda.

The crisis is especially dangerous because it involves the Bundibugyo species of Ebola, for which there is no approved vaccine or specific treatment — unlike the Zaire species targeted by existing vaccines. The WHO says that although contact tracing is improving, the epidemic is still “winning the race”: hidden transmission chains persist, many patients reach advanced stages without care, deaths are occurring outside health facilities and several treatment centers are overwhelmed. The absence of a Bundibugyo vaccine has stripped responders of the single most effective tool that helped contain past outbreaks. (Facts from UN News, the WHO and the ECDC.)

Sources: UN News, WHO, WHO Africa (Sit. Rep. 14), ECDC

Analysts cited figures of up to roughly $120 billion in potential cumulative revenue through fiscal 2033 if Google hits the targets its stake option depends on — those revenue and share-value figures are projections, not booked results. The pact was still moving chip stocks into Friday, Aug. 21, ahead of Nvidia’s Aug. 26 earnings, and reads as another sign that the hyperscalers are racing to lock up custom silicon and diversify away from any single supplier. Climate · El Niño

Forecasters put roughly 2-in-3 odds on 2026 becoming the strongest El Niño on record as Pacific heat hits extremes

+

Fresh mid-to-late-August data show an exceptional buildup of subsurface heat across the central and eastern equatorial Pacific. Reporting on the Aug. 16 pentad data placed the peak subsurface anomaly near +11°C, with the warm layer pressing eastward, while the weekly Niño-3.4 index centered on Aug. 12 reached about +2.7°C. Forecasters now calculate roughly a 69% chance the event exceeds every prior El Niño in the relative-index record dating to 1950, and the US Climate Prediction Center’s probability of a “very strong” El Niño in October–December rose from 81% in July to 95% in August.

Australia’s Bureau of Meteorology reported July equatorial upper-ocean heat as the highest for any month in its record since 1979. Models project the event could peak near record strength in the fourth quarter of 2026 and wind down around mid-2027 — forecasts, not observed outcomes. A record-strength El Niño would tilt the odds toward a warmer global year and reshuffle rainfall and drought patterns across the tropics and beyond. (Facts from the IRI/Columbia ENSO outlook and August ENSO-monitoring reporting; probabilities are model-based.)

Science · Astronomy & Cosmology

Simulations suggest the raw ingredients for rocky planets existed just ~100 million years after the Big Bang

+

A University of Portsmouth-led study argues the chemical building blocks for rocky planets — carbon, oxygen and iron — could have been in place only about 100 million years after the Big Bang, far earlier than the conventional assumption that planet formation ramped up billions of years later. The proposed mechanism: the universe’s first, very massive “Population III” stars died as supernovae, seeding the surrounding gas with heavy elements. Computer models then tracked how that debris settled around later, smaller stars (about 70% the Sun’s mass), producing the debris disks that are precursors to planets.

The researchers are explicit that the work rests on simulations, not any direct observation of an early-universe planet; confirming when the first rocky worlds actually formed will require deeper observations with advanced telescopes. Even as a modeling result, it pushes the plausible dawn of rocky-planet chemistry startlingly close to the cosmic beginning and sharpens questions about how early habitable worlds — and their ingredients — could have appeared. (Facts from the University of Portsmouth, Universe Today, Sci.News and Phys.org.)

Energy · Electrification & Storage

US clean-energy investment hit $75B in Q2, and home batteries outsold rooftop solar for a second straight quarter

+

The Clean Investment Monitor — a Rhodium Group and MIT CEEPR project — reported US clean-energy and transportation investment of $75 billion in the second quarter of 2026, up about 22% from the first quarter and roughly 4% year over year, the second-highest quarter on record. Retail and consumer spending drove much of it (around 56%): EVs, plug-in hybrids, heat pumps and home batteries. A standout signal of the electrification shift: residential battery-storage installations reached about $12 billion, roughly double the prior quarter, surpassing residential solar sales for the second consecutive quarter.

The jump comes as households increasingly pair batteries with solar or buy them outright for resilience against outages and rising grid stress. The figures are as reported by the Monitor and were published in the mid-August window; some coverage frames the quarterly gain as up to about 22%. The report lands against a policy backdrop of shifting federal clean-energy incentives, making the resilience of consumer demand a closely watched indicator. (Facts from the Clean Investment Monitor, Semafor, Forbes and RTO Insider.)

Sources: Clean Investment Monitor, Semafor, Forbes, RTO Insider
