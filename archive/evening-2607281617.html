Congress · A State Farewell

Washington buries Lindsey Graham — and Trump installs the late hawk’s sister in his Senate seat

+

Washington held funeral services Tuesday, July 28, for Sen. Lindsey Graham (R-S.C.), who died July 11 at 71 of an aortic dissection. A morning ceremony in the Capitol Rotunda preceded a 2 p.m. funeral at Washington National Cathedral, where President Trump delivered a tribute; mourners included Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky, both in Washington for White House talks (see Global Affairs). Colleagues eulogized “a true American original,” and Roll Call captured the sentiment: “No one like Lindsey.” A second service and burial follow in Columbia, South Carolina, on Wednesday, July 29, with an F-16 flyover from the 169th Fighter Wing.

The succession is its own story. Gov. Henry McMaster, on Trump’s recommendation, appointed Graham’s sister, Darline Graham Nordone — since 2019 the state’s commissioner for the blind — to serve out the term, which ends in early January; she becomes the first woman to represent South Carolina in the Senate. The pick is a placeholder as the GOP scrambles to defend a fragile majority ahead of a competitive 2026 primary. Graham’s death also removes, as NBC and CNN framed it, the last Republican interventionist able to translate hawkish foreign policy into Trump’s “America First” White House — a vacuum with obvious stakes for the Iran and Ukraine wars now dominating the agenda.

The Left-Leaning Take

Left-of-center commentary offered a divided elegy: genuine credit for Graham’s late-career bipartisan streak and his McCain-inherited institutionalism, set against sharp criticism of the hawkish interventionism that helped underwrite the Iran war and of his arc from Trump critic to Trump enabler. The install-the-sister move drew particular unease — a Senate seat handed out on presidential say-so reads, to the left, as loyalty politics dressed as mourning. (No in-window signed column from a whitelisted left-leaning columnist was located — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

On the right the loss registers as strategic, not merely personal: Graham was the party’s most effective conduit for hawkish foreign policy into a non-interventionist White House, and CNN’s reporting quotes Republicans calling him “irreplaceable.” But his death exposes an unsettled foreign-policy identity — the ascendant restraint/“America First” wing is quietly unbothered that a leading war hawk’s voice is gone, an intra-right split the Iran war has sharpened. (No in-window whitelisted-columnist piece on Graham’s legacy was located — provisional.)

Trade · Courts & the 2026 Map

Trump concedes his new tariffs do “the same thing” the Supreme Court struck down — as fresh lawsuits land and the GOP goes on offense

+

Speaking Tuesday, July 28, President Trump acknowledged that the duties he reimposed last week are functionally identical to the “Liberation Day” tariffs the Supreme Court invalidated earlier this year: “It’s a shame that I have to go a harder way for the tariffs because the Supreme Court, in a very close decision, ruled against me … Now, I have other ways of doing the same thing, but it’s a more cumbersome way of doing it.” After the Section 122 stopgap lapsed July 24, the administration pivoted to Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, layering duties of 10% to 12.5% — justified on forced-labor-enforcement grounds — across an estimated $949 billion (about 28%) of annual imports. As legal trackers (SCOTUSblog) note, the new structure was chosen precisely to survive the judicial roadblock.

The move drew fire on two fronts. Two small-business suits — one for spice importer Burlap & Barrel and watch retailer Collective Horology, another led by toymaker Learning Resources — argue the levies illegally circumvent the high court’s ruling, with legal experts flagging the “major questions” doctrine. Politically, Republicans are counterattacking rather than retreating: the NRCC launched a digital ad campaign Tuesday hammering Democrats who opposed the tariffs, betting the fight breaks their way — even as an Economist/YouGov figure shows 58% of Americans now say Trump’s policies have left them financially worse off, up from 42% in March 2025.

The Left-Leaning Take

The left frames this as open defiance of the Supreme Court. Sen. Ron Wyden accused the administration of “ordering USTR to reconstruct his illegal global tariffs under the guise of addressing forced labor,” and left-leaning commentary casts the Section 301 pivot as a rule-of-law end-run that voters will still pay for at the register — the affordability politics of the morning now compounded by a constitutional one. (No in-window whitelisted left-leaning column on the pivot was located — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Institutional and libertarian conservatives remain the tariffs’ sharpest critics: Reason argues flatly that “Trump’s newest tariffs are likely illegal too,” and the WSJ editorial board and National Review have run against the policy on both economics and law. The pro-Trump counter is operational — White House spokesman Kush Desai calls Section 301 “a legally durable tool,” and the NRCC ad blitz shows the political apparatus treating tariffs as a winning wedge. The intra-right fight, plainly labeled, is over the tariffs themselves, not merely their optics. (No in-window whitelisted columnist located — provisional.)

White House · The Iran War

Trump and Netanyahu, meeting for the first time since they went to war, reaffirm an “ironclad” anti-nuclear pledge — but Trump waves off pressure to resume strikes

+

President Trump hosted Prime Minister Netanyahu at the White House on Tuesday, July 28 — their first meeting since the two launched their joint war on Iran on February 28, and the first since a June rupture in which Trump reportedly called Netanyahu “f---ing crazy.” The White House called the talks “positive and productive”; an Israeli official described “an excellent and comprehensive discussion … first and foremost Iran,” and said the leaders reaffirmed an “ironclad commitment” that Iran never obtain nuclear weapons, with attention to Iran’s alleged expansion of the “Pickaxe Mountain” enrichment complex. No final agreements emerged. Crucially, per CNN, Trump brushed off pressure from hawks to resume the airstrike campaign paused since July 24, keeping an off-ramp open. The agenda also covered the US-brokered Israel–Lebanon (Hezbollah) framework — with follow-on talks set for Rome on August 4–6 — and expanding the Abraham Accords.

On the Iranian side the mood is defiant: Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said Tehran will take “any action” to keep control of the Strait of Hormuz and has “no plans” to negotiate directly with Washington, even as mediators from Pakistan, Egypt and Qatar work to revive a memorandum on Hormuz shipping. The gap between “no talks” in Tehran and “close to a deal” in the mediators’ telling is the war’s central ambiguity, and Tuesday did little to close it. But Trump’s refusal to re-escalate, paired with the ironclad-but-vague nuclear language, suggests Washington is betting that a paused war plus back-channel pressure beats a resumed bombing campaign it has struggled to bring to a decisive end.

White House · Russia’s War on Ukraine

Zelensky leaves the Oval Office with a “good meeting” and a Patriot-interceptor production deal in view as Trump says “it’s time to end the war”

+

Ahead of the Netanyahu sit-down, Trump met Zelensky for roughly an hour in the Oval Office — closed to the press — and the Ukrainian leader called it a “good meeting.” On Telegram, Zelensky thanked Trump “for everything we do together to protect the lives of Ukrainians and achieve peace” and said they discussed “licenses for the production of interceptors for the Patriots” — building on Trump’s announcement at this month’s NATO summit in Ankara that the US would license Ukraine to manufacture Patriot air-defense systems, a long-standing Kyiv request. Talks also touched Ukraine producing more of its own weapons and, per reporting, further sanctions on Russia. A White House official framed the objective bluntly: “It’s time to end the war,” pointing to a Russia–Ukraine settlement track.

The warmth is a marked reversal from the leaders’ televised February 2025 Oval Office blow-up, and reflects a Kyiv that has blunted recent Russian advances while Washington leans toward a negotiated end. The concrete deliverable — domestic Patriot-interceptor production — matters more than the atmospherics: it would let Ukraine sustain its own air defense against the drone-and-missile barrages that again killed civilians this week, rather than depending on finite US transfers. The unresolved question is the settlement itself; “time to end the war” is an American priority Kyiv accepts only alongside the security guarantees Zelensky has made his “number-one priority.”

Gaza · Reconstruction

Israel’s cabinet clears a first international force into Gaza — a 200-strong “Board of Peace” pilot in Rafah

+

In a thread the morning brief did not cover, Israel’s security cabinet voted July 26 to approve the limited entry of an International Stabilization Force (ISF) into Gaza and the launch of a pilot humanitarian scheme under President Trump’s “Board of Peace” plan, beginning in the Tal as-Sultan district of Rafah in the south. The initial deployment is small — roughly 200 troops from “friendly countries,” with Morocco and Uganda named — tasked with establishing a controlled safe zone from which Israeli forces would withdraw, restoring basic services and providing portable housing for up to 50,000 displaced Palestinians who pass a strict screening for weapons or Hamas ties. Far-right National Security Minister Itamar Ben Gvir voted against; Foreign Minister Gideon Sa’ar voted in favor.

Analysts read the move as a “calculated manoeuvre” (Al Jazeera): large enough for Washington to claim the reconstruction phase of its Gaza plan has begun, small enough that Netanyahu concedes little control on the ground and keeps his coalition intact over Ben Gvir’s objection. Whether the pilot scales beyond a single Rafah district or stalls as a symbolic first step will turn on whether services and housing actually materialize — and whether the screening regime is seen as protection or as coerced relocation. It is the most concrete post-war governance move in Gaza to date, and it lands while the region’s attention is fixed on Iran.

Economy · 2026 Midterms

Trump takes his economy message to Michigan as fresh tariffs and $4 gas deepen open Republican midterm dread

+

President Trump traveled to suburban Detroit on Monday, July 27 — his third Michigan trip of the term — visiting a General Motors facility in Milford to defend his economic record in a swing state where his trade war has bitten. The backdrop: on July 23–24 the administration let stopgap levies lapse and imposed new duties of 10% to 12.5% on roughly 60 trading partners (Canada, Mexico, China and the EU among them), justified on forced-labor-enforcement grounds, plus an additional 50% tariff on some $20 billion of select Canadian goods — wine, hockey sticks, cement. The replacement duties are structured to skirt a February 2026 Supreme Court ruling that found the original “Liberation Day” tariffs unlawful. AAA put the national average for regular gas at about $4.09 a gallon as of July 23, up roughly 15 cents on the week and about 20% year-over-year, after a May peak near $4.55 tied to the Strait of Hormuz disruption.

The political conditions are punishing for the incumbent party. A Fox News poll found just 33% approve of Trump’s handling of the economy; a July 22 YouGov survey found 46% say the tariffs raised prices “a lot”; and an Economist/YouGov poll found 47% now call the economy “poor,” the highest since 2023, with the share saying Trump has left them financially worse off climbing to 58%. Republican unease is now on the record: Marc Short, Mike Pence’s former chief of staff, warned “this is going to be punishing for Republicans politically,” and Sen. Ted Cruz cautioned on his podcast that a tariff-driven recession would make 2026 “a bloodbath — a Democrat House, and you might even face a Democrat Senate.” With roughly 100 days to the vote, the convergence of new import duties and gas near $4 hands the opposition a unified cost-of-living line.

The Left-Leaning Take

Left-of-center opinion casts the tariffs as a self-inflicted, regressive price shock and a political gift to Democrats. An MSNBC/MaddowBlog piece argued the new levies “make things worse for Republicans ahead of the midterms,” surrendering the GOP’s traditional edge on the economy; Daily Kos called them “economy-destroying” and stressed they dodge a Supreme Court ruling; and a source told the Boston Globe Trump is on “a death march for the economy.” The common thread: affordability — gas, groceries, import costs — is now the defining midterm liability, and it is entirely self-authored. (A distinct in-window Vox, Slate, Guardian or Mother Jones column was not located — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Strikingly, the institutional conservative press is largely against the policy — the intra-right fight is over the tariffs themselves, not just their politics. The WSJ editorial board writes that “tariffs are an obsession for President Trump, much as climate was for the Biden Administration,” warning voters “know that tariffs are adding to their pain — Republicans could pay the price in November.” National Review has run a steady anti-tariff line (“Trump’s Canadian Tariffs Make No Sense,” July 22). The pro-Trump counter is mostly operational rather than editorial: Trump styles himself “the affordability president,” touts a tariff “windfall,” and predicts pricing decides 2026 in the GOP’s favor. (WSJ and NR pieces partly paywalled — quotes via The Hill/RawStory.)

National Security · The Iran War

As Trump prepares to host Netanyahu, Republican confidence in the war’s management cracks — Tillis says Hegseth has “lost my confidence”

+

The US airstrike campaign against Iran remained paused into Tuesday, July 28, after roughly 13 consecutive nights of bombing were abruptly suspended on July 24; Iran has held its counterstrikes in parallel. But the off-ramp is unsettled: Iran’s Foreign Ministry said as of July 27 there are “no talks underway with the US,” even as regional mediators describe active back-channel diplomacy centered on reviving a 14-point memorandum over Strait of Hormuz shipping. Prime Minister Netanyahu, in Washington for talks with Trump today, says he “fully” backs the pressure campaign. As Lawfare’s analysis has argued, the campaign has proceeded without the congressional authorization an international armed conflict requires.

The domestic politics of the war are turning against the administration. Sen. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.) said Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth “has lost my confidence,” and Sen. Susan Collins (R-Maine) called the purge of senior officers “deeply troubling” while demanding to know the Iran “endgame” — establishment-hawk anxiety about competence rather than anti-war sentiment. The Hill has reported a broader “crisis of confidence” in the Senate GOP conference as Hegseth presses Congress for roughly $350 billion in additional funding, and polling has found large majorities saying the conflict has proved harder than the administration expected. With about 100 days to the midterms, an unauthorized, open-ended war whose costs voters feel at the pump is precisely the fault line the opposition hopes to widen.

The Left-Leaning Take

The left reads the pause and the GOP wobble as confirmation the strikes were discretionary all along — a war of choice Americans broadly oppose while households absorb the cost. The recurring argument, echoed in an MSNBC opinion piece contending the bombing “may be legal” but is not thereby justified given targets that serve civilians, is that legality is not legitimacy, and that what restrains Trump is internal caution and political fear, not the limits Congress tried to impose. (Fresh in-window signed columns from Vox, Slate, Mother Jones or the Guardian on the pause itself were not located — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

This is largely an intra-right rupture. The doubters are Republican senators — Tillis and Collins on competence, and a restraint faction (reflected in National Review’s earlier “A War Restrained by the People” and dovetailing with reported Vance/Caine caution) warning an open-ended campaign is drifting toward the “forever war” Trump ran against. The pro-escalation majority counters that a pause reads as weakness and cites Tehran’s intransigence. Labeled as anti-Trump-right dissent, David Frum (Atlantic) has argued the war rests on “Trump’s whim.” (A dedicated in-window WSJ, Dispatch or Reason editorial on the pause was not located — provisional.)

Red Sea · Oil & Security

Houthi blockade keeps Brent above $100 as Saudi Arabia’s Red Sea export route stays under fire

+

Yemen’s Iran-aligned Houthis have opened a second chokepoint crisis alongside the disrupted Strait of Hormuz. On July 23 the group claimed missile and drone strikes on two Saudi tankers in the Red Sea, sending Brent crude up more than 7% to break above $100 a barrel for the first time since late May; on July 25 a strike set the Jizan Aramco refinery ablaze and again targeted Yanbu — the Red Sea outlet of the East–West (Petroline) pipeline, which became the kingdom’s main crude-export bypass once Hormuz traffic was choked off. The strategic significance is that both of Saudi Arabia’s principal export routes are now under simultaneous threat, which is what has held oil at triple digits rather than any single strike. Trump has said he will hold Iran responsible for further Houthi attacks. Technology · Artificial Intelligence

Moonshot AI releases Kimi K3’s open weights — the largest open model yet, and it’s topping frontier leaderboards

+

On Monday, July 27, Beijing-based Moonshot AI released the full downloadable weights of Kimi K3, a 2.8-trillion-parameter sparse mixture-of-experts model — now the largest open-weight AI system publicly available. It activates only 16 of 896 experts per token, uses the company’s new Kimi Delta Attention architecture, natively handles text, images and video, and offers a one-million-token context window. MXFP4 quantization brings the download to roughly 1.4 terabytes, and self-hosting still requires on the order of 64 Nvidia H100 or B200 GPUs across eight servers; API pricing runs about $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens, well below comparable US frontier models.

On third-party leaderboards — vendor and independent benchmarks, not formal audits — K3 is reported at #2 on the Vals AI index, #3 on Artificial Analysis’s Intelligence Index (behind only Claude Fable and GPT-5.6), and #1 on LMArena’s Frontend Code Arena at 1,679 points, ahead of Claude Fable 5 and GPT-5.6. The drop landed the same week as DeepSeek V4’s stable release (July 24), making late July an unusually dense stretch for Chinese open-weight launches and fresh evidence that Chinese labs are competing at the frontier despite US compute-export limits. Some coverage flags undisclosed hallucination-risk data in the technical report; as ever, launch-window benchmark figures warrant caution.

Sources: Bloomberg (partial — paywalled), Tom’s Hardware, Scientific American, Interconnects
Energy · Electrification & Storage

Australia’s largest solar-plus-battery hybrid locks in AU$1.3 billion as Ark Energy greenlights Richmond Valley

+

Ark Energy — the Australian arm of South Korea’s Korea Zinc — has reached a final investment decision on its Richmond Valley solar-plus-storage project in northern New South Wales, with the energy trade press clustering coverage on July 27 (the parent board approved the move at an extraordinary meeting in Seoul on July 21). The AU$1.3 billion (about US$908 million) package comprises AU$586 million in equity and AU$716 million in debt. Its priority stage pairs a 200-megawatt solar plant with a co-located 275MW / 2,200MWh lithium-iron-phosphate battery — described as Australia’s biggest solar-battery hybrid — sited about 25 km south of Casino in the Northern Rivers region.

It is the first build-to-own development in Ark Energy’s portfolio to reach a final investment decision. Financial close is targeted for September 2026, construction is expected to begin in October, and operations are slated for January 2029; separately, Ark added a 150MW solar project to Australia’s environmental-approval queue. RenewEconomy framed the financing as a “strong endorsement” of large-scale hybrid solar-storage economics — a concrete data point on how utility-scale storage is increasingly bankable on its own merits.

France and Spain battle their worst wildfires “in living memory” as 300,000+ flee and a fourth heatwave bears down

+

Science · Biomedicine

A new cancer vaccine hijacks COVID-19 immune memory to attack tumors in preclinical tests

+

In a study published in Nature Communications on July 27, researchers from Celloram, University Hospitals and Case Western Reserve University unveiled PROTEXI, a dendritic-cell vaccine platform that repurposes the pre-existing antiviral immune memory — from SARS-CoV-2 infection and vaccination — that billions of people already carry, and redirects it against cancer. Rather than building tumor-specific responses from scratch, PROTEXI leverages existing antiviral CD4+ helper T-cell immunity to boost tumor-specific CD8+ “killer” T-cell responses and generate durable antitumor memory.

In preclinical melanoma and breast-cancer models, the vaccine reduced tumor burden, enhanced the effectiveness of other immunotherapies, and produced long-lasting immune memory. The team says it plans first-in-human trials of the personalized vaccine in sarcoma patients. The findings are preclinical — there is no human efficacy data yet — but the strategy of borrowing a widespread, ready-made immune response is a notable twist on cancer-vaccine design.
