Election Politics · The Address

Trump says his Thursday primetime address will be about “free and fair elections,” teasing declassified intelligence and “really, really big news”

+

Asked Tuesday, July 14 about the “Speech to the Nation” he has scheduled for 9 p.m. ET Thursday, July 16, President Trump said it would center on “free and fair elections” and “other things,” calling it “really, really big news” and warning that “our country has to shape up.” Reuters reported the address is expected to unveil newly declassified intelligence tied to investigations into U.S. elections and what the White House casts as security risks in voting machines; aides say Trump is also likely to address the renewed war with Iran.

The speech lands atop a months-long election push. Trump has pressed Congress to pass the SAVE America Act — which would require documentary proof of citizenship and photo ID to vote in federal elections and sharply curtail mail voting — and has urged Senate Republicans to abolish the filibuster to pass it on a simple majority. His March executive order asserting federal control over voter rolls and mail ballots has been blocked in court, and the Supreme Court recently declined to disturb mail-in voting. Local election officials across the country, most of them Republicans, have called the SAVE Act unworkable, and the fight has stalled other business on Capitol Hill.

The Left-Leaning Take

No single whitelisted liberal column on the announced speech surfaced in-window; this is standing framing. Progressive outlets read a primetime address built on “declassified intelligence” about voting machines as election denialism dressed up as national security — a bid to pre-delegitimize the midterms and to sell the SAVE Act, which critics (Democracy Now among them) call “Trump’s sweeping voter-suppression bill.” They stress that courts have already blocked his voter-roll order and that Republican election officials themselves say the citizenship-proof mandate would sow chaos.

The Right-Leaning Take

Mainstream-right enthusiasm centers on the substance, not the stagecraft: the SAVE Act’s proof-of-citizenship and voter-ID requirements drew loud cheers at the America 250 celebration, and supporters frame them as basic election-integrity guardrails ahead of the midterms. But this is where the right splits. Anti-Trump conservatives at The Bulwark — an intra-right dissent, not the mainstream position — warn that commandeering primetime to roll out “declassified” claims about voting machines is exactly the machinery of 2020-style election denial, and that abolishing the filibuster to force it through would be a self-inflicted institutional wound.

Sources: CNN, TIME, Forbes, UPI, Spokesman-Review
The Supreme Court · Ethics & Security

In rare testimony, Barrett recounts a swatting attempt and Kagan backs an enforceable ethics code — but the two split on how to police the Court

+

Justices Amy Coney Barrett and Elena Kagan — the first justices to face Congress since 2019 — testified Tuesday, July 14, before House and then Senate appropriators on the Court’s fiscal-2027 budget. The Court is seeking $228.4 million, up nearly 10% from the $207.8 million appropriated for 2026, largely to bolster security. Barrett made an unusually personal plea, recounting a recent swatting call at her home and disclosing that Court security had issued her a bulletproof vest; Kagan told lawmakers threats against the justices are on track to rise by more than a third this year.

The sharper exchange came on ethics. Kagan said she would support the Court’s 2023 code being paired with “some kind of enforcement mechanism,” while calling the design “an extremely difficult question” because “you wouldn’t want an enforcement system that is controlled by the executive branch or by the legislature.” Barrett was cooler — “I’m less certain,” she said, while insisting she is “fully committed to the code” — leaving daylight between the two on the central accountability question. Rep. Rosa DeLauro pressed the point: “It’s entirely self-policing … every other body has that. It’s just the Supreme Court that doesn’t.”

The Left-Leaning Take

No dedicated in-window whitelisted liberal column surfaced; this is standing framing. Liberal critics treated Kagan’s opening as vindication — a sitting justice conceding the current self-policing regime is inadequate — and argued Congress should condition any security increase on a binding, independent enforcement mechanism rather than accept another round of voluntary promises after the luxury-travel disclosures. Barrett’s “less certain” hedge, in this reading, is the whole problem.

The Right-Leaning Take

Security funding itself draws broad conservative and bipartisan support given the documented, escalating threats against judges. On ethics, the mainstream right echoes Kagan’s separation-of-powers caution — an enforcement panel controlled by Congress or the executive would compromise judicial independence — and casts Barrett’s wariness as principled, not evasive. A quieter intra-right current still eyes Barrett warily after her votes against the administration on tariffs and birthright citizenship this term. No dedicated NR/Dispatch/WSJ editorial on the hearing surfaced in-window.

The Iran War · Blockade

The U.S. naval blockade of Iran takes effect as strikes enter a fourth day — and Trump threatens to hit Iranian power plants and bridges “next week”

+

The U.S. blockade of vessels transiting to and from Iranian ports formally took effect at 20:00 GMT (4 p.m. ET) Tuesday, July 14, as CENTCOM carried out a fourth consecutive day of strikes — Iranian state media reported explosions in Bandar Abbas and near Sirik — saying the aim is to degrade Iran’s ability to attack commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz. In a notable escalation earlier reported by CENTCOM, the U.S. mounted its first-ever offensive maritime operation using unmanned surface drones against an Iranian naval installation. Trump publicly threatened to strike Iranian power plants and bridges “next week” and said a Russia sanctions package could soon be expanded to cover Iran and Hezbollah.

Iran kept up its retaliation in waves through Tuesday: the IRGC said it struck two “non-compliant” UAE-flagged supertankers in the strait and triggered fresh missile alerts in Bahrain and Kuwait. The standoff has unraveled last month’s interim U.S.–Iran agreement, which was to reopen the strait and pause hostilities for 60 days of talks; Trump formally terminated the ceasefire last week. With roughly a fifth of the world’s energy supply normally moving through Hormuz — and Washington still demanding a 20% “toll” the UN’s maritime body says has no legal basis — the deadlock is lifting fuel prices and snarling global trade. Trump’s Thursday primetime address is expected to touch the war.

Sources: Al Jazeera, NPR, CNBC, CNN
Iran · Intrigue

Iran’s Revolutionary Guard detains former president Ahmadinejad over an alleged Mossad plot to install him as leader

+

Israeli reporting published July 13 says former Iranian president Mahmoud Ahmadinejad is in the custody of the IRGC’s intelligence wing — described variously as house arrest — after Iranian authorities uncovered a large portion of his contacts with Israel. According to accounts in Haaretz and the Times of Israel, a Mossad operation begun around 2022 sought to recruit Ahmadinejad as an asset and, in a later phase overseen personally by then-Mossad chief David Barnea, to install him as Iran’s leader after a hoped-for collapse of the regime. Barnea is said to have met Ahmadinejad in Budapest, with a second meeting in June 2025, days before Israel launched the opening strikes of its 12-day campaign.

The claims are extraordinary and rest, so far, on Israeli-sourced reporting citing anonymous officials; Iran has not confirmed them and the specifics cannot be independently verified. If borne out, the episode would signal both the depth of Israeli penetration of the Iranian elite and the degree of paranoia now gripping the leadership as the war with the United States grinds on — and it recasts Ahmadinejad’s low-profile reappearance at Ali Khamenei’s funeral in a starker light. Treat as developing and single-sourced.

Sources: Times of Israel, Haaretz, Jerusalem Post
Gaza · Reconstruction

The EU rallies 65 governments to pledge about $1 billion for a Gaza recovery fund — a sliver of the $70 billion the rebuild will cost

+

The European Union announced July 13 that it had marshaled roughly €900 million (about $1 billion) in pledges for a Gaza recovery fund, drawing commitments from some 65 governments and organizations including the United States and the United Nations. European Commissioner for the Mediterranean Dubravka Šuica unveiled the fund after a Brussels meeting of the Palestine Donors Group. It is a rare concrete step toward reconstruction — but a modest one against the scale of the need.

The UN, World Bank and EU estimate Gaza’s rebuild will cost around $70 billion, with more than 60 million tons of rubble — enough to fill nearly 3,000 container ships — that will take upward of seven years to clear before demining even begins. And the money runs into a hard political ceiling: the Israel–Hamas ceasefire that took hold in October is effectively stalled, leaving open when reconstruction could begin and how much of the pledged sum will actually be delivered.

Sources: AP / Philadelphia Inquirer, AP / KSAT, AP / Korea Herald

The Iran War · Escalation

U.S. strikes Iran for a third straight night and restarts the Hormuz blockade with a 20% cargo “toll” as Iran hits Gulf tankers

+

U.S. Central Command carried out a third consecutive night of strikes on Iran into Tuesday, July 14, hitting targets across a wide arc — Bushehr, Chabahar, Jask, Konarak, Abu Musa and Bandar Abbas; Iran’s IRNA said four sites in Bushehr province were struck around midday local time. CENTCOM said the U.S. naval blockade of shipping to and from Iranian ports officially resumes Tuesday at 4 p.m. ET. President Trump, styling the U.S. the “Guardian of the Hormuz Strait,” says Washington will be “reimbursed” 20% on all cargo transiting the waterway — a demand the UN’s International Maritime Organization says has “no legal basis.” Trump has scheduled a primetime address for Thursday at 9 p.m. EDT.

Iran struck two UAE-flagged tankers with cruise missiles, killing at least one crew member; India summoned Iran’s deputy ambassador after an Indian sailor was among the casualties. Tehran also claimed retaliatory missile and drone strikes on U.S. bases in Kuwait, Bahrain and Jordan — the IRGC named Bahrain’s Juffair base — but those Iranian claims are not independently confirmed. Markets moved on the risk: Brent rose roughly 2% to about $85 a barrel and WTI about 2.3% to near $80. Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, pushed back publicly that “20% is of course too much.”

Liberal commentary frames the toll as naked extortion, not strategy. A Slate piece argues the blockade-plus-fee scheme “looks more than a little corrupt,” casting it as part of a Trump pattern of monetizing U.S. power — noting Hormuz transit “used to be free.” Critics quoted across wire coverage call it “economic extortion” and “state-backed highway robbery” that violates freedom-of-navigation norms, and question whether Washington can even guarantee the safe passage it proposes to charge for.

Market-oriented and libertarian (Reason-style) voices, echoed by tanker-industry executives, call a 20% levy unworkable and legally dubious. The Graham Seat · Succession

Darline Graham Nordone, Lindsey Graham’s sister, is set to be sworn in Tuesday as South Carolina’s first woman U.S. senator

+

South Carolina Gov. Henry McMaster on Monday, July 13, appointed Darline Graham Nordone, 62 — the sister of the late Sen. Lindsey Graham — to serve out the remainder of his term, which ends in early January. Senate Majority Leader John Thune said she will be sworn in Tuesday afternoon at 2:30 p.m. ET, making her the first woman to represent South Carolina in the U.S. Senate. Nordone has never held elected office; she is a commissioner on the South Carolina Commission for the Blind and has long worked with people with disabilities. Graham, who died over the weekend, had been her legal guardian after their parents died when he was 22 and she 13.

Trump publicly recommended her to McMaster on Truth Social as “a fabulous tribute to Lindsey,” reportedly passing over Rep. Nancy Mace, who had sought the seat; Sen. Tim Scott and Thune endorsed the pick. “Lindsey has always been there for me, and now I will be there for him,” Nordone said. A special primary to choose nominees for the November contest is set for Aug. 11, so the appointment settles only the caretaker seat.

The Left-Leaning Take

No dedicated in-window left column on the appointment surfaced — this is standing framing, not an invented piece. Liberal and good-government critics cast the pick as raw nepotism and dynastic entitlement (“a Senate seat is a public trust, not a family keepsake”), stressing that Nordone has no governing record and that Trump personally engineered the choice. The “nepotism is alive and well” line was widely amplified.

The Right-Leaning Take

Republican officials present the appointment as a fitting tribute to Graham and a story of family loyalty, emphasizing Nordone’s decades of service to disabled South Carolinians. Scott called her “a fantastic pick” who best understands “Lindsey’s love for family, our state, and our country.” An intra-GOP subplot runs beneath the tributes: Trump’s steer toward Nordone sidelined Nancy Mace, a marker of the president’s grip on the seat ahead of the Aug. 11 primary. Ukraine · War & Diplomacy

Ten nations launch an anti-ballistic-missile coalition in Paris — hours before Russia pounds Kyiv with a barrage of ballistic missiles

+

At a July 13 “Coalition of the Willing” summit in Paris (roughly 25 leaders), Ukraine plus Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden and the UK announced an “Integrated Anti-Ballistic Missile Coalition” to counter Russia’s missile threat — centered on Ukraine’s “Freyja” project, a European-backed, lower-cost alternative to the U.S. Patriot that backers say could be operational within about 12 months. Overnight into July 14, Russia struck Kyiv with an estimated six to eight ballistic missiles beginning around 00:16. Tolls diverge: Ukraine’s State Emergency Service cited 19 killed (including a child) and 61 injured in the capital, while the Kyiv Post reported up to 27 dead across the city and region. Zelensky renewed his push for interceptors — “Patriot missiles shouldn’t stay in warehouses.” Separately, Russia summoned Germany’s ambassador on July 14 over Berlin’s expanding military support. The coalition reflects an allied pivot toward Europe-made air defense as U.S.-supplied interceptor stocks run thin.

China · Pacific

Beijing’s embassy in Kiribati accuses critics of “double standards” as fallout from its South Pacific missile test deepens

+

On July 14 the Chinese Embassy in Kiribati publicly pushed back at regional criticism of Beijing’s July 6 submarine-launched ballistic missile test — reportedly a JL-series SLBM flown roughly 7,300 km from the South China Sea into the South Pacific carrying a simulated warhead. The embassy called the test “routine” and no threat, arguing other powers (the U.S., UK, France, Russia, India) have run “hundreds” of Pacific tests while “China has only conducted 3 strategic missile tests since 1949 in the Pacific,” and decrying “hypocrisy” from states acquiring nuclear-powered submarines — a jab at AUKUS.

The rebuttal answered a chorus of Pacific leaders: Palau’s president warned the launch fueled “nuclear anxiety,” New Zealand’s prime minister called it “incredibly unwelcome,” and Australia labeled it “destabilising.” An alliance of Pacific peace groups urged island leaders to oppose missile tests and military exercises in the region. The exchange sharpens a China–Pacific contest over militarization, following the Australia–Fiji “Ocean of Peace” pact. Note: outlets differ on whether the missile was a JL-2 or JL-3.

Sudan · Sanctions

EU foreign ministers ban imports of Sudanese gold to choke war financing — but decline to blacklist the UAE-backed RSF

+

On July 13 EU foreign ministers approved a ban on the purchase, import or transfer of gold originating in Sudan, plus a ban on exporting mercury and cyanide to the country — a Dutch-French initiative aimed at cutting revenue that fuels the civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces (Gen. Abdel Fattah al-Burhan) and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (Mohamed Hamdan “Hemedti” Dagalo), fighting since April 2023. Sudan is one of Africa’s largest gold producers, and analysts estimate 50–70% of its gold is smuggled out annually, financing both sides.

Notably, the package did not add the RSF to the EU terrorism list or impose new RSF-specific measures, despite MEPs passing a resolution the prior week urging exactly that — a gap critics tie to the RSF’s UAE backing. The move follows recent Amnesty International and UN findings of RSF crimes against humanity and a genocidal campaign in El-Fasher. It marks a rare concrete EU economic lever on a war often overshadowed by Ukraine and the Middle East.

SK Hynix raises a record $26.5 billion in the largest-ever U.S. listing by a foreign company to expand AI-memory output

+

South Korean memory giant SK Hynix priced and debuted its Nasdaq offering the week of July 10, raising roughly $26.5 billion — the biggest foreign IPO in U.S. history and among the largest U.S. share sales ever. The listing is a marker of how completely the AI build-out now drives capital markets, with memory suppliers riding the same wave as the accelerator designers they feed.

Climate · Extreme Weather

A rapid analysis attributes more than 10,000 excess deaths in Europe’s late-June heatwaves — 42% of them to climate change

+

An analysis released around July 12–13 estimates that European countries suffered more than 10,000 excess deaths during the extreme heatwaves that struck western Europe in late June 2026, with over 9,000 of the dead aged 65 and older. Researchers from Imperial College London, the UK Met Office and the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine combined weather data, climate models and mortality studies and concluded that 42% of the deaths were attributable to the additional heat that human-caused warming added to the event.

Country tallies include roughly 5,120 deaths in Germany, 2,025 in France, 1,747 in Belgium, 1,028 in Spain and about 480 in the Netherlands, drawing on EuroMOMO mortality monitoring (backed by the ECDC and WHO). Separately, the World Weather Attribution group said the heatwaves would have been “virtually impossible” without climate change; England set monthly records of 35.1°C in May and 37.7°C in June. The figures underscore how heat — often an under-counted killer — is now the deadliest face of a warming climate in Europe.

Sources: Euronews, CBC, US News / Reuters, LSHTM
Health · Neuroscience

A 214,000-person study across 14 countries finds dementia risk factors vary so widely that one-size-fits-all prevention fails

+

A USC-led study of more than 214,000 older adults across 14 countries and regions finds that the most common modifiable dementia risk factors — low education, high blood pressure, smoking, obesity, hearing loss — differ dramatically by country, so prevention programs designed for wealthy Western nations are “structurally mismatched” elsewhere. Low education affects 86% of older adults in China versus 12% in the United States, while high BMI is common in 45% of Americans but just 13% of Indians. Across populations, hearing loss emerged as the single highest-yield modifiable target.

The work was published in The Lancet Healthy Longevity and presented at the Alzheimer’s Association International Conference (AAIC) 2026 in London around July 12–13. Because most dementia-prevention evidence comes from high-income countries, the authors argue national strategies must be tailored to local risk profiles rather than copied wholesale — a shift with real stakes as dementia cases climb fastest in low- and middle-income nations.

Sources: EurekAlert (USC), USC Today, ScienceDaily, Medical Xpress
Energy · Electrification

Global EV sales hit a record 2 million in June as Europe surges and the U.S. slips further behind

+

Global sales of fully electric and plug-in hybrid vehicles reached a record 2.0 million units in June 2026, lifting year-to-date sales to 9.6 million — up 7% year-on-year and 11% month-on-month — according to Benchmark Mineral Intelligence (which acquired Rho Motion in 2024). Europe was the engine, with about 530,000 EVs sold, up 31% year-on-year and its strongest month on record.

North America moved the opposite way: roughly 130,000 units in June, down 13% from a year earlier, with first-half sales down 20% to 730,000 as the pull-forward from expiring U.S. incentives faded. China’s domestic market softened (June sales down 11% year-on-year), prompting Chinese automakers to ramp exports to a monthly record of nearly 500,000 new-energy vehicles. The data underscore a widening gap between accelerating electrification abroad and a stalling U.S. market. (Reported July 9, marginally outside the tight window, but a genuine on-remit development.)
