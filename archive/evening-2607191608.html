Civil Liberties · New This Evening

Rubio and Miller unveil an “unprecedented global offensive” against the “radical left,” expanding the NSPM-7 crackdown with new terror designations, visa bans and an “Antifa summit” of 65 nations

+

The administration escalated its domestic-terrorism campaign this week into a global one. At a State Department “Ministerial on the Resurgence of Political Terrorism” — informally the “Antifa summit” — drawing representatives from more than 65 countries, Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced the U.S. would soon designate additional left-wing groups as terrorist organizations and imposed new visa restrictions on what State called members of “Far-Left Terrorist and other aligned groups.” Deputy chief of staff Stephen Miller, calling left-wing violence a “fatal cancer to civilization,” said the whole-of-government effort would “disrupt, identify, defund, debank, arrest and prosecute” what he termed political terrorists operating inside the country, and the White House framed the gathering as the start of an “unprecedented global offensive.”

The engine is National Security Presidential Memorandum 7 (NSPM-7), signed September 25, 2025 and titled “Countering Domestic Terrorism and Organized Political Violence.” It directs law-enforcement and intelligence agencies — including the FBI (which has stood up a dedicated NSPM-7 mission center), DOJ counterterrorism task forces, and a Treasury/IRS effort probing nonprofits — to investigate and financially choke networks accused of fomenting political violence, listing “anti-capitalist,” “anti-Christian” and “anti-American” views among indicators. Because it is a presidential memorandum, it carries no congressional authorization; the ACLU, the Brennan Center and 31 members of Congress have warned it risks targeting protected dissent, protest and speech.

The Left-Leaning Take

Progressive outlets (Mother Jones, Democracy Now!, Vox) read this as the machinery of authoritarianism switched on in the open: a directive that treats “anti-capitalist” or “anti-Christian” belief as a terrorism tripwire, then deputizes the FBI, IRS, Treasury and CIA to defund, debank and arrest the nonprofits and protesters who hold it. Rubio’s visa bans and 65-nation “Antifa summit,” on this view, export the crackdown abroad and dress the criminalization of dissent in the language of counterterrorism — all without a vote in Congress.

The Right-Leaning Take

The administration and allied media cast NSPM-7 as an overdue, whole-of-government answer to genuine, lethal left-wing political violence. But the fault line runs through the right: libertarian Reason has consistently warned that debanking and IRS-driven “defunding” are precisely the tools most ripe for abuse, and constitutionalist conservatives argue the test is whether agencies stay fixed on actual violent plots and material support rather than drifting into punishing peaceful dissent. The anti-Trump right (e.g., The Bulwark) frames the memo as authoritarian overreach — an intra-right objection, not the mainstream-right position. Elections · New Overnight

A federal appeals court hands Trump a partial win on mail voting, reviving the USPS voter-list rule — even as a separate injunction still blocks the order in two dozen states

+

A three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit late Friday, July 17, stayed a district-court injunction and cleared the U.S. Postal Service to move ahead with a proposed election-mail rule that would require states to hand over lists of voters who requested mail or absentee ballots — plus serialized ballot barcodes — before USPS transmits federal ballots. The panel found USPS had made a strong showing it is likely to win on the merits: that the NAACP’s challenge is not yet ripe, and that the rule likely would not violate an earlier settlement even if finalized. The judges also credited the agency’s argument that leaving the injunction in place would prevent it from issuing a final rule before the November 2026 general election. The order pauses the block imposed by U.S. District Judge Emmet Sullivan.

The victory is only partial. A separate injunction by U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani in Massachusetts — blocking the underlying March executive order in 23 states plus D.C. on the ground that it infringes states’ constitutional authority over elections — remains in force, so USPS cannot implement the rule in those jurisdictions regardless of Friday’s ruling. The decision lands atop a turbulent week: Trump’s Thursday, July 16 primetime claim that China illicitly obtained 220 million U.S. voter files (a figure fact-checkers note describes largely public data, with no evidence votes were altered), DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin’s Friday threat of fines and “prison time” for state officials who won’t run rolls through a citizenship-verification database, and the DOJ’s pending suits demanding unredacted voter lists.

The Left-Leaning Take

Democracy Docket and progressive voting advocates cast the ruling as a procedural opening that lets the administration gum up ballot delivery ahead of the midterms — a partial reprieve for an order they see as an unconstitutional federal takeover of elections built on invented fraud. Mother Jones’ voting correspondent Ari Berman calls the wider campaign “completely unprecedented” groundwork for midterm interference, noting the SAVE Act’s demand for a passport or birth certificate to register would trip up roughly half of Americans who lack passports. The through-line: Mullin’s prison threats and the roll-demand suits amount to coercion of the nonpartisan officials who run elections.

The Right-Leaning Take

Conservative outlets (RedState, Townhall, the Gateway Pundit) hailed the stay as a “huge election-integrity win,” restoring the Postal Service’s ability to verify who receives a ballot. But the intra-right split persists: the Wall Street Journal editorial board has called Trump’s SAVE Act fixation a “political backfire” that reaches well beyond popular voter-ID and would render most driver’s licenses insufficient to register, while Reason (libertarian) warns the federal roll-grab and USPS mandate are exactly the centralization conservatives claim to oppose. Midterms · Polling

A new Washington Post–Ipsos poll finds Democrats clinging to a slim House lead as their spring advantage narrows — but an enthusiasm gap and an economy-first electorate cut their way

+

With the midterms roughly four months off, a Washington Post–Ipsos poll released July 18 (fielded July 8–13, 2,648 respondents) shows Democrats leading Republicans 48% to 45% on the generic House ballot among registered voters — a narrowing from their five-point edge in May. The margin widens to 53% to 45% among voters “absolutely certain to vote,” an enthusiasm advantage analysts see as pivotal. Because Republican-favorable redistricting is in effect this cycle, estimates suggest Democrats must win the popular vote by about three to four points to flip the chamber — roughly the margin the poll shows.

The economy dominates: 54% of registered voters name the economy and high prices as the most important factor in their vote, ahead of immigration (28%), the Iran/Israel conflict and foreign policy (20%), honesty and corruption (17%), and health care and abortion (15%). Coverage diverged on the takeaway — some outlets led with Republicans gaining ground since spring, others with Democrats’ turnout edge — underscoring how tight and unsettled the race remains.

The Left-Leaning Take

The optimistic progressive read: the enthusiasm gap is the whole ballgame, and an electorate furious about prices — layered on top of Trump’s election-fraud gambit and an unpopular foreign war — is exactly the environment that drives base turnout and delivers the three-to-four-point win Democrats need. The narrowing since May is a warning to keep the focus on affordability, not a sign of trouble.

The Right-Leaning Take

The read from the right: the map, not the topline, decides this, and redistricting means a nominal Democratic lead of a few points may not be enough. That the economy tops every other issue is the opening — and, echoing the Wall Street Journal’s editorial advice, a reminder that Republicans do better campaigning on prices and the future than relitigating past elections.

Tech · Quantum Computing

IBM, Oak Ridge and the Cleveland Clinic run the first-known quantum simulation of a fusion-fuel material — cracking chemistry that had defeated classical supercomputers

+

A team from Oak Ridge National Laboratory, the Cleveland Clinic and IBM has reported the first-known computations of a fusion-reactor material on a quantum computer — calculating nine molecular configurations of FLiBe, a molten salt of fluorine, lithium and beryllium proposed as a “blanket” around fusion plasma to breed tritium, the scarce hydrogen isotope needed to sustain the reaction. Announced July 6 and posted to arXiv, the work matched the accuracy of the most demanding classical methods for chemistry that had been effectively out of reach for conventional supercomputers.

Tritium supply is one of fusion’s central bottlenecks: reactors must breed their own fuel, and modeling how it is produced and extracted from molten salt has stubbornly resisted simulation. The collaboration frames this as a step toward designing self-sufficient reactors, and says the next goals are speeding data transfer between quantum and classical hardware and scaling to larger molecular interactions. It is a concrete example of quantum computing beginning to earn its keep on a real materials-science problem rather than a benchmark.

Energy · Electricity

Solar became the EU’s single biggest source of power in June, supplying a record quarter of the bloc’s electricity

+

Solar generated a record 25% of the European Union’s electricity in June — about 52 terawatt-hours — making it the bloc’s largest single power source for only the third month ever, according to analysis from the energy think-tank Ember published July 14. The figure tops the previous record of 47 TWh (23%) set just a month earlier, and it caps a run in which solar has grown more than 20% a year since 2021, the fastest-expanding source in the EU, with installed capacity now beyond 400 gigawatts after a 65-GW build-out in 2025 alone.

The national numbers are striking: solar supplied more than a third of power in Spain (34%) and Germany (36%) in June, and nearly a quarter in Poland (24%). The milestone doubles as a reminder of the grid’s new balancing challenge — midday solar surpluses and evening ramps — that is driving Europe’s parallel scramble for batteries and flexible demand, even as the same heat now straining the continent lifts both generation and air-conditioning load.

Sources: Ember, Euronews, Electrek, PV Tech
Science · Biomedicine

A Cambridge lab tests 39 sweeteners against gut bacteria and finds most can reshape the microbiome — sometimes dramatically when mixed with drugs

+

Researchers at the University of Cambridge grew 25 species of gut bacteria — beneficial, neutral and potentially harmful — and exposed each to 39 commercial artificial and low-calorie sweeteners. About three-quarters of the sweeteners affected the growth of at least one species, in some cases slowing or halting bacteria associated with a healthy gut. The study, published July 16 in Molecular Systems Biology, is a controlled look at direct, dose-dependent effects rather than a claim about diet outcomes in people.

The more surprising findings came from combinations. The team mapped more than 100 interactions in which a sweetener’s effect shifted when paired with caffeine, flavorings or medicines — strengthening it in 34 cases and weakening it in 68. In one, the sweetener isosteviol combined with the antidepressant duloxetine sharply impaired two bacteria tied to blood-sugar regulation and gut health, a hint that the interplay between everyday additives, drugs and the microbiome is more complex than either alone.

Sources: ScienceDaily, Medical Xpress, News-Medical
Science · Genetics

A genomic study rewrites the koala’s story: its first great collapse came about 100,000 years ago — driven by climate, long before humans arrived

+

By calculating the koala’s mutation rate for the first time and analyzing hundreds of genomes, researchers at the University of Sydney and Texas A&M have concluded that the species suffered a dramatic population decline beginning roughly 100,000 years ago — tens of thousands of years before people reached Australia — reaching a critical genetic bottleneck around 60,000 years ago. Drying conditions split western koalas from eastern ones, and the evidence suggests only a remnant eastern population survived to give rise to today’s five east-coast groups.

The work, published in Molecular Biology and Evolution and widely reported this week, reframes a conservation debate: the koala’s deep vulnerability was set by ancient glacial-cycle climate shifts rather than by early human activity. That deep history — a species that squeezed through a genetic bottleneck and is now low in diversity — sharpens the stakes as modern habitat loss, disease and heat press on the animals again.

Sources: ScienceDaily, University of Sydney, Phys.org
