The Shutdown · Day Two, Evening

Fourth ‘no’ of the week: the Senate again fails to reopen the government, and now the fight is over back pay

+

The stalemate hardened into the weekend. On Friday afternoon the Senate failed for the fourth time to advance the House-passed stopgap, 54–44 — well short of the 60 needed to break a filibuster. Minority Leader Chuck Schumer held his caucus almost intact; only Democrats Catherine Cortez Masto and John Fetterman, plus independent Angus King, crossed over, while Republican Rand Paul voted no from the other direction. Majority Leader John Thune announced the chamber would vote yet again on Monday, part of a strategy to keep Democrats repeatedly on the record against funding; a handful of senators in both parties have begun quiet side conversations about the expiring ACA subsidies that are the core of the impasse.

A new front opened over money owed. The administration signalled that federal employees may not automatically be entitled to back pay once the lapse ends — the Office of Personnel Management quietly stripped citations of the 2019 Federal Employee Fair Treatment Act from its shutdown guidance, arguing Congress must affirmatively authorize the payments in any deal that reopens the government. Unions, who won Thursday’s temporary restraining order halting the shutdown RIFs, called the back-pay position a fresh attempt to use workers as leverage; the roughly 750,000 furloughed and the many more working unpaid now face a second weekend without a paycheck in sight. (Fast-moving; the back-pay question is contested and would ultimately be settled by the reopening legislation.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads the back-pay maneuver as the mask slipping: first permanent firings during the lapse, now a threat to stiff workers on wages they are owed, all to pressure the minority into abandoning health subsidies for millions. Thune’s repeat votes, they argue, are theater — the real negotiation only starts when Republicans stop pretending the ACA cliff isn’t their problem too. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right says the math is simple: the House passed a clean stopgap, and Democrats have now voted four times to keep the government closed over an unrelated health-care demand. The WSJ editorial line is to reopen first and debate subsidies on their own merits. Libertarians (Reason) note a shutdown is a poor tool for either side. Anti-Trump conservatives at The Bulwark warn that dangling back pay to punish civil servants is the kind of norm-breaking that outlasts this fight — an intra-right dissent, not the party line. (Directional.)

Courts · Troops in American Cities

To the Supreme Court: after losing in Chicago, the administration asks the justices to clear the way for Guard troops

+

The morning’s appeals-court setback moved up the ladder. The administration filed an emergency application at the Supreme Court asking the justices to let it immediately deploy National Guard troops in the Chicago area — the first time the high court has been drawn into the president’s drive to put soldiers on the streets of U.S. cities over their governors’ objections. The filing follows the lower courts’ findings that the deployments were “likely” unconstitutional and that “political opposition is not rebellion.”

The legal map remains tangled. In the parallel Portland case, the Ninth Circuit voted to rehear the dispute before an 11-judge en banc panel, a step that keeps troops out of that city for now even after a three-judge panel had sided with the administration. Illinois officials, who kept roughly 200 federalized Texas Guard members barred from deployment, urged the justices to stay out. A ruling from the Supreme Court — even a brief, unsigned order on the emergency docket — could set the terms for every pending Guard fight at once. (Active litigation; the Court had not ruled on the application as of this writing, and the questions remain unresolved.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a president who lost in the trial courts and the circuit courts now gambling that a friendly Supreme Court majority will hand him a standing army for domestic use. The lower-court language — no rebellion, no justification — is the whole case, they argue; letting him deploy anyway would bless using the military against cities for their politics. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right argues the president needs the Court to settle a genuine question: whether he can protect federal personnel and enforce federal law when local officials actively obstruct. But federalism-minded and libertarian conservatives (Reason) warn the right against cheering a precedent that would let a future Democratic president federalize Guard units over Republican governors’ objections — a power, they say, better left unclaimed. (Directional.)

Now Munich: drones shut Germany’s second-busiest airport for hours, stranding thousands

+

The drone campaign that shadowed this week’s Copenhagen summit reached German soil. Munich Airport — Germany’s second-busiest — was brought to a standstill after several drones were sighted in its airspace late Thursday night, with roughly 17 departing flights cancelled shortly after 10 p.m. local time and about 15 inbound flights diverted to Stuttgart, Nuremberg, Frankfurt and Vienna. Some 3,000 travellers were affected; operations resumed around 5 a.m.

The closure fits the pattern European officials have been describing for weeks — unidentified drones probing airports and military sites across the continent, from Copenhagen to Poland, in what many capitals call a deliberate “hybrid” test of allied reaction widely linked to Russia, though Moscow denies involvement and formal attribution in individual cases stays cautious. Coming days after EU leaders in Copenhagen backed a “drone wall” and faster counter-drone investment, the Munich shutdown is an immediate, concrete illustration of exactly the vulnerability that initiative is meant to close. (Attribution per European officials remains provisional; no actor has been confirmed responsible for the Munich sightings.)

The Shutdown · Day Two

‘The RIFs have begun’ — then a judge halts them: the shutdown’s mass firings start and stall within hours

+

The threat became real, and then it didn’t. Budget director Russell Vought announced on social media that “the RIFs have begun,” and the administration started issuing reduction-in-force notices — permanent layoffs, not furloughs — across several agencies as the government shutdown entered its second day. A budget-office spokesperson called the reductions “substantial”; notices or plans were reported at the Education Department, among federal health workers, and at the Treasury, which was said to be targeting roughly 1,300 employees.

Within hours, a federal judge intervened. U.S. District Judge Susan Illston in the Northern District of California granted a temporary restraining order sought by the American Federation of Government Employees and other unions, which had sued Sept. 30 arguing the firings were illegal. Illston found the plaintiffs “likely to succeed” on their claim that OMB and OPM’s direction to pursue RIFs during a funding lapse “rests on illegal grounds,” calling the action “illegal,” an “excess of authority” and “arbitrary and capricious.” Her order bars new RIF notices and pauses the clock on those already issued at the covered agencies, with a further hearing set later this month. The underlying budget impasse — Democrats withholding the votes to reach 60 unless a stopgap extends the expiring Affordable Care Act subsidies — is unchanged. (Fast-moving; the TRO is temporary and the scope of actual firings was contested as of this writing.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left says the court order confirms what they alleged: using a funding lapse to purge the civil service permanently is lawless coercion, not budgeting. A loyalist budget chief tried to weaponize workers’ jobs to break the minority, they argue, and a judge stopped it cold on day two — proof Democrats should hold the line on the ACA rather than reward the tactic. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right says Democrats manufactured the shutdown and now complain about its consequences; a bloated workforce can stand trimming, and the WSJ editorial line is to pass the clean stopgap and argue health policy separately. Libertarians (Reason) are sympathetic to cuts but wary of the method. Anti-Trump conservatives at The Bulwark warn that shutdown-triggered mass firings set a precedent every future president will abuse — an intra-right dissent, not the party line. (Directional.)

Courts · Troops in American Cities

‘Political opposition is not rebellion’: an appeals court keeps the National Guard out of Chicago’s streets

+

A federal appeals panel dealt the administration a setback in its bid to put troops on the streets of Chicago. The court rejected the government’s request to lift a district judge’s order blocking deployment of the National Guard in Illinois, finding the president’s plan “likely” violated the Constitution’s Tenth Amendment and that the facts did “not justify the president’s actions.” In a line now echoing across the litigation, the panel wrote that “political opposition is not rebellion,” and saw “insufficient evidence of a rebellion or danger of rebellion in Illinois.”

The ruling leaves roughly 200 Texas Guard members in the Chicago area federalized but barred from being deployed, and tracks the earlier block on a Portland deployment — the administration has signalled it will press its authority upward, potentially to the Supreme Court. Gov. JB Pritzker called the decision a win for Illinois. The cases turn on the same unresolved question: whether a president may federalize and deploy a state’s Guard domestically over its governor’s objection, absent an insurrection. (Active litigation; the block is preliminary and the administration’s appeal options remain open.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads the ruling as the courts naming the thing plainly: a president trying to use soldiers as a domestic police force against cities whose politics he dislikes, with no rebellion to justify it. “Political opposition is not rebellion,” they say, is the whole case — the deployments were intimidation dressed as law-and-order, and judges across the country keep saying so. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right argues a president must be able to protect federal personnel and property when local officials won’t, and that obstruction of immigration enforcement is the real disorder. But federalism-minded and libertarian conservatives (Reason) are uneasy: domestic Guard deployment over a governor’s objection is a power they would not hand a future Democratic president, and courts drawing a hard line may be doing the right a long-run favor. (Directional.)

The drones that forced the summit: mystery craft over Danish airports and military sites put Europe on a war footing

+

The urgency behind the Copenhagen talks came from the sky. In the days before the summit, unidentified drones flew over Copenhagen airport and Danish military sites, forcing airspace closures and disruption and following a string of similar incursions over Poland and other members of the alliance. European officials have described the pattern as a deliberate test of allied reaction — a “hybrid” campaign of pressure widely linked to Russia, though attribution in several cases remains officially cautious.

The incursions have pushed drone defence to the top of the continent’s security agenda and lent the “drone wall” initiative its momentum, while sharpening a hard question for NATO: how to detect and down cheap, deniable aircraft that probe below the threshold of open war without being drawn into a wider conflict. Several states have scrambled jets and hardened borders in response, and the alliance’s eastern members are pressing for faster, shared early-warning and counter-drone capability. Astrophysics · Transients

Caught in the act: minutes-long cosmic X-ray flashes traced to colliding neutron stars forging a magnetar

+

Astronomers report strong evidence that a class of mysterious, minutes-long X-ray flashes is produced when two neutron stars collide and briefly form a magnetar — a hyper-magnetized neutron star — before it collapses. The flashes had puzzled observers because they last far longer than an ordinary burst but far shorter than most persistent sources; linking them to neutron-star mergers ties a distinct observational signature to one of the universe’s most violent events.

If it holds, the result gives astronomers a new electromagnetic handle on mergers that are otherwise studied chiefly through gravitational waves, and a window onto the extreme physics of the magnetar that survives, however briefly, in the wreckage. It also sharpens follow-up strategies for the next generation of wide-field X-ray monitors hunting these transients. (An interpretation of a transient population; individual events will be scrutinized against alternative explanations.)

Evolution · Paleobiology

Bats began in Europe: a genomic-and-fossil study pushes their origin back about 65 million years — with flight and echolocation emerging early

+

A combined genomic and fossil study concludes that bats first evolved in Europe, roughly 65 to 60 million years ago — soon after the mass extinction that ended the age of dinosaurs — before dispersing across the globe. Crucially, the analysis places both powered flight and echolocation near the very beginning of bat evolution, rather than as late refinements, reframing a long debate over which of the group’s signature traits came first.

Bats are among the most species-rich and ecologically important groups of mammals, yet their early history has been murky because their delicate skeletons fossilize poorly. Pinning the origin to a time and place, and bundling the key adaptations together early, gives researchers a sharper framework for how one lineage of small mammals conquered the night sky. (A new reconstruction; origin estimates from molecular clocks and sparse fossils are routinely revised by later finds.)

Energy · Electrification

A record month for EVs: global sales hit 2.1 million in September, up 26% — even as a US incentive ends

+

Global sales of battery-electric and plug-in hybrid vehicles set a monthly record in September, reaching about 2.1 million units — up roughly 26% year on year, according to industry trackers. China again led by a wide margin with more than 1.3 million sales, while the United States saw a late rush of buyers ahead of the expiry of federal EV incentives and Europe also notched a monthly high. The share of fully electric cars in global sales is now on track to push above 30% in the second half of the year.

The figures underline how much of the growth is concentrated in China, where falling prices and fierce competition keep demand buoyant, and raise the question of what happens to the U.S. market once the subsidy cliff passes. The surge in electric mileage feeds directly into the grid and storage build-out reshaping power systems — more EVs mean more flexible load and, increasingly, vehicle-to-grid capacity.
