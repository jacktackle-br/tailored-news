Iran War · Strait of Hormuz

Trump again says a Hormuz deal is days away — but the draft on the table would hand Iran new control of the chokepoint, and Tehran denies direct talks are even happening

+

The Strait of Hormuz — the passage for roughly a fifth of the world’s seaborne oil and gas — has been effectively closed since the US–Israel military campaign against Iran earlier this year, keeping crude elevated (Brent around $80, WTI near $76 in-window). On Wednesday (Aug. 5), President Trump said the United States and Iran were having “very good discussions” and that a deal to reopen the strait could come within days, again warning Iran would be “hit very hard” absent an agreement. Iran and Oman say talks are in their “final stages,” and a senior Gulf official put the odds of a deal by Friday at roughly 50-50; Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent signaled optimism.

The tension running through Tier-1 coverage is what the emerging framework would actually do. CNN’s analysis calls it “an agreement taking shape, but not one Trump wants”: the reported Iran–Oman draft would give Tehran supervision and toll authority over inbound traffic through the strait — powers it did not hold before the war — which Washington, Abu Dhabi and Riyadh have treated as a red line. There is also a live factual dispute: Iran has at points denied that direct US–Iran talks resumed, insisting it is negotiating only through Oman, even as Trump describes active discussions. Trump has repeatedly teased an imminent agreement before, so the “close” framing carries a track record of slippage. Separately, Houthi forces claimed a ballistic-missile attack on a Saudi oil tanker in the Red Sea, a reminder that regional shipping attacks continued even as talks advanced.

The Left-Leaning Take

Trump launched a costly war ostensibly to reopen the strait and is now poised to accept terms that leave Iran with more leverage than before — supervision of a global chokepoint, plus tolls — a self-inflicted strategic loss dressed up as a win, with American consumers paying at the pump in the meantime. (Directional synthesis from restraint/Guardian-adjacent coverage; no dedicated in-window Mother Jones/Vox/Slate column on the draft was found.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The hawkish right (Sen. Tom Cotton, quoted in-window) argues you “cannot expect revolutionary terrorists to make a deal and stick to it,” so any agreement ceding Iran control of Hormuz rewards aggression and won’t hold. A competing realist strand counters that restoring the pre-war status quo would have required regime change or an open-ended military commitment, making a negotiated settlement the least-bad option. Aviation Safety · Marine One

The FAA and NTSB open an investigation after controllers let a passenger jet take off as Marine One lifted off with Trump aboard — the exact scenario a post-crash rule was written to prevent

+

On Tuesday afternoon (Aug. 4), Marine One departed the White House grounds with President Trump aboard, bound for Joint Base Andrews. About a minute later, Envoy Air Flight 3742 (an Embraer E170 to Pensacola) was cleared for takeoff from Runway 19 at Reagan National (DCA), and preliminary flight tracking shows the two aircraft came closer than standard minimums (1.5 miles horizontal / 500 feet vertical) as the jet climbed above the helicopter. The FAA opened a review and the NTSB an investigation. The FAA’s preliminary finding described “a momentary loss of separation, after which the aircraft continued to move away from one another,” and said a controller stayed in contact with both crews; the White House said Trump was never in danger.

The core issue is a protocol failure. Under rules adopted after the Jan. 29, 2025 midair collision between an Army Black Hawk and a regional jet that killed 67 people over the Potomac, DCA is supposed to halt takeoffs and landings whenever a presidential helicopter crosses the corridor — and controllers did not stop traffic. Air-traffic-control audio reviewed by several outlets indicates Marine One’s pilots announced at least three times that they were preparing to depart without the tower clearly acknowledging. Tier-1 accounts agree on the sequence, differing mainly in emphasis between “severity” and “protocol breakdown.”

The Left-Leaning Take

The pointed irony is that the President’s own flight triggered a safety-protocol failure at the very airport where 67 people died 18 months ago — and that it happened amid FAA staffing shortfalls and turnover that Democrats warned against, undercutting Trump’s posture as the aviation-safety president. (Directional synthesis; dedicated left columns were thin in the sub-24h window.)

The Right-Leaning Take

This was a momentary loss of separation, not a near-catastrophe — the aircraft were already diverging, the President was never at risk, and it reflects a controller/communication lapse, not administration policy. The right-leaning read (Daily Caller, Fox framing) treats the wall-to-wall coverage as eagerness to manufacture a Trump crisis from a routine, self-correcting event. (Directional; no in-window WSJ or National Review editorial found.)

Sources: Washington Post, CNN, NBC News, CBS News, CNBC
Justice Department · Attorney General Confirmation

With Collins a firm “no,” Trump’s AG pick Todd Blanche courts Murkowski and Cassidy ahead of a Friday cloture vote his nomination could still lose

+

Todd Blanche — Trump’s former personal criminal-defense lawyer and acting attorney general since Pam Bondi’s ouster — was advanced by the Senate Judiciary Committee on a party-line 12–10 vote (Aug. 4) after he agreed in writing to rescind the DOJ’s roughly $1.78 billion “anti-weaponization fund.” Majority Leader John Thune filed cloture Wednesday, setting a first floor vote Friday and a final confirmation vote expected Saturday. Sen. Susan Collins (R-Maine) has announced she will vote no; Sens. Lisa Murkowski (R-Alaska) and Bill Cassidy (R-La.) remain publicly undecided and both met with Blanche on Capitol Hill Wednesday (Aug. 5), with Cassidy saying he wants assurance Blanche won’t simply act as Trump’s lawyer. With GOP leader Mitch McConnell reportedly absent and Democrats uniformly opposed, a defection by either Murkowski or Cassidy would sink the nomination — leaving the math on a knife’s edge into the weekend.

The legal core (Lawfare, weighted near Tier-1 here): the fund grew out of a settlement of the Trump family’s civil suit against the IRS over leaked tax records, drawing on the federal Judgment Fund to pay anyone claiming to be a “victim of weaponization.” Lawfare argues Blanche’s rescission may lack durable legal effect — it could be withdrawn, or the Trump family could sue to enforce — and reports that one piece of the settlement remains in place: a May 19 document purporting to extend broad legal protections to Trump, his sons, the Trump Organization and “affiliates” for prior conduct, which critics call a self-pardon by the back door. Blanche has publicly maintained that DOJ sits “under the executive branch” and that Trump may influence its investigations — the crux of the independence fight.

The Left-Leaning Take

Blanche is unfit because he is, functionally, still Trump’s defense attorney — the man who negotiated a settlement shielding the family from prosecution now asks to run the department that would do the prosecuting. The rescinded slush fund doesn’t cure the corruption; the residual immunity document does the real work, and confirming him ratifies a personalized DOJ. (Synthesized from Slate, The Hill and Boston Globe opinion; Bloomberg “poor choice.”)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream-right signal is mixed rather than cheerleading. National Review’s Corner stressed the Senate’s duty to check presidential power and judge nominees consistently — not a full endorsement. The pro-Blanche case (a WSJ op-ed, “What I Saw While Working for Todd Blanche,” and DOJ allies) frames him as a capable prosecutor smeared for having defended Trump. The unease from Collins and Cassidy is itself an intra-right check.

Sources: Washington Post, The Hill, CNBC, NPR, Lawfare (fund), Lawfare (settlement)

United States · Russia’s War on Ukraine

The Senate barrels toward passage of the sweeping “Graham” Russia–Iran sanctions bill — as Trump demands added power to tariff Iran and the pressure campaign shifts to Congress

+

The US Senate is moving toward final passage of the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, named for the South Carolina senator and longtime Ukraine hawk who died July 12 of an aortic dissection at 71. The chamber cleared a procedural cloture vote 86–12 in late July — Sen. Rand Paul the lone Republican “no” — just hours after Graham’s funeral and a closed-door appeal to senators by Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky, who watched the vote from the chamber. The bill would authorize President Trump to impose steep secondary tariffs (reported up to 200%) on the largest purchasers of Russian oil and gas — explicitly targeting China and India — expand sanctions on Russian banks, oligarchs and the oil “shadow fleet,” and extend Iran sanctions through 2031. As of Aug. 5, the Senate Foreign Relations Committee circulated a statement touting broad support and signaling imminent final action, with House consideration expected after the August recess.

The genuinely new development: with battlefield diplomacy stalled and Russian barrages on Kyiv continuing, the pressure has shifted to Congress and the threat of secondary sanctions on Moscow’s energy customers — leverage that runs through Beijing and New Delhi, so passage risks collateral damage to US trade with two major economies. In a late complication, Trump publicly demanded lawmakers amend the bill to grant him explicit authority to tariff Iran, injecting friction over how much discretion the executive should retain. (Outlets differ on the headline tariff ceiling — most now cite up to 200%, against earlier “500%” framing — and some loosely describe the bill as “passed” when only the cloture step is confirmed; final Senate passage and House action remain pending.)

Middle East · Israel–Hamas War

Netanyahu says Israel won’t leave Gaza until Hamas fully disarms, opening rare public daylight with Trump’s “Board of Peace” phase-two plan

+

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu declared this week that Israeli forces will not withdraw from their current lines in Gaza until Hamas has completely disarmed, casting doubt on the phase-two framework brokered under Trump’s “Board of Peace.” That deal called for Hamas to begin surrendering its weapons while Israel halts strikes and pulls back from the roughly 60% of Gaza it controls. Netanyahu’s comments amounted to a rare public display of daylight between Jerusalem and Washington over sequencing: Israel says any withdrawal hinges on disarmament first, while Hamas says Israel must end strikes and withdraw first, and each accuses the other of violating the truce.

The disarmament-versus-withdrawal sequencing is the crux that could collapse phase two. Netanyahu is also managing domestic pressure — polling has shown a large majority of Israelis favoring a deal to recover hostages, while his coalition’s right flank pushes for deeper control of Gaza. Per Gaza’s Health Ministry, at least 1,252 people have been killed since the ceasefire took effect, with humanitarian conditions in displacement camps remaining dire; those figures are not independently verified by Israel. Framing differs by outlet, with some attributing the “no withdrawal until disarmament” line to Netanyahu personally and others to the Board of Peace mechanism itself.

Sources: Al Jazeera, Euronews, Courthouse News (AP)
Asia-Pacific · Korean Peninsula

North Korea fires a short-range ballistic missile on the Hiroshima anniversary, days before annual US–South Korea drills

+

North Korea launched a short-range ballistic missile toward the waters off its east coast on Thursday, Aug. 6, according to South Korea’s Joint Chiefs of Staff, which detected the launch from the Wonsan area around 5 p.m. local time (0900 GMT). Japan’s Defense Ministry confirmed a probable ballistic-missile launch and said it did not land in or around Japan’s exclusive economic zone. The test came the same day Japan marked the 81st anniversary of the Hiroshima atomic bombing, and a day after Pyongyang said it would pursue unspecified “additional military options” in response to Japan’s expanding defense capabilities.

The launch also lands just before the annual US–South Korea military exercises, typically held in the latter half of August, which the North denounces as invasion rehearsals. It fits Pyongyang’s established pattern of timing tests to political anniversaries and allied drills, but the explicit framing against Japan’s military buildup — on the Hiroshima anniversary — reads as deliberate signaling toward Tokyo. It is a routine-caliber weapon, but a fresh single-day event and a reminder that Kim Jong Un continues arsenal expansion with no active US–DPRK diplomatic track since 2019. (South Korea initially called it an “unidentified projectile” before assessments settled on a short-range ballistic missile; count and type specifics were still preliminary.)

Sources: The Japan Times, ABC News (AP), The Manila Times (AP), The Republic (AP)

Technology · Semiconductors & AI

Anthropic confirms it is building an in-house team to design its own AI chips, joining the hyperscaler race to cut dependence on Nvidia

+

On Aug. 5, Anthropic confirmed for the first time that it is standing up an internal silicon team to design custom chips for its Claude models, opening job listings across front-end design, pre-silicon verification, physical design, analog/mixed-signal, foundry and advanced packaging (reported salary band roughly $320K–$485K). The company describes a hardware/software “co-design” approach — chip and model shaped together, in the vein of Apple’s M-series or Google’s TPUs — and stressed the effort does not displace its existing multi-chip strategy across AWS Trainium, Google TPUs, Nvidia and AMD. The move puts Anthropic alongside OpenAI, Meta, Google and Amazon in pursuing custom silicon to reduce reliance on Nvidia.

Caveats worth flagging: a widely repeated figure that custom silicon could cut per-token inference costs by about half traces to a secondary outlet, not an official Anthropic target — treat it as a reported aspiration, not a committed metric. This is a hiring-and-strategy confirmation, not a shipping chip; no tape-out or product timeline was announced, and prior reporting that Anthropic held talks with Samsung as a manufacturing partner remains unconfirmed as a deal.

Energy · Grid & Storage

Base Power raises $1B at a $13B valuation and launches “Base Core,” a 40-kWh home battery it says installs in minutes and feeds the grid

+

Home-battery startup Base Power announced (Aug. 3) a $1 billion Series D at a roughly $13 billion post-money valuation — its second billion-dollar round in about ten months, pushing total funding past $2.5 billion. Alongside the raise it launched “Base Core,” a roughly 40-kWh unit with a 20-kW inverter (about triple a typical home battery) engineered to install without custom wiring for whole-home backup. Base bundles cheap storage with electricity service: it sells power per kWh in retail-choice markets such as Texas and Chicago and, in regulated markets like El Paso, hands battery dispatch to utilities that pay for grid access — effectively building a distributed virtual power plant.

The company says it is installing about 100 batteries a day (roughly 8 MWh/day) and aims to roughly double that pace by the end of 2026. Caveat: the valuation, install-rate and deployment-doubling figures are company-stated, and the announcement sits just outside a strict 24-hour window — but it is the most consequential fresh energy and electrification story of the period.

Science · Human Genetics

A growth-hormone-receptor variant inherited from Neanderthals makes carriers’ cells respond more strongly — and is linked to modestly greater muscle mass

+

A study published online in Current Biology on Aug. 5 reports that a variant of the growth-hormone receptor that modern humans inherited from Neanderthals (around 47,000 years ago) increases the cell’s signaling response to growth hormone. In lab tests, cells carrying the Neanderthal variant grew about 40% more than cells with the common modern version when stimulated. Analyzing data from just over 1.1 million adults, the team found carriers of at least one Neanderthal copy averaged roughly 271 grams more muscle mass, along with skeletal differences (a shorter jaw ramus, higher likelihood of an overbite, shorter tooth roots). The variant is most common today in South and East Asian populations, reaching up to about 24% in some.

The work is peer-reviewed and published, and drew coverage from Science, Nature and Scientific American. A note on certainty: the effect sizes are population-level associations — the ~271 g muscle difference is an average, not an individual guarantee — and the 40% figure is an in-vitro cellular measurement.

Sources: Science, Nature, Scientific American, Current Biology (study)
Space · Direct-to-Cell Satellites

SpaceX launches three next-gen “BlueBird” satellites for AST SpaceMobile, pushing its direct-to-smartphone constellation to 13 spacecraft

+

Early on Wednesday, Aug. 5, a SpaceX Falcon 9 lifted off from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral (around 3:49 a.m. EDT / 0749 UTC) carrying AST SpaceMobile’s BlueBird satellites 11, 12 and 13. The three Block 2 satellites deployed to low Earth orbit over about 11 minutes, bringing the total BlueBird fleet to 13 — a milestone toward AST’s goal of continuous direct-to-cell service to standard, unmodified 4G/5G smartphones. The Falcon 9 first stage completed its 30th landing, on the droneship “A Shortfall of Gravitas.”

AST says the next-gen satellites should deliver nearly double the peak download speeds of its Block 1 units, which recently demonstrated about 98.9 Mbps direct to phones. Caveat: those throughput numbers are company-provided, and full commercial, continuous coverage still depends on many more satellites reaching orbit.

Sources: Space.com, Spaceflight Now, Business Wire (AST)

Premier League · Arsenal

Guimarães completes his Arsenal medical as the Gunners shake off a sloppy 3–1 friendly loss to Real Betis in Dublin — and Vinícius looks set to stay at Madrid

+

Arsenal’s pursuit of Bruno Guimarães is essentially done. After agreeing a roughly £75m fee with Newcastle (reported as structured instalments), the Brazil midfielder and Magpies captain has completed his Arsenal medical and is set to sign a four-year contract, with an official announcement expected imminently as of Aug. 6. Mikel Arteta, who has coveted him for years, has already spoken with the player. On the other long-running saga, the Vinícius Júnior pursuit appears to be cooling: reports around Aug. 5 indicate the winger is now set to stay at Real Madrid after positive contract-extension talks, with Arsenal unwilling to match Madrid’s asking price (around €170m). Arseblog’s “Star Power” piece framed the fan debate over whether Arteta could ever accommodate a “maverick” like Vinícius.

On the pitch, Arsenal’s pre-season friendly against Real Betis was played Wednesday night (Aug. 5) at Dublin’s Aviva Stadium — and the Gunners lost 3–1. Betis raced to a 3–0 first-half lead through Rodrigo Riquelme, a long-range Nelson Deossa strike and Pablo Fornals (capitalizing on a loose Kai Havertz pass); new signing Christos Tzolis started, and Piero Hincapié pulled one back with a header from a Tzolis corner. Arteta said his players were “fuming” and admitted the display was undone by sloppy mistakes. The bigger worry was an injury: 19-year-old midfielder Louie Copley hurt his knee after coming on, and Arteta said he was “quite worried” the damage could be serious.
