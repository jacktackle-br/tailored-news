The Budget · Shutdown Countdown

A shutdown now looks all but certain: leaders leave the White House with no deal, and the blame war is fully joined with barely a day on the clock

+

The last realistic off-ramp before the Oct. 1 deadline narrowed further. President Trump’s sit-down with the four congressional leaders — Senate Majority Leader John Thune, Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, Speaker Mike Johnson and Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, with Vice President JD Vance and budget director Russ Vought in the room — broke up with no agreement. Schumer called the talks “candid” and “frank” and suggested Trump was more open to a health-care deal than his own leaders; Johnson insisted the GOP stopgap is “clean” and “non-partisan” and that Democrats are manufacturing a crisis.

Nothing on the table bridges the core divide: Democrats are withholding the eight-or-so votes needed to clear the Senate’s 60-vote bar unless the bill extends the expiring enhanced ACA premium subsidies, and Republicans want health policy handled separately. Dueling partisan stopgaps already failed on the Senate floor earlier this month (44–48 and 47–45), and no new vote has been scheduled that changes the math before funding lapses at 12:01 a.m. Oct. 1. A Washington Post flash poll found more respondents ready to blame Trump and Republicans (47%) than Democrats (30%), with 71% favoring an extension of the subsidies. (Fast-moving; a last-minute stopgap could still change the picture.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads the collapsed meeting as vindication: the party that runs the White House and both chambers can end this any time by extending subsidies that keep premiums from spiking, and the early polling shows the public isn’t buying the “Democrats shut it down” framing. Hold the line, they argue — the leverage is real and the cause is popular. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right says Democrats are choosing a shutdown to jam an expensive Obamacare extension onto an unrelated funding bill; the WSJ editorial board calls the subsidy panic manufactured. Libertarians (Reason) see bipartisan brinkmanship over a budget no one will restrain. Anti-Trump conservatives at The Bulwark counter that with every lever in GOP hands the fallout lands on Republicans regardless — an intra-right dissent, not the party line. (Directional.)

The Pentagon · Civil-Military Relations

On the eve of an unprecedented summons: Hegseth pulls hundreds of generals and admirals to Quantico for a ‘warrior ethos’ address, with Trump set to attend

+

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has ordered roughly 800 of the military’s most senior generals, admirals and their top enlisted advisers to convene at Marine Corps Base Quantico in Virginia — a mass, short-notice gathering with no publicly stated purpose that is without clear precedent. Reporting ahead of the meeting says Hegseth plans to lay out a vision built on what he calls the “warrior ethos,” new physical-fitness and appearance standards, and an end to “woke” priorities. President Trump is expected to address the assembled brass, and the event is slated to be livestreamed.

The summons follows a year of turnover at the top under Hegseth — directed cuts to the ranks of general and flag officers, the removal of the Navy’s top officer and the Air Force vice chief without stated cause, and the firing of the services’ senior lawyers. Critics inside and outside the Pentagon have questioned the security wisdom, cost and message of pulling combatant commanders from posts worldwide on short notice; the White House has waved off the concerns. (Details per pre-event reporting; the substance of the address had not been delivered as of this writing.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a costly loyalty spectacle: yanking the entire senior officer corps across oceans for an anti-“woke” lecture, staged for the cameras with the president in the room, blurs the line between an apolitical military and Trump’s movement — the latest step in a pattern of purges that thins experienced leadership and chills dissent. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right largely welcomes the refocus: a Pentagon distracted by social priorities and bloated brass is overdue for a lethality-and-standards reset, and the theatrics matter less than the message. Libertarians (Reason) are wary of the expense and of politicizing the officer corps. Anti-Trump conservatives at The Bulwark go further, warning the loyalty overtones are exactly what a professional military should avoid — an intra-right alarm, not the party line. (Directional.)

Gaza · Middle East

The sequencing wall gives way: Trump and Netanyahu unveil a 20-point plan the PM says he backs — and the war’s fate now rests with Hamas

+

What the morning brief framed as a mutual-distrust stalemate moved sharply this afternoon. At a joint White House appearance, President Trump laid out a 20-point proposal to end the Gaza war and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said flatly, “I support your plan.” The framework would halt the fighting, surge humanitarian aid, and require Hamas to release the remaining Israeli hostages on a tight timeline (Trump cited release within days of an agreement) in exchange for the freeing of some 2,000 Palestinian prisoners, with Hamas barred from any future governing role in Gaza. Netanyahu warned that if Hamas refuses, Israel will “finish the job” the hard way.

In a striking side-development, hours before the cameras Netanyahu phoned Qatar’s prime minister from the Oval Office and apologized for Israel’s Sept. 9 strike on Doha that killed a Qatari security officer — a concession aimed at keeping the key mediator on board. The ball is now in Hamas’s court: the group said it is reviewing the plan. The hard questions the morning flagged — the order of any Israeli withdrawal, who verifies disarmament, and who governs the enclave afterward — are unresolved, but for the first time the two leaders are publicly aligned on a single text. (A framework, not a signed deal; Hamas’s answer will decide whether it holds.)

Europe · Moldova

Moldova’s pro-EU governing party holds its majority, beating back what officials called a Russian ‘hybrid war’ to swing the vote

+

President Maia Sandu’s pro-European Party of Action and Solidarity (PAS) won the weekend parliamentary election with roughly 50% of the vote to about 24% for the pro-Russian Patriotic Electoral Bloc, preliminary results show — enough to keep an outright majority of around 55 of the legislature’s 101 seats. Turnout was near 52%, with the large diaspora again tilting decisively toward the West. European leaders hailed the result as a reaffirmation of Moldova’s EU-membership path.

The vote unfolded amid what Moldovan authorities described as an extensive Russian “hybrid war” — vote-buying, disinformation and cyber pressure — making the margin a meaningful data point for how far Moscow’s interference toolkit can be blunted by a mobilized electorate and a diaspora vote. For Brussels, it removes a near-term threat to enlargement on the EU’s eastern flank; for the Kremlin, it is a setback in a country it has long treated as within its orbit and a bellwether as the war in neighboring Ukraine grinds on.

The Budget · Shutdown Countdown

One day to the deadline: still no funding deal as a shutdown looms at midnight Wednesday, with ACA subsidies the sticking point

+

Federal funding lapses at the end of the fiscal year on Sept. 30, and without a stopgap the government begins shutting down at 12:01 a.m. on Oct. 1 — now roughly a day away. President Trump and the four top congressional leaders — Senate Majority Leader John Thune, Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, House Speaker Mike Johnson and Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries — broke up their White House meeting on Monday with no agreement, and no breakthrough had been reported by Tuesday morning. Schumer said the two sides have “very large differences.”

Republicans, who hold both chambers narrowly, want a “clean” short-term bill that keeps money flowing at current levels into late November. Democrats are withholding their votes unless the measure also extends the enhanced Affordable Care Act premium subsidies that expire at year’s end, warning that premiums will spike otherwise. Because a stopgap needs 60 votes in the Senate and Republicans hold 53, roughly eight Democrats would have to cross over — and few have signaled they will. The standoff arrives five weeks before the Nov. 3 midterms, sharpening every incentive to assign blame. (Fast-moving; a last-minute stopgap or short extension could still change the picture before the deadline.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left says Democrats finally have leverage and should use it: with health-insurance costs about to jump for millions, holding the line to save the ACA subsidies is both good policy and good politics. Let a party that controls the White House and both chambers own a shutdown — and own letting health-care help lapse while defending tax breaks for the wealthy. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right frames it as Democrats threatening to shut the government down to force an expensive Obamacare bailout onto an unrelated funding bill; pass the clean stopgap, they argue, and negotiate health policy separately. Libertarians (Reason) see familiar brinkmanship from both parties atop a budget nobody will restrain. Anti-Trump conservatives at The Bulwark counter that the party holding every lever will own the fallout no matter who blinks — an intra-right dissent, not the party line. (Directional.)

Technology · The AI Race

The frontier race tightens: OpenAI’s GPT-6 ‘Astra’ resets the ‘computer-use’ bar as rivals weigh their next move

+

OpenAI’s flagship GPT-6 “Astra,” released on Sept. 3, has reset expectations for “agentic” AI — models that operate software by clicking through browsers, filling forms and editing records rather than just chatting. OpenAI bills it as the best computer-use model yet, citing large jumps on agent benchmarks (Terminal-Bench 4.0 rising to 57.9% from 37.3%, ScreenSpot-Pro to 92.7%) and a roughly 1.05 million-token context window. Notably, it is the first model OpenAI has ever rated “Critical” for cyber capability — an internal flag that the model could meaningfully aid offensive hacking.

The launch has intensified jockeying among the leading labs, with competitors reported to be weighing their own releases and, in some cases, IPO timing after the November midterms. The capability leap is exactly the backdrop to this week’s White House AI meeting (see Politics): the same “computer-use” and cyber gains that make these systems commercially valuable are what safety researchers say demand guardrails. Energy · Grid & Data Centers

Power, not chips, is now the binding constraint on AI — and it is pushing Big Tech toward nuclear

+

The scramble to build AI data centers is colliding with the limits of the electricity grid. In its 2026 outlook, the Uptime Institute concludes that power — not cooling, land or connectivity — is now the binding constraint on data-center expansion. The pressure is concentrated where the machines cluster: in the PJM Interconnection, the 13-state grid that includes Northern Virginia’s “Data Center Alley,” capacity-auction prices have surged on the order of 800% year-over-year, a market signal that supply is scarce.

That crunch is reshaping energy strategy. Hyperscalers are increasingly signing nuclear deals — power-purchase agreements, restarts of shuttered reactors, and interest in small modular reactors — because they promise large, steady, carbon-free output in exactly the regions running short of headroom. U.S. nuclear operators, long stagnant, are now openly courting the load. The bet is not without risk: new reactors are slow and expensive to build, and near-term demand may still be met by gas, straining both grids and climate goals. Climate · State of the Climate

The annual state-of-the-climate review confirms 2025 set fresh records — and the Arctic tundra is now a source of carbon, not a sink

+

The latest annual State of the Climate report — the 36th edition, compiled by the American Meteorological Society from the work of some 625 scientists in 60 countries — confirms that greenhouse-gas concentrations, global temperatures and European wildfire emissions all reached record highs in 2025. Globally averaged carbon dioxide hit 425.6 parts per million, about 53% above the preindustrial level, and estimated fossil-fuel emissions set a record at roughly 10.3 petagrams of carbon per year — more than triple the 1960s average.

The Arctic remains the clearest warning light. Lower-atmosphere temperatures there were the second-highest on record, and the report reiterates a troubling threshold: as permafrost thaws, the region’s tundra has flipped from a carbon sink to a carbon source, releasing CO₂ and methane that further amplify warming. The troposphere has warmed while the stratosphere cools — a fingerprint scientists attribute to human-caused CO₂. (Figures are from the peer-reviewed annual review; single-year records sit within longer-term trends.)

Sources: NILU, NBC News
