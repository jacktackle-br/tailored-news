The Shutdown · The Nuclear Option

Trump demands Republicans ‘go nuclear’ to end the shutdown — and his own Senate tells him no

+

With the Senate deadlocked for a ninth failed cloture vote and the shutdown grinding on, President Trump threw himself at the impasse, demanding on Truth Social that Senate Republicans scrap the legislative filibuster outright: “play their ‘TRUMP CARD,’ and go for what is called the Nuclear Option — Get rid of the Filibuster, and get rid of it, NOW!” The so-called nuclear option would let the GOP’s 53-seat majority change the rules by simple majority and pass its stopgap without the 60 votes Democrats have withheld.

The demand ran straight into a wall inside his own party. Majority Leader John Thune, whose spokesman said his position is “unchanged,” has repeatedly called the filibuster a “bulwark” vital to the institution — and a critical mass of Senate Republicans have stayed on the record against the move, a rare public fracture with the president. Trump’s counter-argument: Democrats would scrap the rule the moment they held the majority, so Republicans should act first. (The underlying funding fight — Democrats’ demand to extend expiring ACA subsidies — remains unresolved.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left (Vox, Slate, Mother Jones, Guardian opinion) reads the all-caps post as the president saying the quiet part out loud: the supermajority rule is an obstacle to one-party rule, and he wants it gone. The irony they note: the only guardrail here is a group of Republican institutionalists — and even that, they argue, is self-interest, since the filibuster is the tool the GOP relies on to block Democratic bills when it is in the minority. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right (WSJ editorial, National Review, The Dispatch) mostly sides with Thune: the filibuster has killed Democratic wish-lists before and a short-term win would be catastrophic the day Democrats run the chamber. Some populists back Trump’s impatience; libertarians (Reason) distrust concentrating that much power in a bare majority. The Bulwark (anti-Trump right) cheers the GOP resistance — an intra-right view, not the party line. (Directional.)

Sources: NOTUS, AP, Straight Arrow News, Fox News
The Shutdown · Spending as Leverage

Vought turns the lapse into a weapon, freezing tens of billions for blue states

+

As the shutdown drags, budget director Russ Vought’s OMB is wielding the funding freeze as a cudgel, with the cuts falling almost entirely on Democratic-led states. Officials have put $18 billion on hold for the NY–NJ Hudson Tunnel (Gateway) and Manhattan’s Second Avenue Subway, moved to cancel nearly $8 billion in Energy Department climate funding across sixteen mostly blue states, and ordered the Army Corps to delay or cancel roughly $11 billion more in Baltimore, Boston, New York and San Francisco — part of some $26 billion OMB has moved to withhold from states that voted against Mr. Trump in 2024.

The administration casts it as shrinking government during a lapse and rooting out spending it deems tied to DEI, while the president trolls Democrats on social media. New York and New Jersey have sued over the roughly $16 billion Hudson tunnel freeze, warning of transit chaos and seeking a fast ruling before construction already under way is forced to halt. (The impoundment litigation is ongoing; Congress appropriated the funds at issue.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees naked political retribution dressed up as fiscal restraint: canceling tunnels, transit and climate projects to punish voters in states that went the other way, and daring the courts to stop it. The deeper alarm, they argue, is constitutional — a White House impounding money Congress already appropriated, the very power the 1974 budget law was written to forbid. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right answers that Democrats forced the lapse and the administration is within its rights to pause questionable or DEI-tied spending while the government is closed. But the discomfort is real: some on the right (Reason, parts of The Dispatch) warn that selectively punishing states and stretching executive impoundment sets a precedent a future Democratic president will happily use the other way. (Directional.)

Sources: Smart Cities Dive, Scripps News, States Newsroom, NBC New York

Europe · Paris

France’s government falls in a day: Lecornu quits 14 hours after naming his cabinet

+

The wobble flagged this morning became a collapse by nightfall. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu resigned barely 14 hours after unveiling his cabinet and before it could hold its first meeting — the shortest-lived government of the Fifth Republic, and the end of a tenure that lasted some 27 days from appointment. He is President Macron’s fifth prime minister in under two years. The trigger was the lineup itself: stacked with familiar Macron-era faces that echoed his predecessor’s, it enraged left, right and Marine Le Pen’s National Rally alike, drawing immediate no-confidence threats that made governing impossible before it began.

Markets recoiled: the euro slid nearly 1% against the dollar and the CAC 40 fell as much as 2.1% before closing down 1.4%, with BNP Paribas, Société Générale and Crédit Agricole all off more than 3%. The resignation hands Macron an ugly choice — find yet another prime minister who can somehow pass a 2026 budget within days in a country whose debt tops 114% of GDP, or call the snap elections he has refused. Le Pen is demanding a dissolution of the National Assembly and floating that Macron himself resign; the left’s Jean-Luc Mélenchon is pushing to remove him. Macron, for now, insists he will serve out his term to 2027.

Sources: NBC News, The Globe and Mail (Reuters), South China Morning Post

The Shutdown · Day Five

Into a second week with no exit: the Senate stays stuck, the firings stay frozen, and the paychecks come due

+

The federal shutdown that began at midnight on October 1 rolled through the weekend with no deal in sight. The Senate has repeatedly failed to advance the Republicans’ “clean” stopgap — a continuing resolution to fund the government through November 21 — falling short of the 60 votes needed to break a filibuster as nearly all Democrats hold out for an extension of the expiring Affordable Care Act subsidies. Majority Leader John Thune says a “handful” of Democrats could reopen the government tomorrow; Minority Leader Chuck Schumer says the repeated failures prove Republicans must come to the table on health care. Neither side has blinked.

The administration’s threat to convert furloughs into permanent layoffs — roughly 4,200 reduction-in-force notices went out across about seven agencies in the first days — remains frozen by a federal court in California, where Judge Susan Illston barred further RIFs and halted those already issued while the case proceeds. Looming over the standoff is the first missed federal paycheck, now days away for some 750,000 furloughed workers, and Mr. Trump’s own signaling that back pay may not be automatic for everyone — a stance unions say runs headlong into the 2019 law guaranteeing it. (Figures are CBO and administration estimates; the RIF litigation is ongoing and no permanent ruling has issued.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left (Vox, Slate, Mother Jones, Guardian opinion) casts this as a manufactured crisis weaponized against workers: an administration using a funding lapse not to cut spending but to punish the civil service with firings a court had to stop, while flirting with stiffing employees on legally owed back pay. Democrats, they argue, are right to hold the line on health subsidies whose lapse would spike premiums for millions — the one point of leverage they have. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right (WSJ editorial, National Review, The Dispatch) says Democrats own this: they are blocking a clean, no-strings bill the government could pass today in order to attach an unrelated health-care demand, and holding federal workers hostage to do it. Some warn the White House against overplaying the firings gambit — it hands the other side a sympathetic story and a court win. Libertarians (Reason) see a pox on both houses and a budget process broken by design. (Directional.)

Sources: Government Executive, Federal News Network, CBS News, Office of the Majority Leader
Executive Power · The Courts Hold the Line

‘Not a rebellion’: a Trump-appointed judge bars the Guard from Portland’s streets — even from out of state

+

As the administration winds down its contested push to put the National Guard into American cities, the courts drew a sharp line in Oregon. U.S. District Judge Karin Immergut — a Trump appointee — blocked the deployment of Oregon’s Guard to Portland, and then issued a second order barring the administration from routing out-of-state Guard units there instead. She found the government had not met the legal threshold to treat protests outside a Portland ICE facility as a “rebellion,” describing the unrest as “sporadic” and “isolated” rather than organized armed hostility.

The ruling leaves the troops federalized but grounded — the administration may keep the Guard under federal command, but cannot put it on Portland’s streets. It lands alongside the parallel fight in Chicago, where appellate courts have likewise kept Guard units out, and after the Supreme Court declined to let the president federalize Illinois’s Guard over Gov. JB Pritzker’s objection. Together the rulings narrow, for now, the domestic-deployment theory the White House has pressed since late September. (The orders are preliminary; the government is expected to appeal.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads a pattern of judges — including the president’s own appointees — refusing to let “rebellion” be redefined to mean ordinary protest. The real tell, they argue, is the attempt to backfill with out-of-state troops after the home-state Guard was blocked: a search for any legal door into occupying blue cities, slammed shut by a judge who actually read the record. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right (WSJ editorial, National Review, The Dispatch) splits: a president should be able to protect federal personnel and property, but a Trump-appointed judge finding no rebellion is hard to wave away, and the prudent course is to win the argument in court, not force it. The Bulwark (anti-Trump right) calls the ruling a guardrail holding — an intra-right view, not the party line. Libertarians (Reason) warn against normalizing troops as domestic police. (Directional.)

Sources: NPR, NBC News, CNN, Lawfare
Immigration Enforcement · Chicago

A Border Patrol agent shoots a U.S. citizen in Brighton Park — and two versions of what happened

+

A federal immigration operation on Chicago’s Southwest Side turned violent on Saturday, when a Border Patrol agent shot Marimar Martinez, a 30-year-old U.S. citizen, multiple times in the Brighton Park neighborhood. The Department of Homeland Security said Martinez and another person had used their vehicles to box in and ram agents, calling the shooting self-defense; Martinez and neighborhood witnesses say it was the agents’ vehicle that rammed her car as she was trailing and filming the operation to warn residents. Federal prosecutors charged Martinez and a second Chicagoan with forcibly assaulting and impeding federal officers; no weapons charge was filed despite DHS’s claim she was armed.

The shooting detonated hours of confrontation between agents and residents and became an instant flashpoint in the fight over the administration’s intensified enforcement surge in the city — the same campaign that has driven the Guard litigation. City officials demanded answers; DHS labeled the pair “domestic terrorists.” The competing accounts have not been reconciled, and the incident is under investigation. (Partial — facts are contested; body-camera footage had not been released as of the window.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees the predictable result of flooding a city with armed federal agents operating with little accountability: a citizen shot for filming, then charged and branded a “terrorist” by the agency that shot her. The missing weapons charge, they argue, guts the official story, and only independent footage — not a DHS press release — should be trusted. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right urges waiting for the evidence: agents enforcing the law were, by the government’s account, surrounded and rammed, and ramming a federal vehicle is a serious crime, not journalism. Some on the right (Reason, elements of The Dispatch) are uneasier — shooting a citizen demands transparency and bodycam release, and “domestic terrorist” rhetoric before an investigation is reckless. (Directional.)

Space · An Interstellar Visitor

A comet from another star skims past Mars, and the orbiters turn to watch

+

3I/ATLAS — only the third interstellar object ever spotted crossing our solar system, after ‘Oumuamua and Borisov — made its closest approach to Mars this weekend, passing within a few tens of millions of kilometers of the planet. The flyby handed scientists a rare chance to study a visitor from another star up close: spacecraft orbiting Mars, which sit far nearer the comet than any telescope on Earth, were pointed to image it as it raced inward toward its late-October perihelion around the Sun.

Discovered only in mid-summer, the comet is unambiguously interstellar — its hyperbolic path means it is just passing through, never to return — and estimates of its icy nucleus have ranged from a few hundred meters up to several kilometers across. Each such object is a free sample of material from another planetary system, and researchers are racing to squeeze chemistry and structure out of a body that will be gone within months.

Biomedicine · Xenotransplantation

A gene-edited pig liver keeps a patient going for weeks — a first that hints at a bridge for failing organs

+

Chinese researchers reported that a gene-edited pig liver transplanted to support a living patient functioned for about 38 days before it was removed — among the first attempts to use a pig liver as a temporary “bridge” in a living human rather than in a brain-dead subject. The organ, from a pig engineered to blunt the human immune response, produced bile and helped sustain the patient while clinicians weighed next steps, offering a proof of concept that porcine livers might one day buy time for people in acute liver failure awaiting a transplant.

The result lands amid a run of xenotransplantation milestones — pig kidneys and hearts have been placed in a handful of patients — and the liver is a notably harder organ, with its thicket of metabolic and clotting functions. Researchers cautioned that this is early, single-case work: rejection, clotting and infection risks remain formidable, and the device-like “bridge” role looks nearer term than a permanent pig-to-human liver.

Sources: Smithsonian, Science News, 2025 in science (overview)

+

The tension between federal retrenchment and a still-cheapening clean-power build-out is becoming the defining story of the energy transition’s next phase.
