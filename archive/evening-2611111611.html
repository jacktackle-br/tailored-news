Senate · Mississippi

Democrat Scott Colom wins Mississippi’s Senate race as Hyde-Smith concedes, the party’s first Senate win there since 1982

+

The Associated Press called Mississippi’s Senate race for Democrat Scott Colom on Wednesday, eight days after Election Day. The call came after absentee and affidavit (provisional) ballots, including affidavit ballots from Hinds County, were counted. Colom, a district attorney, defeated Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith in the closest Senate race in the country. His lead was about 4,600 votes; reports give 4,585 or 4,669, depending on when the count was taken. Hyde-Smith called Colom to concede late Wednesday afternoon and said she respected voters’ decision. Mississippi has no automatic recount, and some earlier coverage expected a legal challenge, but none had been announced after the concession.

Colom will be the first Democrat Mississippi has elected to the Senate since 1982. He will also be the first Black person elected to statewide office in the state since Reconstruction. Trump carried Mississippi by about 22 points in 2024. Colom ran on cost of living, calling his opponent “Cindy High-Prices,” and a viral ad seized on Hyde-Smith’s advice that people upset about beef prices should “pick another protein.” Reports say the win brings Democrats to at least 55 seats in the incoming Senate. Not all outlets have confirmed that figure, and the Alaska race is still to be called. Colom will be sworn in in January.

Left-leaning coverage (TPM, Democracy Now) presents the result as a payoff for Black voter turnout in the South and as personal vindication: Hyde-Smith once blocked Colom’s nomination to a federal judgeship. Colom has also criticized his own party, saying, “Mississippi has never been a lost cause, we’ve been a betrayed cause.”

House · Veterans

On Veterans Day, House passes the sweeping veterans package by voice vote after Democrats fail to demand a recorded vote

+

On Wednesday afternoon, the House passed the Take Care of America’s Veterans Act (TCAVA) by voice vote. TCAVA is a package of nearly 60 veterans bills, and the House Clerk’s floor record puts its passage at about 2:46 p.m. Eastern. Rep. Addison McDowell (R-N.C.), who was presiding, brought the bill up. No Democrat demanded the recorded vote that the minority normally can force, so the package passed without one. Democrats called it a fast-track maneuver. A GOP aide said Democrats simply had no member ready at the microphone.

The package’s centerpiece raises pay for about 58,000 combat-injured, medically retired veterans. The CBO estimates the average increase at roughly $1,450 a month. The fight is over how it is paid for. Critics, including the VFW and DAV, object to a provision that would reduce future disability ratings for conditions such as sleep apnea and tinnitus, and the VFW’s national commander called the timing an insult. Veterans Affairs Chairman Mike Bost says the change applies only going forward and will not cut any current recipient’s benefits. Separately, a Democratic discharge petition reached 218 signatures and has cleared a path for a vote on a standalone Major Richard Star Act without the offset. Democrats expected their own vote as early as Thursday. TCAVA now goes to the Senate.

The Left-Leaning Take

No in-window Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion piece could be verified. Progressives such as Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez argue that Republicans are paying for one group of veterans by “gutting” benefits for others. On this view, passing the bill by voice vote on Veterans Day spared Republicans a recorded vote on cuts they would rather not defend.

The Right-Leaning Take

No in-window National Review, Dispatch or WSJ editorial piece could be verified. Conservative outlets (RedState) said Democrats “took their eye off the ball” and handed the GOP a Veterans Day win. Bost argues that the package is “the only viable path forward” for the Star Act and the largest veterans expansion in decades.

Climate · COP31

COP31 leaders’ summit opens in Antalya without the U.S., China or India, as Guterres calls for a “Climate Justice Compact”

+

The two-day high-level segment of the UN climate conference opened Wednesday in Antalya, Türkiye, with leaders and senior officials from about 100 countries. President Erdoğan opened the summit. He said the developed countries that used up most of the global carbon budget should carry more of the burden for cutting emissions and paying for the transition. He also warned that some now treat the energy transition as a threat to security and prosperity. UN Secretary-General António Guterres said the climate crisis “is outrunning us,” noting that emissions hit a record in 2025. He proposed a new “Climate Justice Compact” built on shared responsibility, security and benefits. The Turkish presidency has made electrification the summit’s main theme.

Analysis: The guest list says as much as the speeches. The leaders of the three largest emitters, the United States, China and India, are absent, and reports list several European, Canadian and Japanese leaders as staying away too, though accounts of who attended differ. Follow-up high-level sessions are set for November 17–18. Combined with the sharp drop in rich countries’ new climate-finance pledges reported this morning, the absences leave the host’s electrification agenda and Guterres’s compact with little political weight behind them. Negotiators still have until the conference closes on November 20 to produce concrete outcomes.

Courts · Press

Federal judge lets Trump’s defamation suit against the BBC over the Panorama edit go forward, finding actual malice plausibly alleged

+

On Tuesday, U.S. District Judge Jeffrey Kuntz in the Southern District of Florida denied the BBC’s motion to dismiss the defamation count in President Trump’s suit over a Panorama documentary. The program spliced together two parts of his January 6, 2021 speech that were delivered about 55 minutes apart. Kuntz rejected the BBC’s argument that a Florida court lacks jurisdiction, pointing to the broadcaster’s efforts to reach Florida viewers. He also found that the complaint plausibly alleges actual malice, meaning reckless disregard for the truth rather than mere negligence. He cited the length of the gap between the two clips and the BBC chairman’s own acknowledgment that the edit gave a misleading impression.

The judge dismissed a second count under Florida’s consumer-protection law, ruling that the documentary is not commercial speech. Reports differ on whether that cuts the headline damages figure: most still describe a $10 billion suit, while Euronews says the claim was halved. Trump called the ruling “A Huge Victory.” The BBC said it was “surprised by this decision but respect[s] the Court’s authority” and will keep fighting. A separate order requires Trump to sit for a deposition by December 11 or risk sanctions, which could include dismissal. Trump still has to prove actual malice at trial, and damages will be contested because the program did not air in Florida.

The Left-Leaning Take

No in-window Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion piece on the ruling could be verified. The press-freedom concern raised in coverage is that a sitting president is using a U.S. court to pursue a foreign public broadcaster for billions over a program that did not air in the United States. Critics also note that a motion-to-dismiss ruling only means a claim can proceed, not that it is likely to win.

The Right-Leaning Take

No in-window National Review, Dispatch or WSJ editorial piece could be verified. On the libertarian right, Reason’s Volokh Conspiracy wrote Tuesday that the ruling is legally sound at the pleading stage. Its post notes that sloppy journalism or a failure to investigate is not actual malice on its own, but that altering a quote in a way that materially changes its meaning can be.

Ukraine · Diplomacy

Abu Dhabi talks end with a large prisoner swap agreed but no breakthrough on territory, as Witkoff and Kushner head to Moscow

+

The second day of U.S.-mediated talks between Russia and Ukraine in Abu Dhabi ended Wednesday after a session of about two hours behind closed doors. Russia’s chief negotiator, Vladimir Medinsky, said the sides had agreed to a large-scale exchange of prisoners of war, with the size and timing to be announced soon. A source close to Ukraine’s team, led by Rustem Umerov, told Interfax-Ukraine that the swap was agreed after the first day. Medinsky said postwar security arrangements were a central focus. Umerov listed Kyiv’s priorities as ending hostilities, reliable security guarantees, economic recovery and humanitarian steps.

Reuters-sourced reports found no early sign of a breakthrough. Putin has not publicly dropped his demand that Ukraine give up all of Donbas. Rubio called the talks productive but cautioned that ending the war will take time. Jared Kushner said he and Steve Witkoff plan to fly to Moscow on Thursday to brief Putin, and then to meet President Zelensky in Ireland. The last Abu Dhabi round, in February, produced a 314-prisoner swap but no agreement on a ceasefire or territory.

Iran · G7

Rubio tells G7 partners in Paris there is “no need” for major combat against Iran for now, crediting sanctions and the blockade

+

Speaking after the G7 foreign ministers’ meeting in Paris on Tuesday, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the United States sees no need for major combat operations against Iran for the time being. He credited sanctions and a naval blockade that he said are cutting off Iran’s oil sales. He warned that the military stands ready if Tehran crosses U.S. “red lines.” He was also pessimistic about diplomacy, calling Iran’s conditions unrealistic and saying Tehran did not appear serious, though he allowed that this could change. His remarks follow Trump’s statement on Monday that he would hold off on strikes after appeals from regional states, including Qatar.

European ministers pressed Rubio to avoid a new escalation. French Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot said the risk “has not been completely ruled out.” According to Japan’s foreign ministry, the G7 adopted a 12-page statement that backs winter aid to Ukraine, calls for a complete ceasefire, and reaffirms opposition to forcible changes to the status quo in the Taiwan Strait and the South and East China Seas. The full text had not been published as of late Tuesday. The meeting was the first major gathering of U.S. allies since Trump’s midterm losses.

Indonesia · Politics

More than 100 Indonesian lawmakers sign a petition that could lead to Vice President Gibran’s impeachment, as Prabowo tells him to “fight”

+

Lawmakers from several parties have signed a petition that could start impeachment proceedings against Vice President Gibran Rakabuming Raka. Counts vary: Reuters, via U.S. News, says more than 100, while ThePrint says 120 from eight parties. The trigger is a pair of court rulings on his eligibility. The Supreme Court ordered the education ministry to withdraw his school-equivalence certificate, and the Constitutional Court found no evidence that he held a foreign diploma equivalent to senior high school. Under Indonesian law, the House leadership must now form a special committee to decide whether he violated the Constitution. If it finds that he did, the Constitutional Court rules on the matter.

A senior official said Gibran raised resigning at a Tuesday meeting with President Prabowo Subianto. Gibran later told reporters that Prabowo advised him to “fight” to prove his credentials, and he did not mention resignation. Analysts read the move against the backdrop of a widening rift between Prabowo and Gibran’s father, former President Joko Widodo. Because Prabowo’s coalition dominates parliament, one ANU researcher said it is hard to imagine lawmakers moving without his approval.

Nvidia falls another 5.5% as shareholders sue the company and Jensen Huang over his relationship with an alleged Chinese intelligence asset

+

Nvidia shares fell about 5.5% on Tuesday, their second straight day of losses, as fallout continued from reports about CEO Jensen Huang’s relationship with Alice Huang (also known as Huang Le). The suit was brought by investor Mark Kistenmacher, with the Rosen Law Firm handling it, and it cites the anti-fraud rules of the Securities Exchange Act.

Nvidia’s board has opened an independent inquiry. The Times reports that the board hired Wachtell Lipton, and the board says it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised. Trump said the FBI is “looking into it,” and one report says the Justice Department’s National Security Division is involved alongside Commerce. Climate · COP31

Rich countries’ new public climate-finance pledges have fallen about 70%, Carbon Brief finds, as COP31 leaders arrive in Antalya

+

A Carbon Brief analysis published as COP31’s leaders’ segment begins finds that developed countries have pledged about $11.7 billion a year in public climate finance for the rest of the decade. That is down more than 70% from roughly $40 billion a year previously: 71% by its count, or 70% if pledges to the Tropical Forest Forever Facility are included. The analysis covers the 23 developed countries obliged to provide climate finance, plus the EU, and converts multi-year pledges into annual averages. Among the G7, which has historically supplied about 80% of developed-country climate finance, only Canada and the U.K. have made new multi-year pledges. Australia, which runs the negotiations under the Turkish–Australian co-presidency, has announced none.

Developing countries are pushing for a formal climate-finance work program, while the EU’s climate commissioner reportedly called fresh demands “dead in the water.” The leaders’ segment runs November 11–12, and Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese has arrived. The host is promoting a voluntary target to make electricity 35% of global energy use by 2035. The United States has sent no official delegation.

Space · Starship

SpaceX rolls out the booster for Starship Flight 15, targeting Friday for the first attempt to catch both stages

+

SpaceX moved Super Heavy Booster 22 to the Starbase launch pad this week for stacking and a “squeeze test.” It is targeting Friday, November 13, at 5:45 p.m. EDT for Starship Flight 15, pending regulatory approval. The flight will try to recover both the booster and the Starship upper stage. If it works, it would be the first full recovery of both stages of an orbital rocket.

The test matters for NASA’s Moon plans. Under the updated Artemis architecture, Artemis III becomes a 2027 Earth-orbit test of docking between Orion and lander test vehicles from SpaceX and Blue Origin. Artemis IV, the landing mission, is planned for 2028 and depends on Starship refueling in Earth orbit. NASA’s posted timeline puts SpaceX’s in-orbit propellant-transfer demonstration in the first quarter of 2027.
