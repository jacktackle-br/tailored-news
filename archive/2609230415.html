White House · United Nations

Trump tells the UN he’ll ‘annihilate’ Iran or cut a deal after the midterms — as Cuban and Iranian envoys walk out

+

In his address to the 81st UN General Assembly on Tuesday, Sept. 22, President Trump said he faces a “big decision” on the roughly seven-month US–Iran war: strike a deal, or “annihilate the Islamic Republic” and “drive them into hell with no chance of survival and no hope of future greatness.” He predicted a deal would come only after November’s midterms — “I believe we’ll make a deal right after the election because it doesn’t make sense for them not to.” Under the banner “While Others Have Talked, I Have Acted,” he defended U.S. military force in Iran and Venezuela, said the world should encourage AI rather than “rein” it in, and declared the U.S. “totally rejects any attempt to construct a globalist scheme to control artificial intelligence.”

Members of the Cuban delegation walked out after Trump told the island “freedom will be coming,” and members of the Iranian delegation walked out at points as well; the hall applauded only politely. Fact-checkers at PBS and PolitiFact flagged several claims — on the Iran war and on AI — as misleading. On Ukraine, Trump said he believes Kyiv and Moscow “will make a deal” but offered no specifics. (Speech characterizations are drawn from wire and network coverage; casualty and support figures around the Iran war remain contested.)

Technology Policy · United Nations

Trump rejects a ‘globalist’ grip on AI — a day before the Security Council seats Altman and Amodei to weigh its dangers

+

The clearest policy line in Trump’s UN speech was on artificial intelligence: the U.S., he said, “totally rejects any attempt to construct a globalist scheme to control” AI, urging governments to encourage the technology rather than restrain it — a restatement of his administration’s deregulation-and-dominance posture at home. It set up a striking contrast the next morning. On Wednesday, Sept. 23, France convened a UN Security Council session on AI and international security — the first time the Council has directly hosted frontier AI developers.

OpenAI’s Sam Altman, Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, deep-learning pioneer Yoshua Bengio and Hugging Face’s Clément Delangue were slated to brief the 15 members, with leading Chinese developers also represented — a rare moment of U.S. and Chinese AI labs before the same body on shared safety concerns. The juxtaposition — Washington waving off international oversight while the builders themselves warn a UN chamber about catastrophic risk — is the story. (Speaker lineups for Council sessions can shift; the White House framing is from its own release.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left calls it reckless to strip away guardrails and hand the most consequential technology of the age to a handful of companies. That the labs’ own chiefs are briefing the Security Council on the dangers, they argue, is the tell: “anti-globalist” is cover for letting Big Tech govern itself. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right cheers refusing to cede AI to a UN bureaucracy and keeping the U.S. ahead of China, where over-regulation would only hand Beijing the lead. Libertarians (Reason) are wary of both a supranational regime and rule-by-executive-order; some safety-minded conservatives quietly share the worry that “no rules” is its own gamble. (Directional.)

As Pezeshkian carries Iran’s case to the UN, US and Iranian envoys hold their first face-to-face war talks

+

Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian addressed the General Assembly on Wednesday, Sept. 23 — an unusual appearance by the leader of a country at war with the United States. Hardliners at home, including the ultraconservative Kayhan, had pressed him to cancel the trip or turn the UN into “a courtroom for American crimes.” The bigger development came on the sidelines the day before: Trump envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner held a roughly three-hour meeting with Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi — the first structured, face-to-face contact of the conflict — with Qatar’s Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani mediating.

Witkoff called the talks “promising” and “constructive.” Araghchi is reported to have laid out Iran’s conditions, centered on reopening the Strait of Hormuz: an immediate lifting of the naval blockade, the release of frozen Iranian assets, and an end to the war “on all fronts,” including Lebanon. Both sides signaled another round in the near future, though no Trump–Pezeshkian meeting was scheduled. (Read-outs come largely from the parties and the Qatari mediator; conditions described are not an agreement.)

DeepSeek bets on 160,000 Huawei chips as Nvidia scrambles to keep AI from outrunning the grid

+

China’s DeepSeek plans to deploy at least 160,000 Huawei Ascend 950DT chips at a new data center in Inner Mongolia — potentially one of the largest known clusters of Huawei AI silicon — a marker of Beijing’s drive to replace Nvidia as U.S. export controls bite. The order comes with caveats: shortages of top-end memory may cap Huawei’s output and stretch fulfillment beyond a year, and DeepSeek still leans on Nvidia accelerators for the crucial training step. Even so, Huawei is now reported to hold a bigger AI-chip share than Nvidia inside China, with its software gap narrowing.

Nvidia, meanwhile, is confronting power as AI’s next bottleneck. The company has pushed back on calls to slow the build-out and is standing up an energy alliance — working with Google and power companies to make data centers flexible enough to respond to grid conditions, and helping big customers lock down land, electricity and shell capacity. The through-line of the week: compute is now gated as much by megawatts and geopolitics as by chips. (Deployment figures and timelines come from company sourcing and reporting.)

Energy · Climate Week

IEA: renewables will be about 80% of new power in 2026 — but electrification ‘by itself is not enough’

+

At Climate Week NYC on Tuesday, Sept. 22, IEA Executive Director Fatih Birol said the agency’s figures show roughly 80% of all new power plants built in 2026 will run on renewables, a few percentage points on nuclear, and the rest on fossil fuels — a milestone for clean-power deployment. But the IEA’s new report carried a pointed caveat: electrification “by itself is not enough” to hit climate targets.

If low-emission power keeps growing only in line with current policy, the agency warns, it would just about cover the extra demand from electrifying cars, heating and industry — delivering only a modest decline in emissions rather than the steep cuts the goals require. The message lands amid a COP31 electrification pledge that stops short of a firm clean-power commitment, at what organizers called the biggest Climate Week yet, with more than 1,500 events across the city. (Figures are the IEA’s own projections for 2026.)

Science · Space

NASA powers up the Roman telescope’s 300-megapixel eye, clearing a key step toward first science

+

NASA has switched on the 300-megapixel infrared camera aboard the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope — the largest camera ever flown for NASA astrophysics — capturing its first test frames (early images showed characteristic green rings of light) as the observatory works through commissioning. The Wide Field Instrument, activated in mid-September and reported this week, is built to survey vast stretches of sky at Hubble-like sharpness; Roman’s planet-hunting coronagraph also checked out as expected.

Together the milestones put the roughly $4 billion telescope a major step closer to science operations in early 2027, when it will chart dark energy, hunt exoplanets and image billions of galaxies. A bonus: a successful thruster burn plus extra fuel means Roman may have propellant for around 22 years, more than double its 10-year design life. (The instrument is now in months of calibration ahead of full operations.)

Science · Neuroscience

Brain scans give long COVID’s fog a physical culprit: injured dopamine neurons

+

Researchers at Canada’s Centre for Addiction and Mental Health (CAMH) report the first direct evidence that long COVID is associated with injury to the brain’s dopamine-releasing neurons. Using PET imaging (published in eBioMedicine and reported this week), the team found that, compared with healthy volunteers, people with long COVID had significantly lower levels of a dopamine-neuron marker across the striatum — a region central to motivation, movement and cognition — consistent with a loss of dopamine nerve-terminal density.

The imaging tracked patients’ symptoms: lower signals in the ventral striatum lined up with greater loss of motivation, and reductions in the dorsal putamen with slower movement — offering a biological handle on the chronic fatigue, brain fog and slowed thinking that define the condition. It is a small study (about 24 patients), and the group plans a clinical trial targeting dopamine function to see whether symptoms can be eased. (Early-stage findings; small sample, awaiting replication.)

Sources: ScienceDaily, Pharmacy Times, Medical Xpress, Inside Precision Medicine
