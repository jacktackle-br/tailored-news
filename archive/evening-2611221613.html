Senate · Democrats · White House

Trump says it would be “very hard” to work with Schumer as majority leader, nine days before Senate Democrats vote on Van Hollen’s challenge

+

In a Truth Social post on Sunday, President Trump said it would be very hard to work with Chuck Schumer if Schumer stays leader of the Senate Democrats when they take the majority in January. Fox News reported that Trump also predicted Schumer would “fail again.” Trump did not endorse Schumer’s challenger, Sen. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland. Van Hollen told NBC’s “Meet the Press” that he did not read the post as an endorsement, the Washington Post reported.

Senate Democrats hold the secret-ballot vote on Dec 1. It is the caucus’s first contested leadership election in more than three decades. The Post counts 16 senators publicly backing Schumer and six backing Van Hollen. Most incoming senators, and veterans such as Ron Wyden and Patty Murray, have not said how they will vote. Winning takes roughly 28 votes if every eligible Democrat votes. Schumer says he has them and points to the seats Democrats won under him. Van Hollen is running as the underdog on a message of change, with backing from Bernie Sanders and Michigan Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed.

The Left-Leaning Take

Writing in Slate, Jill Filipovic argues that a progressive challenge to Schumer shows a healthy party, not a party in disarray. She ties Van Hollen’s support to the economic populism she says powered Democrats’ midterm wins. Mother Jones is more skeptical about the outcome. It calls Van Hollen the underdog and says the race will be settled inside the caucus, not by activists. Neither piece responds to Trump’s post.

The Right-Leaning Take

National Review presents the race as Democratic leaders “facing the left” after the midterms. A post in its Corner calls Van Hollen’s bid partly a proxy fight over Israel, the first of many such fights in the new majority. From the anti-Trump right (a dissent within the right, not the mainstream-right position), The Bulwark calls it “the race everyone finds awkward”: a proxy for a long-running Democratic rift that is happening right after a strong election.

Intelligence · Russia · Ukraine

Intelligence chief Jay Clayton says Putin is “not at all” America’s friend in Ukraine, but calls Trump’s “my friend” greeting “entirely appropriate”

+

On ABC’s “This Week” on Sunday, Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton was asked about Trump’s description of Vladimir Putin as a friend at last week’s summit of the U.S., Chinese and Russian leaders in Shenzhen, on the sidelines of APEC. Clayton said the description was “entirely appropriate” at talks among the world’s three largest nuclear powers. Pressed on whether Putin is America’s friend, he said “not at all” when it comes to Ukraine, citing Russia’s invasion. Asked separately, Trump would not say whether Putin is a friend to the United States as a whole: “I can’t speak to America.”

At the summit, Trump greeted Putin with “He’s my friend” and later posted a photo of their handshake captioned “My Friend.” Trump pressed for nuclear arms control, but the leaders’ joint statement does not mention inspections or reducing stockpiles. Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk replied that Putin “is not our friend and will never be.” The exchange comes as Washington tries to arrange a new, multilateral round of Ukraine talks that would include European representatives.

The Left-Leaning Take

Slate called the Shenzhen summit “a joke.” It argues that Trump arrived with no agenda and little leverage and ended up calling both dictators “my friend,” a scene the piece says dramatized the decline of American influence. No in-window left-leaning piece on Clayton’s Sunday remarks surfaced.

The Right-Leaning Take

National Review’s summit pieces are critical but measured. They say the meeting produced few results beyond symbolism, call the joint statement “thin gruel,” and say it handed Beijing a diplomatic showcase while neither Moscow nor Beijing had any reason to accept limits on its arsenal. From the anti-Trump right (a dissent within the right), The Bulwark called the three leaders “the worst throuple on the planet” and said Putin was the summit’s “unambiguously happy camper.”

Ukraine · Russia

Russian drone collapses part of an apartment block in Dymer, near Kyiv, killing at least four, with more than a dozen people feared trapped

+

Early Sunday, a Russian drone hit a five-story residential building in Dymer, north of Kyiv, and part of it collapsed. President Zelenskiy said at least 14 people were missing and nine had been evacuated. The AP reported at least four people killed in drone attacks on the Kyiv region. Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty later put the Dymer toll at no fewer than five, including an 18-year-old. Ukraine’s emergency service said about 11 people were believed trapped. Reuters footage showed rescuers stopping their search because of an incoming aerial threat, followed by three explosions in the town.

The strike comes as diplomacy moves toward a broader format. After a Coalition of the Willing meeting in Berlin last week, Zelenskiy said Europe would be represented in future talks with Russia, and U.S. envoy Jared Kushner said Moscow had accepted multilateral talks. No date has been set. Each new strike on civilian housing adds pressure on Kyiv’s European backers for more air defense before those talks begin.

Iran · Gulf · Hormuz

Three more tankers are hit near Hormuz and Iran holds talks with Qatari mediators, as Trump says he wants a “permanent” nuclear deal

+

At least three oil tankers were attacked in or near the Strait of Hormuz on Saturday, the AP reported. Britain’s maritime security center said two were struck by projectiles while leaving the Gulf. Iran’s navy said a third hit a mine east of the strait and caught fire. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said they had hit U.S. Patriot radar sites at the Riffa and Awali bases in Bahrain, in retaliation for a U.S. strike on Iran’s Hengam Island. Bahrain rejected that account and said the target was a civilian industrial site. Neither Bahrain nor the U.S. military has confirmed any hit on the radars. Iran’s deputy foreign minister said a Qatari delegation held about two hours of talks in Tehran but that these were not yet formal negotiations. Iran still demands an end to the U.S. blockade of its ports, the release of frozen assets and the lifting of oil sanctions before it reopens the strait.

In a Fox News interview on Saturday, Trump said he would make a “permanent” deal that would keep Iran from getting a nuclear weapon even under a future “incompetent president.” Days earlier he had written that “we don’t need a deal with Iran.” CBS reported he may not decide on his next move until 2027. The pressure points are building at sea: reports say Iran has more than a dozen tankers ready near Kharg Island to test the blockade. Neither side has shown it can wait out the other, which leaves Qatar’s shuttle diplomacy as the only active channel.

Cyber · FBI · White House

FBI investigates an apparent breach of its national crime database, and Trump says it is “not good” but might turn up secrets about the Russia probe

+

The FBI is investigating an apparent intrusion into the National Crime Information Center (NCIC), the nationwide database federal, state and local police use to share information on warrants, stolen property and background checks. CNN reported the breach on Friday night, citing two people briefed on the inquiry, and the Associated Press confirmed it through an anonymous source. CNN said the intrusion was discovered “in recent days.” Neither outlet reported how the attackers got in, who they are, or whether any records were viewed or taken. The FBI declined to comment on Saturday. It is the bureau’s second reported breach since September, when the group ShinyHunters claimed to have taken data on thousands of current and former employees from an FBI jobs portal; officials have not linked the two incidents.

In a phone interview with Fox News on Saturday, President Trump said he had only just learned of the breach: “It’s not good, I don’t like that.” He then said “maybe we’ll learn things that we would have never learned otherwise,” mentioning the “Russia, Russia, Russia hoax” and voter fraud, without evidence. NCIC holds criminal-justice records, not investigative files, so it would not contain material from the Russia investigations. The breach follows a week of outages at the FAA, several banks and Microsoft services that officials have described as technical failures rather than attacks, and Trump’s firing on Thursday of National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross. Pentagon chief information officer Kirsten Davies is serving as acting director.

Separately, the Washington Post reported Saturday that Senate Republicans are poised to block at least two Trump Pentagon nominees who have waited more than a year: Austin Dahmer, for strategy, plans and capabilities, and Alex Velez-Green, both for senior posts in the Pentagon’s policy office. Neither has the votes, and neither is expected in the Senate’s final batch of confirmations this year. The resistance is aimed at Pentagon policy chief Elbridge Colby. Republican defense hawks object to the withdrawal of an Army brigade from Romania, and House Armed Services Chairman Mike Rogers accuses Colby of misleading him, which Colby denies. The White House urged confirmation “without delay”; a Pentagon official said the nominees could be put forward again next year.

Mexico · Cartels · Security

Mexico captures FBI most-wanted cartel boss “El Chapo Isidro” in Sinaloa, in an overnight battle that left 11 dead

+

Mexican forces arrested Fausto Isidro Meza Flores, known as “El Chapo Isidro,” late Friday in Guasave, Sinaloa. Mexican authorities say he leads the Beltrán Leyva organization and is allied with the “Mayos” faction of the Sinaloa Cartel. He has been on the FBI’s Ten Most Wanted list since February 2025, with a US reward of up to $5 million, and faces US drug-trafficking charges. Mexico’s Defense Ministry said Saturday that seven alleged gunmen and four soldiers were killed. A federal official told the AP that gunmen threw up at least 10 roadblocks across Guasave, and troops could not get him out until about 4am. Video showed civilians running from gunfire at a baseball game. Security Secretary Omar García Harfuch said troops will stay in Sinaloa, and the government said US intelligence helped, without giving details.

The arrest gives President Claudia Sheinbaum a high-profile result as the Trump administration presses Mexico to do more against cartels. Analysts warn that removing a kingpin can inflame a state already torn by infighting between Sinaloa factions. Reports differ on who else was detained: Newsweek names an alleged second-in-command and a financial operator, while CBS reports two lieutenants and five other suspects.

UN · AI · Arms Control

UN talks on “killer robots” end with a two-year extension and no commitment to binding rules

+

Talks in Geneva under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons ended Saturday with states agreeing to keep working on a possible instrument on lethal autonomous weapons through 2028. They set no date for formal treaty negotiations and did not say what an agreement would require. That misses UN Secretary-General António Guterres’s goal of launching negotiations in 2026. Reuters reported that about 100 countries favored moving quickly to binding rules. Under the convention’s consensus rule, a few states can block progress, and campaigners blamed Russia and the United States for watering down the outcome.

Stop Killer Robots called the result a “watered-down mandate,” and Beatrice Fihn of Lex International called it “pushing the can down the road for two more years.” Existing international humanitarian law already applies to AI-enabled weapons. The missing piece is a dedicated treaty setting limits on systems that select and engage targets without meaningful human control, and drones in Ukraine and elsewhere are moving that way faster than the diplomacy.

Climate · COP31 · Diplomacy

COP31 breaks down without a deal in Antalya, as the EU and others reject a final text that left out fossil fuels

+

The UN climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, ended Sunday without an agreement. COP31 President Murat Kurum told the closing plenary there was “no consensus” on the text, “so the decisions were not adopted.” Reuters said the talks “fell into disarray” after the European Union and others rejected the final draft. The summit was due to close Friday and ran two extra days. Reuters noted that the last comparable breakdown in UN climate talks was Copenhagen in 2009. According to the AP, the final draft did not mention fossil fuels. Its only nod was an indirect “recall” of the 2023 Dubai agreement to transition away from them, after Saudi Arabia and other oil producers objected to stronger language. Earlier drafts had already dropped a proposed $120 billion adaptation-finance target.

The text would have said for the first time that warming is on course to exceed 1.5°C, and would have called for electrifying the global economy without requiring the power to be clean. EU climate commissioner Wopke Hoekstra had said beforehand that “it is better to have no agreement than a bad agreement,” and Panama had promised to formally object. Tuvalu’s climate minister, Maina Talia, said “it seems like we are negotiating with the Saudis.” Australia’s Chris Bowen, who chaired the negotiations, said the world would keep moving away from fossil fuels whatever the outcome. What happens next procedurally was not yet clear.

Mathematics · AI

A claimed AI-assisted proof of the Riemann hypothesis passes independent machine checks, and mathematicians say they don’t yet understand it

+

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, posted a short note on Friday saying that, “working with Claude,” he believes he has found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, the 166-year-old conjecture about the distribution of prime numbers, and of its generalized form. He released it as a machine-checkable formalization in the Lean proof assistant, reportedly more than 17 million lines long, but not the AI-written human-readable explanation, which he says runs past 250,000 pages. On Saturday MIT’s Andrew Sutherland reported compiling the code independently and having Lean’s kernel recheck it using only the standard axioms, calling it “an historic moment.” Lean and Mathlib community members then issued a statement expressing confidence in the formal proof.

Mathematicians are cautious. Imperial College’s Kevin Buzzard said nobody yet understands what the proof shows. Others stress that experts still need to confirm that the formal Lean statement really matches the hypothesis as mathematicians mean it. Fields medalist James Maynard told Nature he suspects the proof will turn out to be correct. Princeton’s Maksym Radziwiłł told Science that the approach pushes the work of understanding onto everyone else. There is no peer-reviewed paper, and Scientific American separately reports competing claims that a human-produced proof may have appeared months earlier. Disclosure: this brief is compiled with Claude, the Anthropic model involved.

Chips · Nvidia · Export Controls

California’s Nvidia investigation covers possible state criminal violations over chip sales to China, Bloomberg reports

+

Bloomberg reported on Saturday that California Attorney General Rob Bonta’s investigation of Nvidia extends to possible violations of state criminal law, not just civil law. Bonta, who subpoenaed the company on Nov 19, is examining whether Nvidia or its former chief executive, Jensen Huang, helped China obtain advanced chips or confidential information, and whether the company misled investors about the risk. A state cannot enforce federal export controls, but it can use its own fraud and securities laws. Any charges would depend on what individuals knew and intended, and California could also seek disgorgement of proceeds.

The probe is preliminary, with no findings or charges, and Nvidia had not responded publicly. It adds legal pressure as the company’s board searches for a new chief executive after Huang’s departure. That departure is tied to his reported relationship with a former director of Singapore’s Megaspeed, a firm investigated over export-control violations. Bonta said the relationship and the chip sales “may or may not be connected.”

College Football · Upset Saturday

Michigan stuns unbeaten No. 4 UCLA 18–16 in triple overtime, and Utah is now the last unbeaten team in the FBS

+

Michigan beat No. 4 UCLA 18–16 in three overtimes on Senior Day in Ann Arbor, ending the Bruins’ unbeaten season and a 10-game winning streak and making the Wolverines (6–5) bowl-eligible. The winner came on the final play: a 3-yard two-point pass from Bryce Underwood, benched earlier in the season, to Jalen Hoffman. Elsewhere, Washington beat No. 6 Indiana 27–24 in overtime after deflecting a 37-yard field-goal try, and fans stormed the field. No. 2 Utah came back from a blown 17-point lead to beat TCU 38–17 with three touchdowns in the final 5:46, staying the FBS’s only unbeaten team and clinching a Big 12 title-game spot. No. 3 Notre Dame beat SMU 24–3 to improve to 10–1.
