The Iran War · War Powers

Trump declares the Iran ceasefire “over” and orders a second day of strikes as Congress’s war-powers rebuke goes ignored

+

President Trump declared the U.S.–Iran ceasefire “over” on Wednesday, July 8, while closing the NATO summit in Ankara, and U.S. Central Command carried out a second consecutive day of strikes on July 9, describing them as an effort “to further degrade Iran’s ability to attack commercial shipping and innocent civilian mariners in the Strait of Hormuz.” The exchange was triggered by an Iranian attack on tanker traffic in the strait on Tuesday, July 7. Outlets differ on scale: NBC News reported “more than 80” targets, while other desks cited roughly 90 over the two days — air-defense systems, coastal surveillance, missile and drone stores, and naval and logistics assets. Iran retaliated against U.S.-allied Gulf states and fired what the IRGC said were about 10 ballistic missiles at a U.S. air base in Jordan; Iran’s health ministry put the two-day toll at at least 14 killed and 78 wounded (figures that are government-sourced and not independently verified). Trump said “every time they hit us, we hit them 20,” called Iran’s leadership “scum,” but signaled he did not want a full-scale war and would let negotiations continue.

The renewed fighting reopens a constitutional fight. On June 23 the Senate passed a War Powers Resolution 50–48 (the House had passed it 215–208), directing the removal of U.S. forces from hostilities with Iran — the first time both chambers cleared such a measure in this conflict — with Republicans Bill Cassidy, Lisa Murkowski, Susan Collins and Rand Paul crossing over. The resolution carries no force of law, and the White House has signaled it views the 1973 War Powers Act as unconstitutional. The escalation now threatens the legislative calendar: Democrats, including Senate Armed Services ranking member Jack Reed, say they will not back the pending NDAA or a roughly $67 billion emergency defense supplemental without a formal war authorization. On the legal side, the Brennan Center and libertarian scholar Ilya Somin (Cato) argue the campaign is “very obviously a war” and unconstitutional absent congressional authorization.

The Left-Leaning Take

The left frames this as an unauthorized, unconstitutional war waged by fiat. Slate’s reporting on Congress’s short-lived revolt stressed that Trump “didn’t bother citing legal subtleties” and instead berated GOP senators privately for undercutting his leverage — presenting the war-powers fight as executive contempt for Article I. The broader reform framing (Brennan Center, ACLU) is that Trump has usurped Congress’s war powers, spent billions on a conflict lawmakers never authorized, and is now ignoring a resolution both chambers passed.

The Right-Leaning Take

Mainstream-right coverage is sympathetic to the strikes but wary of the trajectory. National Review’s “Iran Gets Back on Trump’s Bad Side” casts Iran as the aggressor that “reignited the war” by hitting Hormuz shipping and breaking the memorandum of understanding, treating the U.S. response as retribution rather than adventurism, while a companion piece questions Trump’s “appetite for humiliation” if provocations continue. Intra-right split (labeled): the non-interventionist wing dissents — Rand Paul voted for the war-powers resolution and Cato’s Ilya Somin flatly calls the war unconstitutional.

David French · NYT / The Dispatch

In an in-window broadcast appearance, French argued conservative anger at Trump over Iran “is not close to dissipating,” spotting real “fury in the Republican base” and noting the absence of any concrete details about an Iran deal or a claimed victory. Elections · Executive Power

Trump empties the bipartisan Election Assistance Commission, leaving it without a quorum months before the midterms

+

On Thursday, July 9, the administration removed the last three members of the U.S. Election Assistance Commission (EAC), the bipartisan agency Congress created after the 2000 election under the Help America Vote Act to support state and local election administration. The two Democratic commissioners, Thomas Hicks and Benjamin Hovland, were fired by email from the White House Presidential Personnel Office; the lone Republican, Christy McCormick, resigned. That leaves the four-seat commission empty and without a quorum — meaning it cannot lawfully take official actions such as certifying voting systems or amending the national mail voter-registration form until replacements are confirmed. The EAC distributes federal election funds, maintains the national registration form, tests and certifies voting equipment, and issues guidance to election officials.

The White House defended the move by asserting the president’s authority to remove officials who “may not be totally aligned” on securing elections, citing the Supreme Court’s recent Trump v. Slaughter decision expanding the president’s power to fire members of independent agencies. Context flagged by Votebeat and the Election Law Blog: in March 2025 Trump ordered the EAC to require documentary proof of citizenship on the national form, but courts blocked the main provisions as exceeding presidential authority. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer called the firings “a brazen attempt to seize control of our elections before a single vote is cast,” and Brennan Center president Michael Waldman warned the agency “cannot lawfully make any decisions that affect how Americans vote” until bipartisan replacements are seated.

That framing casts the purge as an authoritarian power grab timed to the midterms — an attempt to capture federal election machinery before votes are cast, potentially to force through the blocked proof-of-citizenship registration mandate once loyalists are installed.

2026 Elections · Maine Senate

Maine Democrats scramble for a new Senate nominee after Graham Platner exits over a rape allegation

+

Graham Platner — the populist Navy veteran and oyster farmer who had become the Democratic front-runner to challenge Sen. Susan Collins (R-ME) — suspended his campaign on Wednesday, July 8, after Politico reported an allegation from a former girlfriend that he raped her in 2021. Platner denies the allegation, which NPR notes has not been independently verified. Within hours, Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer and DSCC Chair Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand called on him to withdraw; his campaign had already been dogged by a sexting revelation, earlier abuse claims, offensive old social-media posts, and a tattoo he said he later covered.

As of July 9–10, Maine Democrats are moving to replace him. The state party voted to hold a nominating convention; under Maine law Platner must formally withdraw (a deadline reported around July 13) so Democrats can pick a new nominee by roughly July 27. Names floated across coverage include Gov. Janet Mills, Secretary of State Shenna Bellows, former Maine CDC director Dr. Nirav Shah, and former state Senate President Troy Jackson. WaPo and NPR report that Platner, “isolated,” has resisted a clean exit, and that Maine Democrats accuse his campaign of trying to influence who replaces him. A late-June poll captured the coalition tension behind his rise: Collins led him by 23 points among non-college white voters while he led by 37 among college-educated voters.

The Left-Leaning Take

The left treats the collapse as a self-inflicted vetting failure that should not discredit the underlying populism. Slate argues the episode exposes the pitfalls of “vibes-based” candidate recruiting — Democrats fell for a man they never truly vetted. The Intercept urges the party to “ditch Platner but not the politics that won over voters,” framing the scandal as a progressive vetting problem rather than an indictment of working-class, anti-establishment messaging.

The Right-Leaning Take

The right treats it as a Democratic scandal marked by hypocrisy and disarray. National Review’s “A Sniveling Graham Platner Torches the Democrats on His Way Out” mocks his grievance-laden withdrawal video as “tailor-made to sow division” among Maine progressives and casts the establishment’s embrace-then-abandonment as opportunistic — an embarrassment that helps shore up Collins in a seat Democrats badly wanted.

NATO · Ukraine & European Defense

Ankara summit closes with a €70bn Ukraine pledge and a Patriot license for Kyiv as Germany lands a Tomahawk deal

+

NATO’s two-day Ankara summit closed with allies pledging €70 billion (about $80 billion) in military equipment, assistance and training for Ukraine in 2026, plus a commitment to sustain “at least equivalent” levels in 2027. The headline deliverable for Kyiv: Trump said the U.S. will grant Ukraine a license to manufacture Patriot air-defense interceptors domestically — a long-standing Ukrainian request — saying “we’ll give them the right to make Patriots… we’ll show them how to do it,” and adding some interceptors could be sent immediately. Trump and President Zelenskyy met July 8; Trump described a “good relationship” and pledged work on a “security package,” and the Ankara Summit Declaration reaffirmed the alliance’s “ironclad commitment” to collective defense.

On the European rearmament track, Chancellor Friedrich Merz announced Germany reached a deal to buy U.S. Tomahawk cruise missiles (range roughly 1,600 km) and station them on German soil, with U.S. approval expected in August covering the missiles and ground-based launchers — a centerpiece of Berlin’s deterrence posture toward Russia. The UK said about a dozen European allies, Germany among them, would jointly spend some $50 billion over the next decade on new long-range precision-strike weapons, with Germany contributing roughly half. The subtext was the familiar friction — Trump again pressed allies on spending — but the net effect is a meaningful shift toward European-produced and European-based strike and air-defense capacity, partly de-risking Ukraine’s dependence on direct U.S. transfers.

Sources: NPR, Washington Post, TIME, NATO (Summit Declaration), Al Jazeera
Venezuela · Disaster Response

Venezuela’s quake toll rises to 3,889 as disease risk grows and the UN launches a nearly $300M appeal

+

The confirmed death toll from Venezuela’s twin earthquakes (magnitude 7.2 and 7.5, June 24) rose to at least 3,889, National Assembly chief Jorge Rodríguez said in a July 9 update. Nearly 17,000 people were injured and roughly 18,000 lost their homes; officials report 6,462 people rescued and 190 buildings collapsed, with the worst devastation in Caracas and the coastal state of La Guaira. Relief operations are large-scale — more than 9,600 metric tons of food and 12.1 million liters of water distributed, over 30,000 personnel plus tens of thousands of volunteers and nearly 4,000 international rescue workers deployed across 89 temporary camps — and the UN launched an urgent appeal on July 8 for nearly $300 million.

The fresh 24-hour angle is a shift from rescue to a public-health crisis: doctors in La Guaira’s Catia La Mar report rising skin infections and diarrhea and surging demand for chronic-illness medications as displaced people crowd into camps. A significant factual gap to flag: the official government count (3,889) sits far below the U.S. Geological Survey’s modeled estimate of an eventual toll between 10,000 and 100,000, and the figure has climbed steadily over two weeks, suggesting it remains provisional and contested.

Sources: Al Jazeera, NBC News, ABC News, Bloomberg, International Rescue Committee

Space · Astronomy

Euclid uncovers 31 ancient quasars, including the two most distant ever seen

+

The European Space Agency announced that its Euclid space telescope has discovered 31 new quasars at redshifts between 6.6 and 7.8 — light emitted when the universe was only about 670 to 800 million years old, roughly 5% of its current age. The two most distant, at redshifts z = 7.77 and z = 7.69, are the oldest quasars ever documented, and 12 of the 31 date to the universe’s first ~770 million years. Quasars are extremely luminous galactic cores powered by rapidly feeding supermassive black holes, making them beacons for studying how the earliest galaxies and black holes formed.

The haul is striking for its pace: it took astronomers more than a decade to find the first ~10 quasars at z ≥ 7, whereas Euclid surpassed that in roughly a single year of survey observations. The results appear in the journal Astronomy & Astrophysics. Euclid, launched in 2023, is primarily a dark-energy and dark-matter mission whose wide, deep imaging is proving unexpectedly powerful for rare-object discovery.

Sources: NASA Science, ESA, Astronomy & Astrophysics, Imperial College London
AI · Chips

Meta to put its in-house “Iris” AI chip into production in September, aiming to double compute and cut Nvidia reliance

+

According to a Reuters exclusive based on an internal memo, Meta plans to begin mass production in September of its latest custom data-center AI accelerator, code-named “Iris” — the newest part in its MTIA (Meta Training and Inference Accelerator) program. The chip is designed with Broadcom and built by TSMC on a 3nm process, and Meta says internal testing took only about six weeks with no major issues. The stated goals are to roughly double the company’s computing capacity, lower GPU spending amid an industry-wide component shortage, and reduce dependence on Nvidia.

Reporting on the memo indicates Meta intends to accelerate to a new chip generation roughly every six months, with future parts referenced as MTIA v4 (“Santa Barbara”) and v5 (“Olympus”) in 2027 and v6 (“Universal Core”) in 2028. The move lands against a broader semiconductor backdrop of record chip sales even as AI-chip equities saw sharp volatility in early July — part of a wider push by hyperscalers to design their own silicon and blunt Nvidia’s pricing power.

Sources: TechCrunch, Reuters (via Yahoo Finance), The Next Web, Electronics Weekly
AI · Models

OpenAI makes GPT-5.6 generally available in three tiers — Sol, Terra and Luna

+

OpenAI released its GPT-5.6 model family into general availability on July 9, after a June 26 preview. The lineup has three tiers: Sol (the new flagship), Terra (a balanced everyday model), and Luna (a low-cost, fast option). API pricing per million tokens runs $5 input / $30 output for Sol, $2.50 / $15 for Terra, and $1 / $6 for Luna, with OpenAI positioning Terra as matching prior GPT-5.5 performance at roughly half the cost. A premium “Sol Fast” tier ($12.50 / $75) serves the flagship at up to ~750 tokens per second on Cerebras hardware.

Alongside the model launch, GPT-5.6 introduces developer-facing changes including programmatic tool calling in the Responses API and more predictable prompt caching (explicit cache breakpoints, a 30-minute minimum cache lifetime, cache writes billed at 1.25× the uncached input rate, and cache reads keeping the roughly 90% discount). The release sharpens an increasingly crowded frontier-API field heading into the second half of 2026.

Energy · Solar & Storage

U.S. solar keeps booming despite tariffs and tax changes, with battery storage scaling fast behind it

+

Inside Climate News’s “Inside Clean Energy” reports the U.S. is on track to add roughly 43,000 MW of utility-scale solar this year — about a 45% jump over 2025 — even amid new tariffs and federal tax-policy changes. That intermittent solar is increasingly buffered by rapid battery growth: EIA data show developers planning about 43.4 GW of new utility-scale solar in 2026 (a ~60% increase in annual additions) and roughly 24 GW of new utility-scale battery storage, up from a record ~15 GW in 2025.

In the aggregate EIA outlook, solar and storage make up the bulk of a record ~86 GW of planned 2026 U.S. utility-scale additions — solar around 51%, batteries around 28% — with solar, wind and storage accounting for the overwhelming majority of all new capacity. EIA projects total U.S. battery storage rising from about 44.6 GW toward 67+ GW by early 2027. The throughline: clean-energy buildout momentum is continuing despite a less favorable policy environment.

Sources: Inside Climate News, U.S. EIA, Electrek, PV Tech

Wimbledon · Semi-finals

Muchová survives Gauff in a 12-10 tiebreak thriller to set up an all-Czech final

+

Karolína Muchová ended Coco Gauff’s Wimbledon on Thursday, July 9, winning a rollercoaster semi-final 6-2, 1-6, 7-6(10) on Centre Court, taking the deciding tiebreak 12-10. Gauff held a match point at 9-8 but netted a forehand; Muchová saved it and converted after an earlier missed match point when she slipped on the grass. It is Muchová’s first Wimbledon final and makes her the fourth Czech woman to reach the final in six years. In the other semi, ninth seed Linda Nosková beat Ukraine’s Marta Kostyuk 6-4, 6-4 to reach her maiden Grand Slam final.

That sets up an all-Czech women’s final between Muchová and Nosková on Saturday, July 11 — the first women’s final contested by two players from the same nation since the Williams sisters in 2009. On the men’s side, both semi-finals are scheduled for Friday, July 10: British wild card Arthur Fery faces Roland Garros champion Alexander Zverev, followed by top seed and defending champion Jannik Sinner against seven-time champion Novak Djokovic. Carlos Alcaraz missed the tournament recovering from a wrist injury.
