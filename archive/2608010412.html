National Security · War Powers

The Senate again refuses to halt Trump’s Iran war, 49–50, as Fetterman crosses to the GOP and strikes resume in a fifth month

+

On Thursday, July 30, the Senate voted 49–50 to reject a war-powers resolution that would have directed the president to withdraw U.S. forces from hostilities with Iran absent congressional authorization, invoking the 1973 War Powers Resolution. Sen. John Fetterman (D-Pa.) again crossed to vote with Republicans — reported as at least his third such vote on Iran — while three Republicans, Susan Collins (Maine), Lisa Murkowski (Alaska) and Rand Paul (Ky.), joined Democrats in support. The war, now in its fifth month after a June ceasefire collapsed, has never been authorized by Congress; the administration notified Congress on July 10 that it viewed hostilities as resumed, treating that as the start of a fresh 60-day window, and has asserted Article II commander-in-chief authority (per Lawfare’s tracking of the White House war-powers reports).

The vote landed as U.S. Central Command reported a “heavy wave of strikes” overnight and officials signaled more over the weekend. At a Cabinet meeting Trump said, “We’ll be hitting them very hard… at some point, they’re going to say, ‘We just can’t take it anymore,’” and has separately called a 60-day approval requirement “totally unconstitutional.” Polling cited in the coverage put approval of his handling of the conflict around 28%, with rising gas prices a recurring theme. Casualty counts remain contested across outlets and are disputed by the parties.

The Left-Leaning Take

Congress is abdicating its Article I war power to bless an open-ended, unauthorized “forever war.” With polls showing roughly two-thirds of Americans wanting the strikes to stop, Fetterman is cast as a spoiler enabling escalation and self-inflicted economic pain at the pump. (Synthesized from The New Republic, Common Dreams, Democracy Now.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The president holds Article II authority to act against an existential nuclear threat, and war-powers resolutions improperly tie the commander-in-chief’s hands; Fetterman frames his vote as “pro-no-nuclear-Iran,” not pro-war. Intra-right dissent: Rand Paul’s “yes” reflects the party’s libertarian, non-interventionist wing (a Reason-style skepticism of open-ended war), and some Republicans are voicing “forever war” fears.

Sources: The Hill, Roll Call, CNN, The New Republic, Lawfare
Elections · Voting Law

Trump demands Thune scrap the Senate’s August recess to force through the SAVE Act — but the votes aren’t there, and the leader won’t budge

+

Since Monday, July 27, President Trump has escalated demands that Majority Leader John Thune (R-S.D.) keep the Senate in session through its August recess until it passes the SAVE America Act (Safeguard American Voter Eligibility). The bill would require documentary proof of citizenship — typically a passport or birth certificate — to register for federal elections, add photo-ID and voter-roll cross-checks against federal databases, and is one Trump calls a 2026 priority that would “guarantee the midterms.” Republicans hold 53–47, but the bill needs 60 votes to break a filibuster and has no Democratic support. Thune rebuffed him — “Show me how this ends” — noting the chamber has already voted on it several times.

At least 14 senators, led by Mike Lee (R-Utah), back cancelling the recess; others, including Susan Collins, say staying in “doesn’t change what would happen on the SAVE Act.” Trump also pressed to abolish the legislative filibuster outright, which Thune rejected.

The Left-Leaning Take

The bill is voter suppression dressed as fraud prevention. It would disenfranchise millions who lack ready passport or birth-certificate access — including married women who changed their names, and rural and low-income voters — effectively end online and mail registration, and hand federal agencies an election-surveillance role, all to solve a near-nonexistent problem. (Synthesized from the Brennan Center, Common Dreams.)

The Right-Leaning Take

A genuine intra-right split. The Wall Street Journal editorial board backs the bill’s goal but calls nuking the filibuster to pass it “a terrible trade” that would later let progressives “pack the Supreme Court and create new states”; the Washington Examiner agrees the GOP is “doomed” if it blows up the filibuster. The populist right (The Federalist) counters that senators use the filibuster “as an excuse to do nothing for voters,” while The Dispatch doubts the citizenship rule would even help Republicans.

Sources: PBS NewsHour, The Hill, WSJ (editorial), Brennan Center, The Federalist

Middle East · US–Iran War

The US–Iran war widens into a fifth month, drawing in Egypt, Jordan and the Gulf as strikes resume and diplomatic signals stay contradictory

+

After a brief lull meant to revive talks, the U.S. launched what officials called a “heavy wave of strikes” on Iran this week — explosions were reported across southern Iran, including Bandar Abbas and Kish island — after Iran fired missiles at U.S. assets in Jordan. Iran said U.S. strikes killed three of its soldiers on Thursday; at least four U.S. service members have been reported killed in the past week (three in Jordan, one in a drone detonation in Iraq). The conflict keeps spreading: Jordan intercepted Iranian missiles on consecutive days, Kuwait reported one person killed, and for the first time Egypt was hit — drone strikes on the Mediterranean port of Damietta set fires on two gas vessels. Iran denied responsibility for the Egypt strike and no party claimed it. Saudi Arabia said 14 countries had backed its proposed “Maritime Defense Alliance” to protect shipping.

Diplomatic signals are contradictory. Pakistan, mediating, said U.S.–Iran talks are “ongoing,” particularly over the Strait of Hormuz; Iran’s foreign-ministry spokesman said Tehran is not currently negotiating and “will never allow the United States to determine the timing of war and peace.” Casualty tallies diverge sharply by outlet — from roughly 17–18 U.S. deaths to figures ranging into the thousands overall — and should be treated as contested. (See Politics for the domestic fight over the war’s legality.)

Sources: CNN, Bloomberg, NBC News, CBS News, The Hill
Middle East · Gaza Diplomacy

Trump announces a “complete disarmament” deal for Hamas via his “Board of Peace” — but Hamas conditions it on Israel moving first, and Israel stays silent

+

On Friday, July 31, President Trump announced an agreement for “the COMPLETE DISARMAMENT of Hamas and all other armed groups in Gaza,” casting it as a breakthrough in implementing his 20-point plan, brokered by a U.S.-led “Board of Peace.” As he described it, Israeli forces would withdraw “as disarmament is completed,” with an International Stabilization Force working alongside a new Palestinian police force; a Board of Peace official said a detailed disarmament roadmap would be drawn up within 14 days, subject to an international verification body.

The two sides describe materially different deals. Senior Hamas official Ghazi Hamad said the group would disarm only if Israel first ceases fire, halts targeted killings, withdraws to the “Yellow Line,” and permits a surge of humanitarian aid consistent with the October ceasefire. Israel had not publicly responded, and Trump did not detail the sequencing of the reciprocal steps — leaving a real gap between an announced agreement and a mutually confirmed one. Germany’s foreign minister called the news “hopeful.”

Sources: NPR, CBS News, CNN, CNBC, Al Jazeera
Europe & North Africa · Migration

At least 57 die as tens of thousands surge from Morocco into Spain’s enclave of Ceuta, and Madrid deploys the military

+

An estimated 50,000-plus people crossed — many swimming around the sea barrier at Tarajal — into the small Spanish exclave of Ceuta this week after the frontier with Morocco was breached. At least 57 died, by drowning and in a stampede at the breakwater fence. The regional president declared an “absolute humanitarian and social emergency,” Madrid deployed troops, and France reinforced its own border; Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez visited Friday.

Spain said most of those who crossed had returned to Morocco by Friday evening, though thousands — including unaccompanied children — were left sleeping in parks and streets. The death toll rose sharply as the story developed (earlier reports cited 18, then 34, before settling at 57), and the episode again spotlighted Rabat’s leverage over migration flows, echoing the 2021 Ceuta crisis.

Sources: NPR, Al Jazeera, CNN, CBS News, TIME

Semiconductors · AI

AI-chip stocks resume a steep slide as a new Chinese model and doubts over AI spending rattle the sector

+

Chip stocks extended a rout into Friday, July 31. The SMH semiconductor ETF — the 25 largest U.S. chipmakers — was down about 9.5% over a month; Nvidia fell roughly 2.2% on Friday and TSMC closed down more than 3%, while the Nasdaq lost 1.4% and the S&P 500 nearly 1%. An earlier tally put cumulative losses across chip names — including SK Hynix, Samsung and SoftBank — above $1 trillion during the selloff. TSMC’s Thursday earnings beat on profit and revenue but flagged higher-than-forecast capital spending, adding to the jitters over whether the AI build-out can sustain its pace.

Solar · Grid

A West Texas solar hub crosses 1 GW — halfway to a 2 GW-plus complex being built for data centers

+

On July 30, Origis Energy said it had brought roughly 500 MW of new solar capacity online across three phases at its Rockhound complex in Ector County, Texas, lifting the site to nearly 1 gigawatt. The company plans to add about another gigawatt of solar and battery storage, targeting more than 2 GW by 2029, with the next phase marketed for data-center co-location and long-term corporate offtake. The complex already holds long-term contracts with Meta, Occidental, dsm-firmenich and Bekaert.

The milestone is a marker of how surging data-center demand — much of it driven by AI — is now pulling utility-scale solar-plus-storage buildout, with developers pairing panels and batteries directly to large corporate loads rather than selling only into the wholesale grid.

Quantum · Computing

An 18-qubit silicon chip runs its own error correction inside the cryostat, lifting the “underdog” spin-qubit approach

+

In a paper published in Nature on July 29, HRL Laboratories demonstrated an 18-qubit silicon spin quantum processor that operates without real-time control from room-temperature electronics. It replaces racks of external instruments with a custom cryogenic CMOS control chip running at 4 kelvin, linked to the sub-kelvin qubits by a 296-channel superconducting ribbon cable. The device reported average single-qubit gate errors around 0.017% and two-qubit (CNOT) errors around 0.35% — roughly ten times lower control error than prior demonstrations of this qubit type.

Crucially, it executed quantum error-detection and repetition codes with zero room-temperature latency, showing about fivefold error suppression as qubits were added. The result matters because silicon spin qubits — long an underdog to superconducting and trapped-ion designs — ride on the same semiconductor manufacturing that already makes conventional chips, a potential advantage for scaling.

Sources: Nature, The Quantum Insider, Tech Times
Astronomy · Stars

Astronomers capture the clearest image yet of Betelgeuse’s companion, confirming a century-old hunch

+

A team led by Miguel Montargès of the Observatoire de Paris used the SPHERE instrument on the European Southern Observatory’s Very Large Telescope to directly image the long-suspected companion of Betelgeuse, the red supergiant in Orion. The detection reached 6.1-sigma significance — past the 5-sigma threshold physicists treat as confirmation — and was independently confirmed by two post-processing methods.

The companion is estimated at two to three times the Sun’s mass, more than earlier predictions of roughly one solar mass. A companion had been proposed about a century ago to help explain Betelgeuse’s periodic dimming, watched for more than a millennium; the image gives firm evidence that the famous star is not alone.

Sources: Observatoire de Paris, Space.com, Sci.News
