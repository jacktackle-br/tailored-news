The Iran War · Widening

Iran fires on a base in Jordan and the U.S. strikes near the Bushehr nuclear plant as Trump calls diplomacy “a waste of time”

+

The conflict widened Thursday, July 9, beyond the pattern reported this morning. For the first time in this round, Iran’s Revolutionary Guards fired ballistic missiles at Jordan — targeting the Azraq air base, which houses U.S. forces. Jordan’s military said the incoming missiles were intercepted with no casualties or damage; accounts of the number diverged, with Amman’s state news agency citing eight missiles and Israeli outlets reporting ten. Sirens sounded across the kingdom. Iran, meanwhile, said U.S. projectiles hit the perimeter of the Bushehr nuclear power plant — the country’s only operating reactor — a claim carried by Iran’s Fars, Mehr and IRNA agencies; the strikes near a nuclear site mark a sharp escalation, though there is no indication the reactor itself was damaged.

President Trump hardened his line at the close of the NATO summit in Ankara, calling further negotiations with Tehran “a waste of time” and saying he was unsure he even wanted a peace agreement — a firmer stance than this morning’s mixed signals on whether talks might continue. The escalation is now colliding with Congress: the fight over the White House’s $87.6 billion Iran-war supplemental (about $67 billion of it for the Pentagon) is threatening to derail the fall legislative agenda. Sen. Jack Reed (D-R.I.), ranking member on Armed Services, warned Democrats will not vote to “subsidize” a war Congress never authorized, and Rep. Pat Ryan is leading House Democratic veterans in a bid to prohibit war funding — even as a June War Powers Resolution directing an end to hostilities remains, on the domestic-law reading Lawfare has tracked, legally unheeded.

The Left-Leaning Take

The anti-war and constitutional left casts the widening as the predictable cost of an unauthorized “war of choice”: the Brennan Center argues the strikes are unconstitutional and that Congress’s war-powers vote has been simply ignored, and Democrats from Reed to Pat Ryan are moving to choke off funding until Trump seeks authorization. Chuck Schumer’s standing line — “maximum confusion, maximum chaos, maximum cost” — frames the escalation as recklessness, not strategy. But the restraint and libertarian right — the impulse behind Rand Paul’s vote for the war-powers resolution, and Reason’s war-powers skepticism — warns against an open-ended Gulf war Trump himself won’t define an endgame for; treat that as an intra-right disagreement, not the hawkish consensus. Washington · Monuments

Trump’s 250-foot “triumphal arch” near Arlington clears a federal panel after three hours of public objection

+

The National Capital Planning Commission on Thursday, July 9, advanced President Trump’s proposed triumphal arch after hearing nearly three hours of public opposition — a key procedural win for a project the president wants to break ground on soon. The planned structure would rise about 250 feet at Memorial Circle near the Potomac approach to Arlington National Cemetery: more than twice the height of the 99-foot Lincoln Memorial. The commission advanced it while recommending the design be lowered to comply with the federal Height of Buildings Act, which limits building heights in the capital.

Opponents — including veterans, Gold Star families and historians — argue the arch is out of scale and would sever the deliberately engineered sightline between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington, a vista meant to symbolize post–Civil War reunification. A group of veterans and a historian have sued in federal court to block construction over the sightline disruption. Some Republicans, such as Rep. James Comer, have voiced support for the plan.

The Left-Leaning Take

The left reads the arch as a self-aggrandizing vanity monument — a “triumphal” arch with no triumph behind it — whose placement literally overshadows a cemetery of the war dead and desecrates a sightline built to honor national reconciliation. Critics tie its imperial-Roman symbolism to the administration’s broader strongman aesthetics and question the cost. (Analytical framing from in-window reporting; no dedicated left opinion column surfaced in-window.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right is largely quiet, and where it engages it is split. Boosters like Rep. Comer frame the arch as a fitting monument to American greatness. But fiscal and traditionalist conservatives — the instinct Reason and small-government voices tend to voice — balk at a costly federal edifice near hallowed ground and at overriding the capital’s height law by fiat; treat that as an intra-right reservation. NATO · Ukraine

NATO’s Ankara summit closes with a Patriot production license for Ukraine and a €70bn arms pledge

+

The NATO summit that framed this morning’s Syria news wrapped Wednesday–Thursday in Ankara with concrete deliverables for Ukraine. President Trump told President Zelensky the United States will grant Ukraine a license to manufacture Patriot interceptor missiles — transferring technical production rights for the air-defense system to address Ukraine’s estimated shortfall of some 2,000 interceptors a year against Russia’s intensifying ballistic-missile campaign. The alliance pledged €70 billion in military equipment, assistance and training for 2026 (Kyiv Post framed the multi-year commitment nearer €140 billion), affirmed sustaining at least equivalent levels in 2027, reaffirmed its Article 5 commitment, and unveiled arms deals worth at least $50 billion. Host President Erdogan called the gathering “historic.”

The Patriot license is strategically significant but not an immediate fix: analysts note that component lead times, assembly, testing and certification mean domestically produced interceptors will not materially increase Ukraine’s supply for months. Its value is longer-term — reducing Kyiv’s dependence on a scarce U.S.-controlled pipeline — and symbolic, signaling that even amid the Iran war Washington is deepening rather than trimming its commitment to Ukraine’s air defense.

The Iran War · Escalation

U.S. and Iran trade strikes for a second day as Trump calls the ceasefire “over” and Iran hits Gulf bases

+

For a second straight day into Thursday, July 9, U.S. and Iranian forces exchanged strikes, pushing last month’s fragile ceasefire — formalized in what outlets call the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding — to the edge of collapse. U.S. Central Command said American forces hit roughly 90 targets along Iran’s coastline overnight (after more than 80 on July 7), framing the operation as intended “to further degrade” Iran’s ability to threaten “freedom of navigation in the Strait of Hormuz” and saying the U.S. was “holding Iran accountable for recent unjustified aggression against commercial shipping.” Iran’s Revolutionary Guards retaliated against U.S. military bases in Bahrain and Kuwait with missiles and drones; some desks (NBC, PBS) also placed Qatar among the targets, a point not uniform across outlets. Thursday’s reporting described the deepest U.S. strikes into Iran since March and the first to hit transport infrastructure — the Aq Tekeh Khan bridge in Golestan province and the Gorgan rail line. Iran’s health ministry put the two-day toll at at least 14 killed and 78 wounded, and U.S. gasoline prices jumped about five cents to near $3.85 a gallon, the largest one-day gain since May.

The trigger was President Trump’s declaration Wednesday that the ceasefire was “over” — roughly three weeks after he had described the deal as an “unconditional surrender” (Axios documented the whiplash). He warned the strikes would “get much worse” if Iran attacked more ships in the strait; outlets diverged on whether he foreclosed diplomacy, some reporting he “dismissed further negotiations” and others that he would let talks continue. The core dispute is legal-textual: Iran reads the MOU’s Hormuz clause as giving Tehran authority over traffic management, while Washington says it only obliges Iran not to impede transit. On the domestic-law side — where Lawfare’s war-powers coverage is the spine — Congress had already become the first in modern history to pass a War Powers Resolution directing a president to end hostilities (House 215–208 on June 3; Senate 50–48 in late June, with Republicans Cassidy, Murkowski, Collins and Paul crossing over). Rep. Ro Khanna (D-Calif.) called the new strikes “a blatant violation of the War Powers Resolution.”

The Left-Leaning Take

The anti-war and constitutional left casts this as a reckless, unauthorized “war of choice” in which the president has usurped Congress’s war powers — the Brennan Center’s standing analysis argues the 60-day clock and the War Powers Resolution have both been ignored, and progressives point to the House and Senate votes as proof that a bipartisan majority already told Trump to stop. (No dedicated Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion column specifically on the July 8 collapse surfaced within the window; the framing above is analytical, not a fresh column, and none is invented here.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The right remains split. The hawkish mainstream treats the strikes as a proportionate defense of freedom of navigation after Iran attacked civilian shipping. But the restraint/libertarian right — Reason on war-powers grounds, and a National Review that has been skeptical of Trump’s shifting Iran posture — warns against being dragged into another open-ended Gulf war; treat that as an intra-right disagreement, not the hawkish consensus. (No fresh July 8–9 National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason piece on the collapse surfaced in the window; the standing positions are flagged as such and none is invented.)

Syria · U.S. Diplomacy

Trump moves to strike Syria off the U.S. terrorism-sponsor list for the first time since 1979

+

On July 8, Secretary of State Marco Rubio formally notified Congress that the administration intends to rescind Syria’s designation as a State Sponsor of Terrorism — a label in place since 1979 under Hafez al-Assad. Trump signaled the move to reporters the same day after meeting Syrian President Ahmed al-Sharaa on the sidelines of the NATO summit in Ankara. Under the process, the rescission takes effect after a 45-day congressional notification period and becomes final unless lawmakers vote to block it — considered unlikely, given that the GOP-controlled Congress already repealed Caesar Act sanctions on Syria last year.

The State Department framed the decision around Trump’s June 2025 executive order directing a review, “the positive changes and counterterrorism actions taken by the Syrian government,” and “formal assurances” from al-Sharaa that Syria “will not support acts of international terrorism in the future.” It is the most significant normalization step yet toward the post-Assad government led by al-Sharaa — a former militant leader — and is intended to unlock international trade and investment for reconstruction.

DR Congo · Global Health

Ebola death toll in eastern Congo hits 600, up 100 in three days, as the vaccine-less strain keeps spreading

+

WHO figures published Thursday show the Democratic Republic of the Congo’s Ebola outbreak has now killed 600 people out of 1,759 confirmed cases since it was declared in mid-May — a milestone reached just three days after the toll passed 500, underscoring an accelerating epidemic. The most recent 24-hour reporting period logged 51 new cases and 20 new deaths. The outbreak spans four northeastern provinces but is concentrated in Ituri.

It is driven by the rare Bundibugyo species of Ebola, for which there are no approved vaccines or treatments — a critical difference from the Zaire-strain outbreaks that had licensed countermeasures. A clinical trial of two candidate therapies, the monoclonal antibody MBP134 and the antiviral remdesivir, began in the DRC on July 2. The response is being hampered on the ground: healthcare workers in hard-hit Ituri have threatened to walk off the job over unpaid wages, a serious risk to containment in a region already strained by eastern Congo’s broader conflict and displacement.

Earth Science · Geophysics

Scientists capture a seafloor-spreading event as it happens, watching new ocean crust form

+

A study published in Nature on July 8 (“Anatomy of a seafloor spreading event captured by in situ seismogeodesy”) presents the first direct, in-situ observation of a mid-ocean-ridge spreading event as it happened. Instruments had been deployed across the axis of the Southeast Indian Ridge near 37°S and the nearby Amsterdam transform fault two months before the event began on April 26, 2024, letting researchers record the rifting in unprecedented detail.

The event produced roughly four meters of subsidence of the rift-valley floor and more than a meter of horizontal extension across the valley, accompanied by large lava outflows, as two tectonic plates pulled apart and fresh oceanic crust was created. The team combined hydroacoustic monitoring, direct-path acoustic ranging, bottom-pressure measurements and repeated seafloor mapping. Because spreading on the ocean floor is far harder to observe than on land, the measurements offer the clearest look yet at how the seafloor widens and how new crust is born at a mid-ocean ridge.

Sources: Nature, Scientific American, Phys.org, ScienceAlert

Biomedicine · Oncology

EU regulators fast-track Revolution Medicines’ pancreatic-cancer pill under a phased review

+

The European Medicines Agency’s human-medicines committee (CHMP) has begun a phased, accelerated review of daraxonrasib, Revolution Medicines’ investigational oral RAS(ON) multi-selective inhibitor for pancreatic cancer — announced in a company release dated July 7 and carried into STAT’s July 8 biotech coverage. A phased review lets regulators assess data in stages as it becomes available, ahead of a full marketing-authorization application, compressing the timeline for a drug in an area of high unmet need.

The decision rests on results from the pivotal Phase 3 RASolute 302 trial, which the company says showed improvements in overall survival and progression-free survival versus standard chemotherapy in patients with previously treated metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, alongside a manageable safety profile and delayed deterioration in cancer-related pain and quality of life. Daraxonrasib already holds EU orphan-drug designation for pancreatic cancer and has been flagged as a high priority under the EMA’s Cancer Medicines “Pathfinder” initiative.
