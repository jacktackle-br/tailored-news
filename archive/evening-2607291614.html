The Senate · A State Funeral

Washington buries Lindsey Graham — Trump eulogizes a “true American original” as Netanyahu and Zelensky fill the pews

+

Two days of memorials for Sen. Lindsey Graham — the four-term South Carolina Republican who died July 11 at 71, with preliminary medical findings pointing to an aortic dissection — culminated Tuesday, July 28, in a funeral at Washington National Cathedral. President Trump delivered a eulogy calling Graham “a respected statesman, a giant of the United States Senate and a true American original who left us much too soon,” recalling a relationship that began as adversarial — Trump read out Graham’s cell number at a 2015 rally — before turning into a decade-long friendship. Trump also nodded at Graham’s reputation as a hawk, saying “he never saw a war that he didn’t like.”

The service doubled as an unofficial diplomatic summit: Vice President Vance, Secretary of State Rubio, Defense Secretary Hegseth and much of the Cabinet attended alongside Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky, both in Washington partly to mourn a senator who had been a fixture of their capitals (Graham had returned from Kyiv shortly before his death). A separate Capitol Hill tribute preceded the cathedral service, and Graham was to be returned to South Carolina for burial.

The Left-Leaning Take

Progressive commentary met the eulogies with ambivalence: real acknowledgment of Graham’s legislative skill and stature, paired with a pointed reminder that his defining cause was near-reflexive military intervention — the very “never saw a war he didn’t like” line Trump offered as praise. Critics on the left read the funeral’s guest list — a sitting Israeli premier and Ukrainian president amid an active Iran war — as a fitting coda to a career spent urging American force abroad. (No in-window signed Vox, Slate, Mother Jones or Guardian column dedicated to the funeral was located — provisional; framing drawn from wire and cable coverage.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Mainstream conservatives eulogized Graham warmly as a Senate technician and steadfast ally of Israel who “punched above his weight,” downplaying the war-hawk label in favor of his constituent work and his late-career loyalty to Trump. But the moment also exposed a live intra-right fault line: the ascendant restraint wing — the same anti-interventionist current animating Trump’s public feud with Tucker Carlson over the Iran campaign — has spent months arguing Graham’s brand of foreign policy is exactly what the movement should bury with him. (No single in-window signed National Review, WSJ editorial or Dispatch column on the funeral was located — provisional.)

Immigration · Federalism & the Courts

A day after a judge forced open a detention center, ICE drafts contract language declaring state and local laws “shall not apply”

+

Federal procurement documents reviewed by WIRED show ICE moving to wall its private detention network off from state inspectors. On July 9, U.S. District Judge Benjamin Settle ordered the operator of the Northwest ICE Processing Center in Tacoma, Washington, to admit state health officials, ruling that a federal contract cannot override state law — “preemption requires congressional intent, not a contract.” The very next day, ICE posted draft contract terms — mirroring the disputed Tacoma language — declaring that state and local laws “shall not apply” to the facilities and limiting external site inspections to federal personnel and ICE-approved third parties. The terms target four facilities totaling roughly 5,500 beds.

The maneuver squarely tees up a Supremacy Clause fight. As Lawfare and other legal analysts have laid out, Washington will likely invoke the “exclusivity doctrine” — that only the federal government may regulate the admission and removal of noncitizens — while states counter that health-and-safety inspection is a traditional police power that Arizona v. United States did not sweep away, and that a procurement clause cannot manufacture preemption Congress never enacted. Judge Settle’s ruling suggests at least one court has already rejected the contract-as-preemption theory the new terms rely on.

The Left-Leaning Take

The left reads this as the administration trying to place a rapidly expanding detention system beyond any independent oversight — drafting contract boilerplate to nullify state health inspections the day after losing in court, precisely so conditions inside privately run facilities stay out of public and state view. Immigrant-rights advocates and state officials frame it as an end-run around a judicial loss and a template to be copied nationwide. (No in-window signed Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian column on the draft terms was located — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The federal-supremacy case — advanced by the administration and sympathetic legal conservatives — is that immigration detention is an exclusively federal function, and that letting blue-state inspectors set terms inside ICE facilities would let hostile localities throttle enforcement Congress assigned to Washington. On the libertarian right, the instinct cuts the other way: Reason-style skeptics of unaccountable detention are wary of any contractor shielded from all outside inspection, a discomfort with opaque state power that scrambles the usual lines. (No single in-window signed National Review or WSJ editorial on the draft terms was located — provisional.)

The Iran War · Escalation

The five-day calm breaks: Iran fires on a U.S. base in Jordan, and U.S. and Saudi jets strike Iran-backed militias in Iraq

+

The lull that followed Trump’s Friday suspension of a 13-night U.S. bombing campaign collapsed on Wednesday, July 29. Iran’s Revolutionary Guard fired ballistic missiles at Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan, which hosts U.S. forces; Jordan’s military said it intercepted and destroyed five Iranian missiles, and U.S. officials described the barrage as a foiled “surprise attack.” In response, the United States and Saudi Arabia carried out coordinated “limited, targeted” strikes on Iran-aligned militia logistics and weapons sites across eastern Iraq, tied to more than 30 IRGC-directed drone attacks over the prior 72 hours and to strikes on Saudi energy infrastructure. Iraq’s Popular Mobilization Forces said at least 20 fighters were killed and 32 wounded; several Iranian Revolutionary Guard advisers were reported among the dead.

The renewed fighting reopened the war on multiple fronts and rattled energy markets: Iran’s Guard claimed to have halted three oil tankers in the Strait of Hormuz, and crude jumped roughly 7% at one point. Diplomacy meanwhile stalled — Tehran rejected an Omani proposal to jointly manage Hormuz traffic by splitting it into two lanes with 50% of shipping each. Iraqi armed groups condemned a “dangerous escalation,” and the strikes marked the first major U.S. military action in the region since the Friday pause, raising the prospect that a conflict many hoped was winding down is instead widening to draw in Baghdad, Amman and Riyadh.

Diplomacy · The White House

Two wars, two visitors: Netanyahu talks Iran’s nuclear program and Zelensky presses for Patriot interceptors in back-to-back Oval Office meetings

+

With both leaders in Washington for Lindsey Graham’s funeral, Trump held separate Oval Office meetings on Tuesday, July 28, with Netanyahu and Zelensky. The Netanyahu session ran nearly 90 minutes and centered, the prime minister said, on “the common goal of ensuring that Iran does not have nuclear weapons” — a pointed subject as the U.S.–Iran war reignited hours later. The two, who last met on the sidelines of a NATO summit in Ankara earlier in July, were also expected to keep probing a pathway to end the multiyear war with Russia. Taken together, the back-to-back sessions underscored how completely the two theaters — Iran and Ukraine — now dominate Trump’s foreign-policy calendar, even on a day built around a funeral.

Japan · Disaster

Rescuers dig through a collapsed Kumamoto mall as a 7.1 quake’s death toll climbs past a dozen

+

A magnitude-7.1 earthquake struck Japan’s southern Kumamoto Prefecture on Tuesday, July 28, with an epicenter about 20 km (12 miles) south of Kumamoto City, the largest city on central Kyushu with roughly 700,000 residents. By Wednesday soldiers and emergency crews were still combing damaged buildings, and the confirmed death toll had risen to at least 18 — including a foreign national — with at least 62 injured, six seriously. Among the deadliest sites: a partially collapsed shopping mall torn apart by an apparent explosion about an hour after the quake, where rescuers pulled out eight people including two women in their 20s who died, and a chimney collapse at a Nippon Paper Industries factory that killed five.

The quake knocked out power to thousands of homes and ruptured roads across the region, complicating rescue efforts as workers raced to reach people still trapped in rubble. Kumamoto sits on the same fault-riven part of Kyushu devastated by a pair of deadly earthquakes in 2016, and early tallies remained provisional as search-and-rescue continued and authorities assessed aftershock risk.

Sources: NPR, CNN, U.S. News (Reuters), Rappler, Al Jazeera

Economy · Health Policy · 2026 Midterms

Trump’s CMS ends the Medicare drug-plan subsidy that held premiums down — and seniors learn 2027 rates just before the midterms

+

On Tuesday, July 28, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services — led by Administrator Mehmet Oz — announced the conclusion of the Part D Premium Stabilization Demonstration, a program the Biden administration created in 2024 to cushion premium swings as the Part D benefit redesign took effect. The demonstration pays private insurers extra federal money to blunt rate increases; it is providing an estimated $3.6 billion in support this year and, per the Medicare Payment Advisory Commission, cut the average stand-alone Part D premium by roughly 40% in 2025. Some 25 million people hold Part D drug plans (average monthly premium about $36 in 2026). CMS said its review of 2027 bids showed insurers can now price plans accurately without the support — a “return to operating under regular market conditions” — and set the national base beneficiary premium at $41.33 for 2027. Beneficiaries learn their 2027 rates during fall open enrollment, close to the November midterms.

Outlets and officials disagree on the sting. Oz said “premiums will go up by less than $10 for most Medicare recipients, with many even seeing lower premiums,” and called the demonstration an insurer “bailout.” But figures cited in coverage show roughly 45% of enrollees facing monthly increases of $11–$20, a range the Wall Street Journal report also emphasized, which cuts against the “less than $10 for most” framing. The affected universe is contested too: the Democratic National Committee described a hike on “more than 11 million Americans,” while other coverage frames it as up to the roughly 25 million Part D enrollees — a gap that turns on which subset is counted.

The Left-Leaning Take

Progressive outlets cast this as a deliberate affordability hit on seniors, timed to land during open enrollment weeks before the vote. The New Republic (“Trump Is About to Send Seniors’ Drug Costs Surging”) and Common Dreams argued the administration is choosing to let premiums rise rather than extend a program that was demonstrably holding costs down, tying it to a broader pattern of making health care less affordable; the DNC’s Kendall Witmer said Trump and Republicans “are doing everything they can to make healthcare unaffordable for Americans, especially for seniors,” and flagged Oz’s history as a Medicare Advantage booster. (No in-window signed Vox, Slate, Mother Jones or Guardian column on the announcement was located — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The market case — advanced by Oz and echoed by conservative budget hawks — is that the demonstration was an election-timed Biden gimmick and “an unchecked taxpayer-funded bailout” of insurers that distorted pricing by guaranteeing the government would absorb rate increases; ending it forces insurers back to disciplined, competitive bids. The counter-worry on the right is purely political: any premium number that arrives in October is a gift to the opposition, whatever its merits. (No in-window signed National Review, WSJ editorial or Reason piece dedicated to this announcement was located; the mainstream-right editorial position is drawn from the administration’s stated rationale and is provisional.)

Legal · DOJ & the Courts

Comey asks a federal judge to toss the “86 47” seashell indictment, alleging vindictive prosecution and Trump-directed surveillance

+

Former FBI Director James Comey filed motions on July 27–28 asking the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Virginia to dismiss the two-count indictment returned in April 2026 charging him with threatening the president and transmitting a threat in interstate commerce. The charges stem from a since-deleted May 2025 Instagram post showing seashells arranged as “86 47,” captioned “Cool shell formation on my beach walk” (“86” meaning to get rid of; Trump is the 47th president). Comey’s team makes three arguments: that the post is protected First Amendment political speech and not a “true threat” — noting “86 47” is a “well-known political slogan” sold on merchandise and shown at protests; that the case is selective and vindictive, part of “a yearslong campaign by the President to use the criminal process to punish Mr. Comey for his protected speech”; and that Trump directed the Secret Service to surveil Comey after the post.

Coverage identifies U.S. District Judge Louise Flanagan as presiding, with trial set for October 21, 2026. This is the government’s second attempt to prosecute Comey: an earlier Virginia case alleging he lied to Congress was dismissed in November 2025 after a judge found the prosecutor, Lindsey Halligan, had been unlawfully appointed — a dismissal the government appealed in December 2025. As Lawfare’s analysis has stressed, the “true threat” doctrine and the selective- or vindictive-prosecution standard are the twin legal pressure points on which the motions turn.

The Left-Leaning Take

Liberal and pro-democracy commentators frame the seashells case as a textbook weaponization of the Justice Department against a Trump adversary — a First Amendment prosecution so thin that, as The Hill’s “The Memo” put it, it “stirs unease even among some in GOP.” Outlets like Democracy Docket foreground the surveillance allegation and the “true threat” overreach as evidence of retaliatory prosecution, slotting the case into broader trackers of the administration’s investigations of critics. (No single in-window signed Slate, Vox, Mother Jones or Guardian column on the July filings was located — provisional, though the framing is well-attested across coverage.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The clearest in-window right-of-center voice is libertarian: the Cato Institute (“Seashells, Shakedowns …”) calls the prosecution dangerous overreach, warning that “ridiculous cases like the Comey seashell indictment stand to become the rule rather than the exception” and pointing to “the total lack of accountability for prosecutors” — the Reason/libertarian right siding squarely against DOJ. No in-window mainstream-right defense of the indictment (National Review or WSJ editorial) was located, so the pro-prosecution right position is not established for this window.

Europe · Wildfires & Climate

France mounts its largest peacetime evacuation since WWII as a returning heat wave threatens to reignite the Bordeaux megafire

+

More than 300,000 people — roughly 330,000 by the latest tallies — have been driven from homes and vacation sites across southwestern France and Spain by wildfires of exceptional scale, in what French officials describe as the country’s largest peacetime evacuation since World War II. On Tuesday, July 28, authorities ordered nearly 4,000 more people out of Atlantic-coast tourist areas as returning heat threatened firefighters’ fragile hold on a vast blaze west of Bordeaux in the Gironde — a fire that has burned an area roughly four times the size of Paris (about 104,000 acres in France), with some 190,000 acres burned in Spain. President Emmanuel Macron deployed about 1,000 soldiers, France declared a national wildfire emergency for the first time, and Italy sent Canadair water-bombers to Spain.

At least one death has been confirmed — a 78-year-old man with reduced mobility near Manises, outside Valencia. As of the July 29 updates, crews had held the main French blaze through a calmer night but faced fresh flare-ups near Grand Crohot on the Lège-Cap-Ferret peninsula, with a new heat wave (Spain’s fourth of the summer) building this week and no rain forecast for the fire zones. Evacuation totals vary materially by source and date — from about 250,000 on July 25 to “more than 300,000” by July 28–29 — as the crisis has evolved.

Middle East · Gaza Ceasefire

Hamas negotiators open second-phase ceasefire talks in Cairo as strikes keep killing in a Gaza that still hasn’t begun to rebuild

Technology · Semiconductors

China starts mass-producing homegrown DUV chip tools — and a €60 billion selloff hits ASML

+

A report first carried by Bloomberg on July 27 and widely amplified July 28 says a little-known Chinese state-backed firm, Shanghai Aishengna Electronic Technology Group, has begun mass production of domestically developed immersion deep-ultraviolet (DUV) lithography machines, after absorbing teams from top Chinese lithography startups. The consortium is targeting roughly 5 immersion-DUV systems in 2026, rising to about 20 in 2027, for domestic customers including SMIC, Hua Hong Semiconductor and ChangXin Memory Technologies. The tool has reportedly been under evaluation at SMIC since September 2025, is initially aimed at 28-nanometer production, and is engineered with an option to reach more advanced 7nm/5nm nodes via multipatterning. Analysts stress heavy caveats: the reported 5 units equal roughly 3.8% of ASML’s targeted 2026 immersion-DUV capacity, and this is a lower-end tool — not the EUV machines that remain a Western monopoly.

The report accelerated a global semiconductor selloff. ASML shares fell about 10% over two days, wiping more than €60 billion off its market value and dropping the stock to early-June lows. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell for a fourth straight session — its longest losing streak of the year — with the Nasdaq flirting with correction, and analysts also cited Nvidia’s roughly $750 billion in AI-infrastructure commitments feeding “AI debt” jitters. A counter-signal cut the other way: memory prices are still climbing, with third-quarter DRAM contracts settling 20–30% higher this month.

Energy · Solar & Storage

Tesla signs two big solar-and-storage supply deals in a single day, anchored by a 1-TWh-a-year Arizona plant

+

On July 28, Tesla Energy signed two long-term power-purchase agreements. The larger is with KKR-backed developer ContourGlobal for “Project Sterling” in Arizona: Tesla will take about 90% of the output of a plant pairing 509 MWdc / 450 MWac of solar with a 360 MW four-hour battery system (roughly 1.4 GWh), amounting to about 1 terawatt-hour of renewable electricity per year — described as one of the largest solar-plus-battery corporate PPAs ever signed for a single U.S. facility. Sterling connects to the Western Area Power Administration grid with firm transmission rights into California’s CAISO market; on-site construction is slated for 2026, with commercial operations targeted for 2028.

Hours earlier, Tesla agreed to buy the entire output of the 140 MWac “Lumen Farm” solar plant in northeast Texas, developed by EQT-backed Zelestra; that project is expected to begin construction in 2027 and reach full operations in 2029. Neither the financial terms nor the exact contract durations were disclosed for either deal beyond “long-term” — but a single-day double signing of this scale is a concrete marker of how a battery-and-solar giant is now locking up its own clean supply.

Science · Astronomy

NASA’s Swift catches a “wandering” supermassive black hole shredding a star far from its galaxy’s core

+

NASA reported, in a release picked up July 27–28, that the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory observed a tidal disruption event — a black hole tearing apart a star — more than 30,000 light-years from the center of its host galaxy, which lies about 750 million light-years from Earth. It is described as the farthest-from-a-galactic-core such event yet seen; the “wandering” supermassive black hole is estimated at roughly 1 million solar masses. The flare briefly outshone the entire host galaxy in ultraviolet light, and Swift’s Ultraviolet/Optical Telescope measured its temperature at about 30,000°C (54,000°F).

The outburst was first flagged in November 2025 by the Zwicky Transient Facility at Palomar Observatory — surfaced, reporting notes, by an automated transient-classification pipeline — and then followed up by Swift. Wandering supermassive black holes, ejected or left over from galaxy mergers, are notoriously hard to find precisely because they sit far from the bright galactic centers where such monsters usually lurk; catching one mid-meal, lit up by the star it is devouring, offers a rare handle on a population that is otherwise nearly invisible.
