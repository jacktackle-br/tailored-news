Justice · Weaponization

The FBI seizes former Rep. Eric Swalwell’s phone and laptop in a sexual-misconduct probe, and his lawyers cry political retribution

+

FBI agents executed search warrants against former Democratic Rep. Eric Swalwell over the weekend — one as he landed at San Francisco International Airport on Saturday night, a second at his Washington, D.C., home on Sunday — as part of a criminal investigation into allegations of sexual misconduct. A court filing this week in the Northern District of California disclosed that agents seized an iPhone and a MacBook Pro; the raids were widely reported Aug. 20. Swalwell resigned from Congress and abandoned his California gubernatorial bid in April after reporting by CNN and the San Francisco Chronicle detailed misconduct allegations, including a former staffer who said he raped her; the Manhattan District Attorney and the Los Angeles County Sheriff had already opened inquiries.

Swalwell’s legal team said a parallel push to release FBI investigatory files was “meant to punish an outspoken critic of the current administration,” noting Swalwell appears on the “enemies list” in FBI Director Kash Patel’s 2023 book Government Gangsters. The case lands amid a broader pattern of Justice Department action against Trump antagonists — most prominently the indictment of former FBI Director James Comey — raising the live legal question of where a legitimate criminal probe ends and retaliatory use of law enforcement begins. (Facts from The Washington Post, NBC News, CBS News and CNN.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads this as the weaponization playbook turned on a marked man: a prominent Trump critic on Patel’s published enemies list, raided with maximum spectacle and threatened with a selective file dump, in the same season the DOJ has gone after Comey. The caveat progressives insist on: the former staffer’s rape allegation is grave and deserves real investigation — the objection is to politically targeted, leak-driven prosecution, not to accountability. (Directional; no clean in-window Vox/Slate/Mother Jones column isolated.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The directional right notes the allegations are serious enough that they already forced Swalwell out of Congress and out of the governor’s race, and that Democratic-run offices — the Manhattan DA and the LA County Sheriff — opened their own probes, which cuts against the “pure political hit” framing. A civil-libertarian caveat (Reason-style) runs the other way: publicizing investigatory files on an uncharged person is a bad precedent whoever the target is. (Directional; no clean in-window NR/WSJ/Reason piece isolated.)

Immigration · TPS

The first deportation flight to Haiti since TPS ended lands with more than 160 people — diverted from a capital gangs control

+

The first U.S. deportation flight to Haiti since the administration ended Temporary Protected Status for Haitians arrived this week carrying more than 160 people, landing in Cap-Haïtien after officials steered clear of Port-au-Prince’s airport because gangs control much of the capital. Those removed included former TPS holders and people who had already served criminal sentences in the U.S.; one deportee said he had not seen Haiti since he was five. The flight follows a June 2026 Supreme Court ruling that cleared the way to strip status from a Haitian population numbering in the hundreds of thousands.

The move reopened a split screen within the GOP as much as between the parties. The administration cast the removals as a “win for the rule of law,” arguing TPS “was never intended to be an amnesty program.” But Republicans including Rep. Maria Elvira Salazar of Florida and Ohio Gov. Mike DeWine have called the Haiti decision a mistake — DeWine branding it a “job killer” not in the country’s interest — while advocates warn of sending people back to a nation the U.S. itself deems too dangerous for its own citizens. (Facts from The Washington Post, Bloomberg, the Associated Press and The Hill.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left calls it cruelty with a body count risk: deporting long-settled residents — some who left Haiti as small children — into a collapsed state where the deportation plane itself had to avoid the capital’s gang-run airport. Ending status for a population this size, they argue, is not enforcement of the law so much as manufactured emergency, indifferent to the humanitarian consequences. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The administration and its allies frame it as the law finally working: TPS was always meant to be temporary, the Supreme Court signed off, and removals of people who overstayed a lapsed protection — including some with criminal records — are overdue. But this is a genuine intra-right split: Salazar and DeWine warn the humanitarian and economic costs are real and the pace reckless, a caution coming from inside the party, not the opposition. (Directional; intra-GOP disagreement noted.)

Sources: The Washington Post, Bloomberg, Associated Press, The Hill

Middle East · Iran

Trump declares an ‘economic D-Day’ on Iran, threatening any country that keeps trading with Tehran

+

Nearing the six-month mark of the Iran war and facing dwindling stockpiles of key munitions, President Trump declared an “economic D-Day” against Tehran, warning that any country or company that keeps doing business with Iran will face “tremendous” consequences. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent detailed the next phase of what the administration calls Operation Economic Fury — sweeping secondary sanctions on third parties — telling markets: “If you insist on doing business with them, then the U.S. Treasury and U.S. government will put its full might and force against you.” The stated aims are to force Iran to abandon its nuclear program and to fully reopen the Strait of Hormuz, where transits remain far below prewar levels amid Iranian targeting of vessels and a U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports.

The pivot “from missiles to money” lets Trump escalate pressure without immediately ordering another wave of strikes, and Vice President JD Vance framed it as the war entering a “new phase.” The obstacles are considerable: Iran, which has absorbed nearly five decades of sanctions, dismissed the campaign as “economic terrorism” that will “bring more failures”; China has signaled it will not fall in line; and Vance himself conceded the squeeze could rebound on American consumers — with early market jitters already read by some outlets as the policy’s “first victim.” Roughly a fifth of the world’s oil and gas once moved through Hormuz, so the stakes for global energy prices are outsized. (Facts from the Associated Press, CNBC, CBS News and Al Jazeera.)

Sources: Associated Press, CNBC, CBS News, Al Jazeera
Europe & Middle East · West Bank

Israel opens tenders for the E1 settlement, and seven Western governments call it ‘unacceptable’ as the EU readies sanctions

+

Israel this week published a construction tender for 1,234 housing units in the E1 corridor east of Jerusalem — part of a wider plan for some 3,401 units — in an area long seen as a red line because building there would sever the northern and southern West Bank and cut Palestinians off from East Jerusalem, undermining the territorial basis for a contiguous Palestinian state. UN Secretary-General António Guterres called the plan an “existential threat” to a two-state outcome, and the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Canada and Norway issued a joint statement branding the tenders “unacceptable” and demanding Israel halt the project.

The condemnation is now attached to concrete threats. Britain summoned Israel’s chargé d’affaires and signaled a forthcoming package of measures, including sanctions targeting those driving settlement expansion, and the EU has drawn up an initial menu of steps — special labeling of settlement products, curtailed academic cooperation, and suspension of some security and diplomatic ties — to be triggered if construction actually begins. It marks one of the sharpest Western-Israeli ruptures of the year, building on coordinated settler-violence sanctions imposed by several of the same governments earlier in 2026, though whether Brussels can reach the unanimity some measures require remains the open question. (Facts from Bloomberg, Reuters, Al Jazeera and Euronews.)

The AI data-center backlash hardens into a midterm flashpoint as the GOP warns its own candidates the issue is ‘radioactive’

+

A fast-spreading voter backlash against AI data centers crystallized into a bipartisan midterm issue this week, fueled by anger over rising electricity bills, water use and tax breaks for tech firms. On Aug. 19, Axios reported a leaked National Republican Senatorial Committee memo warning GOP campaigns that data centers have become a “sleeper issue” that is politically “radioactive”; on Aug. 20, follow-on coverage from Axios and CNBC documented candidates in both parties cutting ads touting anti-data-center positions and distancing themselves from projects their own leaders had courted. Polling underlines the shift: Gallup found roughly 7 in 10 Americans oppose a data center being built near them.

The specifics driving the anger: electricity prices are forecast to climb about 6% through 2026; lawmakers in more than 30 states introduced upward of 300 data-center bills this year; New York became the first state to impose a one-year statewide moratorium; and at least 75 projects were blocked or delayed in early 2026. The Trump administration has tried to defuse it with a voluntary “Ratepayer Protection Pledge” — signed by Google, Microsoft, Meta, Oracle, xAI, OpenAI and Amazon plus dozens of governors and utilities — under which tech firms promise to cover their own added power costs. The issue is scrambling races in Ohio, Florida, Pennsylvania and Nevada. (Facts from NBC News, CNBC, Axios and Lawfare.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left casts this as a class story: households are handed sky-high utility bills so out-of-state corporations can run energy- and water-guzzling server farms on generous tax breaks — the AI boom’s profits privatized, its costs socialized. Progressives argue Trump’s voluntary pledge is toothless PR (a leaked GOP memo begging tech firms to fix a “toxic brand” proves the party knows it is exposed) and that the real fix is mandatory large-load tariffs and moratoriums. (Directional; no clean in-window Vox/Slate/Mother Jones column isolated.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The abundance-minded right pushes back on the bans: The Dispatch, in an in-window piece arguing “the data-center backlash is really about abundance,” contends the answer to strained grids is building far more energy — nuclear, gas, transmission — not moratoriums that would cede the AI race to China. Free-market conservatives frame data centers as engines of investment and warn against populist price-control instincts, even as the NRSC frets that GOP incumbents who championed the projects now own the utility-bill anger. Courts · Elections

A Missouri judge locks in the Trump-backed GOP congressional map for 2026 and blocks a voter referendum

+

On Aug. 19–20, Cole County Circuit Judge Daniel Green ruled that Missouri’s constitutional referendum provisions “do not apply to congressional redistricting enacted by the General Assembly,” and ordered that the Republican-drawn congressional map “remains in full force and effect for the 2026 general election.” The decision sided with GOP Secretary of State Denny Hoskins, who had rejected a citizen petition seeking to force a November statewide vote on the map. Drawn by the Republican-led legislature after Trump urged GOP-controlled states to redraw boundaries mid-decade, the map is designed to endanger Democratic Rep. Emanuel Cleaver of Kansas City. Opponents are expected to appeal to the Missouri Supreme Court.

Missouri is the second GOP-controlled state, after Texas, to redraw mid-decade at Trump’s urging. The Texas map was greenlit by the U.S. Supreme Court in a 6–3 stay; California voters approved a countering map (Prop 50) to add roughly five Democratic-leaning seats, and North Carolina, Ohio, Florida and Louisiana are among states swept into the resulting arms race. (Facts from NBC News, NOTUS, Reuters and Fox News.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads the ruling as a double disenfranchisement: a Trump-driven gerrymander stacked atop a judge blocking Missourians from exercising their state-constitutional right to a veto referendum. The frame is a raw power grab — politicians insulating themselves from voters to manufacture a House majority they might not win on a fair map — and Democrats argue it justifies their own counter-gerrymanders in self-defense. (Directional; drawn from Democracy Docket and The New Republic.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The directional right holds that the legislature has clear constitutional authority over congressional maps, the referendum gambit was a legally dubious end-run, and the court simply applied Missouri’s constitution as written. Conservatives also invoke a tu quoque — blue states like Illinois and California (via Prop 50) gerrymander aggressively too — casting GOP map-drawing as legitimate hardball and the ruling as securing the House firewall ahead of the midterms. (Directional; no clean in-window NR/WSJ editorial isolated.)

Courts · DOJ

A federal judge tosses the DOJ’s demand for Minnesota’s voter rolls, calling it a ‘fishing expedition’ — the department’s 23rd straight loss

+

U.S. District Judge Katherine Menendez dismissed the Justice Department’s lawsuit demanding Minnesota’s statewide voter-registration list — names, birthdates, addresses and partial Social Security numbers — from Secretary of State Steve Simon, who had refused to hand it over. Menendez wrote that “the government suggests it is permitted to use a civil action as a ‘fishing expedition,’” adding that an ordinary litigant “isn’t allowed to seek discovery first and allege a claim later, and the federal government has no special privilege to do so either.” The ruling (entered Aug. 17) left DOJ 0-for-23 in its nationwide effort to obtain state voter data, a parallel Oregon suit having also been dismissed.

The in-window development: coverage this week reported that newly confirmed Attorney General Todd Blanche is weighing whether to ask the Supreme Court to intervene in the dozens of DOJ suits against states that refused to turn over voter rolls — recasting the string of losses as part of a broader administration push to nationalize election-data collection ahead of the midterms. (Facts from Courthouse News, the Minnesota Reformer, Democracy Docket and Slate.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left frames the ruling as another judicial rejection of an unlawful power grab: the DOJ has no statutory authority to vacuum up sensitive voter data and was using litigation as a pretext to hunt for a violation after the fact. A 0-23 record, they argue, shows the effort is about intimidation and laying groundwork to contest 2026 results, not “election integrity” — with Slate casting it as Trump “can’t stop, won’t stop” undermining the midterms. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The directional right frames access to voter rolls as a legitimate tool to verify accurate lists and detect noncitizen or duplicate registrations, and casts blue-state secretaries as stonewalling reasonable oversight — hence AG Blanche’s interest in taking the fight to the Supreme Court. A civil-libertarian caveat cuts the other way: a federal demand for citizens’ partial Social Security numbers is the kind of centralized data grab the right normally resists, though no clean in-window NR/Reason piece was isolated. (Directional.)

Africa · Global Health

DR Congo’s Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak, now the country’s deadliest, keeps climbing past 2,400 deaths

+

The Democratic Republic of the Congo’s Ebola outbreak — caused by the Bundibugyo strain — has become the deadliest in the country’s history, and the toll kept rising through this week’s updates. As of the Aug. 19 figures, authorities reported roughly 5,200 confirmed cases and about 2,476 deaths, having passed the 2018–2020 outbreak’s toll of around 2,299. The WHO and UN warn it is on track to become one of the worst Ebola epidemics ever recorded, trailing only West Africa’s 2014–2016 outbreak, and it is killing far faster: it reached 2,000 deaths in under three months, with a case-fatality ratio near 47%.

The Bundibugyo species has complicated the response because existing licensed Ebola vaccines and therapeutics were certified against a different species (Zaire ebolavirus), blunting the usual toolkit. The scale and speed are straining DRC’s health system and regional containment, with spillover concerns involving neighboring Uganda, making this a live international health-security emergency. (Facts from the WHO, UN News, Al Jazeera and CNN.)

Science · Human Evolution

A new paper argues the human family tree needs a rewrite — folding Australopithecus and Paranthropus into Homo

+

In a study released Aug. 20 in the American Journal of Biological Anthropology, researcher Ian Towle of Monash University’s Biomedicine Discovery Institute argues that the genera Australopithecus, Paranthropus and Kenyanthropus should be collapsed into an expanded genus Homo, covering roughly the last four to five million years of hominin evolution. His core claim is that the traits long used to separate these groups — larger brains, tool use, distinctive teeth and diets — no longer hold up as clean dividing lines, because a wave of recent fossil finds has blurred them and shown the groups may not represent distinct evolutionary branches.

The proposal is a taxonomic reinterpretation rather than a new fossil discovery, and it is already contested: lumping millions of years of diverse hominins into one genus would strip Homo of much of its diagnostic meaning, and many paleoanthropologists favor keeping finer distinctions. Towle frames it as a call to reconsider a classification system he says is increasingly at odds with the fossil record — a debate sharpened by 2025 discoveries such as the mixed Australopithecus/Homo assemblage reported from Ledi-Geraru, Ethiopia. (Facts from Sci.News, ScienceDaily, The Conversation and Anthropology.net.)

Sources: Sci.News, ScienceDaily, The Conversation, Anthropology.net
