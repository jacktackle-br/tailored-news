Press Freedom · First Amendment

Trump says he is ‘banning’ CNN, MS NOW and Politico from the White House

+

In a Truth Social post on Friday, Sept. 18, President Trump declared he is “banning” CNN, MS NOW (the former MSNBC) and Politico from the White House, accusing them of constantly reporting “FAKE NEWS” and “FICTION and LIES,” and threatening that other outlets — he singled out The New York Times and The Washington Post as “crummy” — could “follow.”

As of Friday afternoon there was no sign the administration had actually acted: reporters from all three outlets were still at the White House, and Trump cited no specific action or story. Press-freedom advocates called it plainly unconstitutional — the Freedom of the Press Foundation’s Seth Stern said it is “difficult to imagine a more blatant violation of the First Amendment,” and the Knight First Amendment Institute’s Jameel Jaffer argued expelling outlets from the press pool, a “public forum,” would be “doubly unconstitutional.” Politico vowed to “vigorously defend” its rights; CNN called the move an “illegal assault” on constitutionally protected reporting. (The declaration is confirmed across Tier-1 outlets; whether it becomes an enforceable ban is not yet established.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left casts it as authoritarian escalation: a president trying to expel news organizations from the People’s House for coverage he dislikes, of a piece with the FCC’s pressure on ABC over Jimmy Kimmel and the broader campaign against critical media. Even an empty threat, the argument goes, is meant to chill reporting and intimidate the pool. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The populist right cheers the grievance against outlets it sees as reflexively hostile. But institutionalist and libertarian conservatives are uneasy: a government punishing the press for its coverage is exactly the state overreach they warn against, and courts are likely to slap it down (Reason’s free-speech instinct; skeptics at National Review and The Dispatch). The Bulwark (anti-Trump right, an intra-right dissent) calls it a naked authoritarian move. (Directional.)

Sources: NPR, CNN, CBS News, Axios, CNBC, TIME
Elections · Voting Rights

Civil-rights groups sue to keep armed ICE officers away from the polls, calling it illegal voter intimidation

+

A coalition of voting-rights organizations — LULAC, Common Cause, UnidosUS and the city and county of Denver, backed by the NAACP Legal Defense Fund and Democracy Forward — filed a federal lawsuit in Washington, D.C. this week seeking to block the deployment of armed ICE and other federal officers at polling places during the 2026 midterms. The suit argues the administration’s repeated suggestions that agents could appear at the polls amount to illegal voter intimidation under the 1965 Voting Rights Act, and that it — alongside aggressive immigration sweeps — has left voters of color fearful of participating.

The policy being challenged traces to Sept. 1, when Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said ICE officers could enter polling places to serve warrants or conduct enforcement. The White House responded that the president “hasn’t discussed plans” for ICE at the polls, and DHS said it “is not planning operations targeting polling locations.” Local officials, including Denver’s clerk, note that federal agents who intimidate voters face criminal penalties. (Filing dates are reported variously as Thursday and Friday across outlets; a separate related suit surfaced earlier in September.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a deliberate suppression play: dangling armed federal agents at polling sites to deter Black, Latino and Asian voters ahead of the midterms, a Voting Rights Act violation dressed up as enforcement. The denials, they argue, don’t undo the chilling effect the rhetoric was designed to create. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right points to the administration’s own denials — no operation is planned — and calls the suit premature and political, litigating a hypothetical. Some libertarians (Reason’s instinct) are nonetheless wary of armed federal officers anywhere near a ballot box as its own kind of government overreach, whatever the intent. (Directional.)

Sources: NPR, Washington Post, NAACP LDF, Democracy Forward

Iran War · Strait of Hormuz

Two tankers hit in the Strait of Hormuz as Trump says his next move could be to ‘annihilate’ Iran’s regime

+

The tanker war in the Gulf escalated sharply on Friday, Sept. 18. The strikes landed as President Trump, in interviews Thursday and Friday, said he faces a “big decision” on whether to “annihilate” the Iranian leadership — telling NewsNation “we’ll see” — even while predicting the war will “end soon” and drive gas prices down. Inside Iran, state media reported hundreds of thousands rallying in Tehran and more than 600,000 signing up for military training, the largest show of support since the February US–Israeli strikes. Trump is set to meet the six Gulf Cooperation Council leaders on the UN sidelines Tuesday to weigh the next phase — the diplomatic bookend to this morning’s record F-35 sale to Saudi Arabia.

Europe · NATO’s Eastern Flank

NATO jets down a drone over Lithuania — a first — as a Russian warship fires flares at a Danish helicopter

+

Building on this morning’s warning from Poland’s Donald Tusk that Russia is preparing “accidental”-looking strikes on Ukraine’s backers, the eastern-flank pressure took concrete form. Italian Typhoon jets flying NATO air-policing shot down a drone in Lithuanian airspace near the village of Pratkūnai on Sept. 15 — the first drone ever downed over Lithuania — a device that likely entered from Belarus and, two senior officials said, was probably carrying explosives. Separately, a Russian warship fired two flares at a Danish military helicopter over international Baltic waters, one passing close to the aircraft.

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen called the Lithuania incident “part of a broader pattern of Russian aggression and provocation against Europe,” and Romania reports airspace incursions rising steeply through 2026. The cumulative effect — drones over Poland, Lithuania and the Baltics, plus naval brinkmanship — is hardening the case for a permanent US presence in Poland and feeding the EU’s strategic-autonomy debate ahead of UN week. Moscow denies targeting NATO territory. (Origin and intent of the Lithuania drone rest on preliminary official assessments.)

Sources: NPR, Al Jazeera, Stars and Stripes, Meduza

Congress · War Powers

House Republicans adjourn for seven weeks — sidestepping Massie’s forced vote to impeach Hegseth

+

Outgoing Rep. Thomas Massie (R-KY) on Sept. 15 introduced eight articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and made the resolution privileged, a maneuver forcing a full House vote within two legislative days. The articles, alleging “high crimes and misdemeanors,” center on an alleged violation of the 1973 War Powers Resolution — continuing the Iran war without congressional authorization and after Congress passed resolutions to end it — and also cite alleged retaliation against Sen. Mark Kelly, an “unlawful” Yemen operation, the capture of former Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro, extrajudicial strikes on suspected drug boats, and disregard for civilian-casualty rules.

On Sept. 16, Speaker Mike Johnson announced the House would cut its week short and begin a roughly seven-week pre-midterm recess after Wednesday-evening votes — punting any impeachment vote until after November. Johnson called the resolution “a publicity stunt” that would “immediately be tabled” and denied the recess was tied to it; Massie and Democrats insisted the early adjournment was designed to spare vulnerable Republicans a bipartisan vote they feared could pass. (Outlets agree on the timeline but not the motive; a graphic school-strike casualty claim in Massie’s articles is an allegation in the resolution, not independently confirmed by Tier-1 wires.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads it as accountability-dodging: Johnson allegedly shut the People’s House for seven weeks rather than let members go on record, because impeachment might actually pass on a bipartisan basis. Outlets like The New Republic and MSNBC cast the early recess as the GOP doing “even less work” to shield Hegseth and the unpopular Iran war. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The hawkish right (National Review) treats Massie’s move as a doomed gesture while conceding a ceasefire “does not stop the clock” on war-powers duties; libertarian Reason sympathizes with the substance — Congress abdicated its war power — even as the vote can’t pass. The Bulwark (anti-Trump right, a dissent) openly backs Massie, using the episode to indict GOP leadership’s refusal to check the administration. (Directional.)

Sources: NPR, CNN, NBC News, The Hill, Reason, The New Republic
Congress · Russia & Iran Sanctions

House sends the Graham Russia–Iran sanctions bill to Trump’s desk, 262–159, as 58 Democrats cross over

+

On Sept. 16, as one of its last major acts before recess, the House voted 262–159 to pass the “Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026,” sending it to President Trump. Named for the late Sen. Lindsey Graham (R-SC), who died unexpectedly in July 2026, the bill had already cleared the Senate (reported 86–11) the prior month. It authorizes tariffs of up to 100% on the largest purchasers of Russian crude and gas (China and India cited), sanctions Russian officials, banks and the “shadow fleet” of tankers, and expands Iran sanctions. 58 Democrats crossed party lines to support it despite leadership opposition, ending a roughly two-year lull in congressional Ukraine-related action.

The nuance is in the drafting: the tariff authority is discretionary — “may,” not “shall” — which critics call a loophole, and CNN reported some Republicans privately worry the measure hands Trump too much tariff power. Whether the president actually wields the new authority against Beijing and New Delhi is now his call. (Vote counts and framing consistent across outlets; the discretionary-vs-mandatory point is the live dispute.)

The Left-Leaning Take

Democratic leaders led the “no” votes: Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries argued the bill doesn’t require Trump to sanction Russia, is riddled with loopholes, and mainly hands the White House sweeping tariff powers that would raise Americans’ costs while doing little for Ukraine. The center-left worry is the blank check, not sparing Putin. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

National Review editorializes in favor — pass it to pressure Putin and honor Graham, “the best option on the table.” Libertarian Reason (with Cato and Ilya Somin) is skeptical-to-opposed, calling the vast, ill-defined tariff powers “a bad law.” The Bulwark (anti-Trump right, a dissent) mocks Republicans for voluntarily handing Trump a “tariff nuke,” another Article I abdication. (Directional.)

Sources: NPR, CNBC, Al Jazeera, CNN, National Review, Reason

Tech · Semiconductors

Huawei pulls its next AI chip forward to early 2027 and unveils an all-optical ‘SuperPoD’ to chase Nvidia

+

At Huawei Connect 2026, which opened in Shanghai on Thursday, Sept. 17, Huawei unveiled the Atlas 960E SuperPoD, which it calls the industry’s first “supernode” to use Near-Package Optics. Official specs cite up to 4,096 NPUs, roughly 8 EFLOPS of FP8 compute and 1 PB of HBM, linked by an all-optical “UnifiedBus” interconnect and designed to train or serve models up to 10 trillion parameters. Huawei says swapping some 48,000 800G optical modules for about 5,500 “Hi-ONE” modules cuts power draw by more than 550 kW.

Crucially, Huawei said it is moving up its Ascend 960DT chip to Q1 2027 — three quarters early — with the 960PR to follow in Q3 2027, and is pitching its interconnect as a would-be global AI standard. It is a direct bid to close the gap with Nvidia despite export controls on advanced tooling. (Some secondary outlets circulated a larger “15,488-chip / 6.7×-Nvidia” cluster claim that differs from Huawei’s official SuperPoD figures and rests on a vendor comparison — treat both cautiously.)

Science · Astronomy

Astronomers confirm the youngest known planet — a newborn gas giant still forming in its birth disk

+

A team has confirmed Elias 2-24 b, a gas giant less than one million years old — shattering the prior record, a cluster of planets all more than about five million years old. Despite its extreme youth, the planet is roughly Jupiter’s mass and orbits a newly formed star about 450 light-years from Earth, still embedded in the disk of dust and gas it is forming from — a rare direct look at planet formation in progress.

Researchers directly imaged the world by combining observations from three facilities: the W. M. Keck Observatory in Hawaii, and ALMA and the ESO Very Large Telescope in Chile. The study, led by Andrea Bernardi, a doctoral candidate at Universidad Diego Portales in Chile, was published Sept. 16 in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, with NASA and wide science-press coverage following. (Single-study result.)

Sources: NASA Science, Space.com, Live Science, ScienceDaily
Climate · Attribution Science

Scientists find warming played a ‘major role’ in the deadly Himalayan flood on the Nepal–Tibet border

+

A World Weather Attribution report released Thursday, Sept. 17, concluded that human-caused climate change played a major role in the catastrophic Aug. 26, 2026 Himalayan flood disaster on the China–Nepal border. The scientists framed it not as a single storm but a “compound crisis” driven by long-term warming, glacier retreat, permafrost thaw and geological instability, finding that “relentless warming has lifted the altitude of the freezing threshold” on the high Himalaya, exposing ice and permafrost in bedrock to longer thaw and weakening mountain slopes. One scientist said there is “no doubt” of climate change’s role.

The disaster sent flash floods and debris flows down a stretch of the Trishuli River, destroying the Gyirong border crossing and striking dozens of settlements. (Casualty figures remain provisional — reports run on the order of 1,400+ deaths and thousands missing in Nepal, plus dozens in Tibet — and exact tolls are unverified pending official confirmation; the attribution finding itself is corroborated across AFP-sourced and Al Jazeera reporting.)

Sources: Al Jazeera, Manila Times (AFP), TIME
Energy · Electrification

India’s clean-power capacity overtakes fossil fuels for the first time — but coal still keeps the lights on

+

New data from Ember and Global Energy Monitor show India’s installed clean-energy capacity has passed fossil-fuel capacity for the first time — 331.7 GW clean versus 302.0 GW fossil, giving non-fossil sources a majority of installed capacity. Solar alone now stands at about 211 GW, up from just 0.07 GW in 2010; between 2025 and mid-2026 India added 75.5 GW of solar against only 3.8 GW of coal, a vivid measure of how lopsided new build has become.

The milestone is about capacity, not generation: coal still supplies roughly 70% of India’s actual electricity, because coal plants run around the clock while solar output falls off at the evening peak. Analysts (CEEW’s Disha Aggarwal) say the binding constraint is now battery storage — deploying it fast enough to shift surplus daytime solar into the evening. (Figures are installed-capacity tallies from industry trackers, not generation shares.)

Sources: Down To Earth, Global Electricity, KNN India
