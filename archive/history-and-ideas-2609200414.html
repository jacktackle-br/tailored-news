History · Slavery & the British State

A new register names 13,000 British slave-traders — kings, MPs, Bank of England founders and dozens of Church clergy — and reopens the reparations fight

+

On September 14 a team led by Professor William Pettigrew of King’s College London launched the online Register of British Slave Traders, the fruit of a seven-year, AHRC-funded project with co-investigators at Lancaster and Manchester. It compiles biographical data on roughly 13,000 people who invested in the trafficking of enslaved Africans between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries — and its granularity is the point. British monarchs were themselves investors and leaders in the trade; more than 100 MPs and dozens of the Bank of England’s founding directors held slaving shares; over 1,000 of the investors were women. The launch coincided with a two-part BBC documentary, Finding Britain’s Slave Traders, presented by the historian David Olusoga.

The detail drawing the sharpest reaction is the established Church’s complicity: researchers identified around 45 Church of England clergymen who invested in the Royal African Company, the single organisation that trafficked more enslaved people than any other. One, Henry Brydges, bought half of a £10,000 shareholding on the very day he became Archdeacon of Rochester; bequests routed slaving profits into parishes “from Cumbria to the Cotswolds.” The revelations land amid an already bitter argument over the Church Commissioners’ £100m reparative fund, which faces legal challenge and deep internal division. The register’s quiet provocation is to reframe British slavery not as a distant colonial enterprise but as capital woven through the monarchy, Parliament, finance and the Church — money that later built hospitals, schools and cultural institutions still standing today.

Sources: King’s College London, The Voice, Religion Media Centre
Philosophy & Ideas · The History of Attention

Meghan O’Gieblyn rereads Simone Weil and Schopenhauer to ask whether ‘attention,’ not ‘will,’ is the faculty that AI is quietly dismantling

+

In the New York Review of Books issue dated September 24, the essayist Meghan O’Gieblyn turns the familiar anxiety about shrinking attention spans into a genuine problem in the history of ideas. Rather than treat distraction as a productivity complaint, she excavates the philosophical lineage of attention itself — anchoring the essay in Simone Weil’s austere claim that “we have to try to cure our faults by attention and not by will,” and reading it against Arthur Schopenhauer’s metaphysics of the will, that strange but powerful account of why we feel perpetually at the mercy of our own desires. The piece is adapted from her forthcoming book, Will and Attention.

The argument’s bite is in the reversal. Where the self-improvement culture — and much of the AI-productivity discourse — frames focus as an act of willpower, O’Gieblyn, via Weil, treats attention as something closer to a receptive, almost religious openness: a capacity for individuality and even grace that is eroded precisely when machines take over our expressiveness and our looking. As AI increasingly writes, summarizes and mediates on our behalf, the essay asks what is lost when the effortful, humbling work of paying attention is outsourced — not merely concentration, but a particular way of being a self. It is a bracing counter to an age that keeps promising to hand our attention back to us by taking still more of our thinking away.

Sources: New York Review of Books, NYRB (issue)
Social Science · Sociology of Religion

Across 81 countries and three decades, the arrival of the internet tracks a measurable retreat of religious identity — a secularization story told in bandwidth

+

A study published this week in Sociology of Religion: A Quarterly Review — “Internet Diffusion and Religious Decline: Evidence from 81 Countries, 1990–2022,” by sociologists at the University of Maribor in Slovenia — offers one of the largest cross-national tests yet of a long-suspected link between digital connectivity and secularization. Drawing on six waves of the World Values Survey and a six-item composite index of religiosity, the authors find that internet penetration is “strongly and negatively associated” with religiosity, with the effect strongest for denominational belonging and for self-identifying as a religious person — the social and identity-based dimensions of faith, rather more than private belief.

The interesting move for a “History & Ideas” reader is what the study implies about mechanism, and about the classic secularization debate. Rather than the internet somehow “disproving” religion, the authors point to the erosion of the local, face-to-face communities and information monopolies that religious institutions historically relied on: connectivity exposes people to pluralism, alternative worldviews and secular networks, loosening the social scaffolding of belief. As commentators — including the economist Tyler Cowen, who flagged the paper — noted, the correlational design cannot fully settle causation, since richer, more educated societies acquire both more internet and less religion at once. But the scale and consistency of the pattern make it a serious data point in the long argument over why the twenty-first-century West, and increasingly the globe, keeps drifting from organized faith.

Sources: PsyPost, Deseret News, Marginal Revolution
