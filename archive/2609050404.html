Voting Rights · SCOTUS

A Boston judge blocks Trump’s USPS mail-ballot rule again as the fight lands back at the Supreme Court, with a Sept. 8 deadline

+

The administration’s bid to reshape mail voting weeks before the Nov. 3 midterms is now pinballing between a federal courthouse in Boston and the Supreme Court. On Thursday, Sept. 3, the Justice Department filed an emergency application asking the justices to lift orders blocking new U.S. Postal Service restrictions rooted in Trump’s March 31, 2026 executive order — the third time in roughly six weeks the mail-voting fight has reached the Court. The contested USPS “final rule” requires states to give the Postal Service a list of voters slated to receive mail ballots, prints a unique barcode on each ballot envelope, and bars USPS from delivering ballots to anyone not on the state-provided “enrolled voter” list. The White House casts it as anti-fraud and anti-noncitizen-voting; opponents call it an unprecedented federal intrusion into state-run elections.

Then on Friday night, Sept. 4, U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani in Boston again granted a preliminary injunction against the envelope and portal-registration provisions, writing that imposing the rule fewer than 70 days out “threatens disenfranchisement of millions” and that officials “are unable to pivot this late in the election cycle.” The Supreme Court has asked for a response by Sept. 8. Timing is the crux: North Carolina began mailing ballots Friday, with other states to follow in coming weeks; roughly a third of 2024 ballots were cast by mail, and about three-quarters of the ~1.3 million active-duty troops are absentee-eligible. A USPS whistleblower separately alleged the compliance system was built in a “rushed, chaotic, and fundamentally flawed” process. (Legal desks weighted near Tier 1; the Court has set no ruling date.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads this as voter suppression dressed as fraud prevention — and, more pointedly, an attempt to seed midterm chaos and pre-delegitimize results the administration may not like. The Constitution’s Elections Clause, the argument runs, leaves the “times, places and manner” of elections to states and Congress, not to a president decreeing ballot rules through the Postal Service; the likeliest casualties are military and overseas voters and the millions who vote by mail. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The institutional and libertarian right is skeptical too, but on federalism rather than turnout: Reason- and National Review-adjacent writers frame a president rewriting ballot delivery by executive order as overreach into a power the Constitution hands the states, and a legally shaky “solution looking for a problem.” The populist/administration lane defends citizenship verification as commonsense. No clean in-window WSJ or NR editorial fully defending the USPS rule surfaced. (Directional; the federalism critique is shared across much of the right.)

Sources: NBC News (Talwani injunction), SCOTUSblog, NPR, Washington Post, CNN
Congress · DOJ Transparency

Johnson scraps two weeks of House votes as a bipartisan Epstein-files discharge petition keeps gathering signatures

+

House GOP leaders confirmed Sept. 3–4 that two of the chamber’s three remaining September workweeks — the weeks of Sept. 21 and Sept. 28 — are canceled, sending members home for roughly 6½ weeks in-district before the Nov. 3 midterms once the planned October recess is folded in. Rep. Thomas Massie (R-Ky.) accused Speaker Mike Johnson of scheduling the recess to “stymie” a vote on the Epstein Files Transparency Act II (H.R. 9694), which Massie introduced with Rep. Ro Khanna (D-Calif.). Johnson denied it. The bill would give state attorneys general, Epstein victims and members of Congress legal standing to sue the Justice Department over withheld, redacted or delayed records; Massie says the government still holds “over three million files.”

Massie filed a discharge petition to force a floor vote, which needs 218 signatures. Counts reported over the past day vary sharply — outlets materially disagree, with Newsweek citing 106 signatories and other reporting putting the tally near 79, mostly Democrats — but they converge on the throughline that Massie is still short and needs more Republicans to cross the threshold. The fight extends a monthslong MAGA-base grievance dating to Attorney General Pam Bondi’s February “Phase 1” rollout and the July DOJ/FBI conclusion that no further disclosure was warranted. (The signature count is the live factual dispute; reported here as rising but short.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a straightforward cover-up — Johnson torching the legislative calendar to spare Trump an Epstein vote — and reads the saga as an administration shielding the powerful, with Bondi having over-promised and under-delivered. The tart irony, they note, is that the most effective pressure is coming from inside the GOP. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

This is the rare story where the populist right lines up with the left on the merits: Massie and much of the MAGA base frame full disclosure as a transparency promise Trump’s own DOJ broke. The pro-leadership lane counters that a pre-midterm recess is ordinary scheduling, not obstruction, and warns the discharge-petition maneuver undercuts the Speaker. The anti-Trump right (The Bulwark) treats the move as damning evidence of a cover-up. (That last is intra-right disagreement, not the more divided mainstream-right line; directional.)

Courts · Immigration Enforcement

An ICE officer is charged in the first federal case of Trump’s deportation surge — for lying to investigators, not for the shooting

+

A federal grand jury in Minnesota indicted ICE officer Christian Castro on Wednesday, Sept. 3, on six counts of making false statements — one for each alleged lie — to investigators about a Jan. 14 shooting in north Minneapolis that wounded Venezuelan immigrant Julio César Sosa-Celis. It is the first federal criminal case against an agent in the mass-deportation campaign Trump has pursued since returning to office. Notably, prosecutors charged the alleged lying about the encounter, not the shooting itself. According to the indictment, Castro and his partner tried to stop a vehicle, pursued a man on foot and tackled him; during the roughly 11-second struggle Sosa-Celis, then 24, came out of his duplex to help, and as the men moved back inside and began closing the front door, Castro stood and fired a single shot through it, striking Sosa-Celis in the leg.

The indictment alleges Castro falsely told investigators he had been hit with a broom and a shovel and that a third man was involved — events prosecutors say never happened. Castro pleaded not guilty in federal court in Texas and was released on a $75,000 unsecured bond with a GPS ankle monitor. The narrow charging decision — false statements rather than use of force — drew immediate attention from legal observers as a marker of how cautiously the DOJ is approaching accountability for agents amid an aggressive enforcement push. (Charges are allegations; Castro is presumed innocent.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads it as a revealingly narrow sliver of accountability: charging the paperwork rather than the bullet, the argument runs, shows a DOJ unwilling to confront the violence its own surge invites, and treats the shooting of a bystander in his own doorway as a footnote to a false-statements case. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The enforcement-first right frames a single indictment as proof the system polices its own and warns against tarring thousands of officers over one contested, fast-moving encounter. Libertarian-leaning voices are less forgiving of the charging theory: prosecuting only the alleged cover-up, not the shot fired through a closing door, is the kind of two-tier leniency that erodes trust in law enforcement. (Directional.)

Sources: CNN, NBC News, CBS Minnesota, The Hill

Middle East · US–Iran Conflict

Iran says U.S. missiles struck one of its oil tankers near Kharg Island, sharply escalating the Strait of Hormuz war

+

Iran on Saturday, Sept. 5, accused the United States of striking an Iranian oil tanker in the Persian Gulf, the latest turn in a widening U.S.–Iran conflict. State broadcaster IRIB said four U.S. missiles hit a tanker roughly six miles (10 km) from Kharg Island — Iran’s main oil-export terminal — with multiple explosions reported and the crew being evacuated; no casualties were cited in initial accounts. The U.S. had not commented at the time of writing. The reported strike came a day after Iran launched anti-ship missiles at vessels in the Strait of Hormuz, and follows Washington resuming strikes after a roughly month-long pause. (Coverage is uniformly attributed to Iranian state media as the source; U.S. confirmation was still pending.)

It fits a sharp escalation of the prior days. Around Sept. 1–2, U.S. Central Command carried out its heaviest strikes on Iran in over a month — U.S. officials cited by Axios put it near 100 targets, including IRGC air-defense sites, radar, mine-laying assets and anti-ship launchers — under a new “tanker for tanker” policy approved by Trump. Iran retaliated with missiles and drones against U.S. bases and Gulf states: Jordan said 13 ballistic missiles entered its airspace (10 intercepted, three landing in uninhabited areas), while Kuwait and Bahrain also reported strikes. Iranian authorities said at least 18 people were killed inside Iran, including four when a wedding at Sirik was hit. (Casualty and attribution figures vary between Iranian claims and targeted governments’ statements; U.S. officials told Reuters there were no American casualties from the base attacks.)

South Atlantic · Falklands

Milei reopens the Falklands dispute with an oil-sanctions threat, citing Trump’s hint that the U.S. could shift its stance

+

Argentine President Javier Milei is sharply escalating the long-dormant Falklands (Malvinas) sovereignty dispute with Britain. In a televised address on Sept. 4, Milei vowed to fast-track legislation sanctioning companies that drill for oil near the islands without Buenos Aires’ approval — extending penalties to those firms’ shareholders, directors and suppliers. The target is the Sea Lion deep-water project, operated by Tel Aviv-listed Navitas Petroleum with London-listed Rockhopper Exploration holding a 35% stake. The field sits about 220 km north of the islands; the partners plan to begin drilling within months and to start pumping oil in 2028. Rockhopper, which describes Sea Lion as a ~1.7 billion-barrel resource, said it does not expect Milei’s move to have a “material effect” on its plans. Milei called the development a “clear and present danger” to Argentine interests.

The move is tied to remarks by Trump questioning Washington’s long-standing support for Britain on the islands. Milei praised Trump for suggesting the U.S. could reconsider its historically neutral position on sovereignty, casting it as proof his alignment with Washington is paying off: “The United States is considering this change in position because it knows that in Argentina, it has a reliable partner.” Britain pushed back firmly, its foreign minister calling the UK’s commitment “unwavering” and grounding islanders’ right to self-determination in international law. (Foreign Policy’s coverage is partly paywalled.)

Sources: CNBC, NPR, CNN, Al Jazeera
Southeast Asia · Transboundary Haze

Malaysia declares an air-quality emergency in Sarawak as wildfire haze from Indonesia hits hazardous levels

+

Malaysia on Friday, Sept. 4, declared an emergency in the Serian division of Sarawak state, on Borneo, after air pollution breached the country’s emergency threshold, driven by haze from fires in neighboring Indonesia. The Air Pollutant Index in Serian — an agricultural town of just under 90,000 near the Kalimantan border — surged to 521 on Friday, deep into hazardous territory, while the state capital Kuching read 311. Authorities ordered 647 schools closed from Monday through at least the end of the following week. Brunei, Singapore and other parts of Borneo have also been affected, with reports raising concern about the upcoming Singapore Formula 1 Grand Prix.

Indonesia’s Forestry Ministry reported satellite detection of 717 hot spots nationwide since Aug. 1, with 103 active in the prior 24 hours; elevated fire activity in Kalimantan and other provinces, plus prolonged dry conditions, has worsened the crisis. Jakarta said it has suspended the business licenses of five companies over alleged fires within their concessions and is investigating 167 cases involving the deliberate burning of about 4,700 hectares (11,700 acres) across nine provinces. The episode revives a recurring ASEAN friction over transboundary haze from land-clearing fires. (Figures per Malaysian and Indonesian authorities and wire reporting.)

Sources: Euronews, AP (via Phys.org), AP (via Houston Chronicle)

Technology · Semiconductors

Micron moves to double its high-bandwidth-memory output to ~100,000 wafers a month, closing the gap on Samsung and SK Hynix

+

Micron told investors it plans to roughly double its high-bandwidth-memory (HBM) production capacity by the end of 2026, targeting about 100,000 wafers per month — up from roughly 40,000–50,000 last year — by adding as much as 60,000 wafers/month of new capacity through aggressive year-end capital spending. The company said its newest 12-layer HBM4 is being scaled up for Nvidia’s next-generation “Vera Rubin” AI accelerators and is projected to reach up to 50% of Micron’s HBM output by year-end. Reported Sept. 4 by South Korea’s Electronic Times and by DigiTimes, the buildout would cut Micron’s capacity gap with market leaders Samsung and SK Hynix to roughly half its current level.

The move underscores how HBM has become the key bottleneck and profit center in AI hardware. Micron has said its HBM4 for Vera Rubin delivers roughly a 2x bandwidth improvement and about 20% better power efficiency over the prior generation, and that it entered high-volume HBM4 production earlier in 2026. Memory pricing has been climbing: Taiwanese makers Nanya and ESMT both reported record August revenue on stronger third-quarter contract prices, reflecting the same AI-memory demand surge driving Micron’s expansion. (DigiTimes is partially paywalled; figures cross-checked across trade coverage.)

Sources: Electronic Times, DigiTimes, Tom’s Hardware, Micron (release)
Energy & Climate · Grid / Batteries

Equinor brings its largest U.S. battery online — a 100 MW / 200 MWh project on the Texas grid

+

Norwegian energy company Equinor said its largest U.S. battery-storage project to date, the Citrus Flatts Energy Center, has begun commercial operations in Harlingen (Cameron County), in South Texas, adding 100 MW / 200 MWh of flexible capacity to the ERCOT grid. The facility was built and is operated by East Point Energy, Equinor’s wholly owned U.S. storage subsidiary, which had acquired the project from Black Mountain Energy Storage. The news was reported Sept. 3 by Electrek and the storage trade press.

Equinor said its next U.S. storage projects are already under construction in Virginia — four batteries totaling 80 MW / 160 MWh — expected to reach the PJM market in early 2027. The Texas project lands amid a record U.S. storage buildout: developers plan to add roughly 24 GW of utility-scale battery capacity in 2026 (up from about 15 GW completed in 2025), and storage plus solar and wind made up over 90% of new U.S. generating capacity in the first half of 2026. (Project details per Equinor and trade coverage.)

Energy & Climate · Siting Policy

A new Columbia survey tallies 888 state and local restrictions blocking wind and solar across the U.S.

+

The Sabin Center for Climate Change Law at Columbia released the sixth edition of its recurring survey, “Opposition to Renewable Energy Facilities in the United States,” covering developments through Dec. 31, 2025. It documents at least 888 state and local restrictions that block or sharply limit renewable-energy projects — 865 adopted locally and 23 at the state level across 19 states — plus at least 567 projects that faced significant local opposition across 48 states. Of the 888 restrictions, 416 targeted wind alone, 294 targeted solar alone, and 123 covered both. Alaska was the only state with no severe restrictions or major controversies identified.

The report notes that roughly 40% of the contested projects have been cancelled, and its authors attribute much of the resistance to organized local opposition often fueled by misinformation. Coverage ran across the trade press and the Sabin Center’s Climate Law Blog dated Sept. 3, with the findings still circulating in weekend roundups. The tally is a widely cited barometer of the permitting and siting friction slowing the U.S. clean-energy buildout even as solar-plus-storage dominates new capacity additions. (Per the Sabin Center report and trade coverage.)

Sources: Utility Dive, Columbia Climate Law Blog, Sabin Center (report), Construction Dive
Science · Biology

A lipid found in edible sea squirts reversed several signs of aging in old mice, a Stanford-led team reports

+

A Stanford-led team reported that dietary supplements of plasmalogens — a class of lipids abundant in edible sea squirts (ascidians) — reversed multiple markers of aging in older mice. Aged animals fed the supplements navigated a water-maze memory test significantly faster (closer to young mice), showed strengthened synaptic connections and reduced inflammation, and even regrew thicker, darker hair. The corresponding author framed the result as evidence that plasmalogens may not merely slow but partially reverse age-related cognitive decline. The work was covered Sept. 2 via ScienceDaily and EurekAlert.

The caveats are substantial: this is an animal-model study, and effects in mice do not establish that eating sea squirts or taking plasmalogen supplements will slow or reverse aging in people. The researchers stressed that further work on effective human doses, delivery and long-term safety is needed before any clinical claims — and popular framing of “a seafood that reverses aging” overstates the underlying mouse data. (Preclinical; human relevance not established.)

Sources: ScienceDaily, EurekAlert, SciTechDaily
