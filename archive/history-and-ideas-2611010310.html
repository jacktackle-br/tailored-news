History · Archaeology

Fingerprints pressed into the clay suggest women and children helped make China’s Terracotta Army

+

The Terracotta Army is usually imagined as the work of skilled male craftsmen working under the Qin state’s strict system of quality control, and many figures do carry the stamped names of master potters. A new study in the Journal of Cultural Heritage, led by Xiaoxi Li, looks instead at evidence the workers left by accident: fingerprints in the wet clay. The team examined 79 prints on 36 figures from one excavation unit, T23 in Pit 1. Fingerprint ridges are spaced more widely as people grow, and adult men tend to have wider spacing than women. Earlier studies struggled to tell adult women from teenage boys, so the researchers modelled all the prints together against modern reference data. Their estimate is that about 45% of the prints came from adult men, about a third from adult women, and about a fifth from children under 15.

The authors give their own caveats. The sample is small and comes from one unit. Inexperienced helpers may have handled the clay more and left more prints than master potters. And a European reference dataset may overstate the share of women and young people. Outlets have also reported the figures inconsistently. Still, the study fits other evidence: Qin lacquerware inscriptions name adult women, young women and young boys as workers in state-run workshops. If the pattern holds in other pits, the emperor’s army was a household and community effort as much as a guild project, and much of the labor behind it has gone unrecorded.

Sources: Smithsonian, Oct 28, Phys.org, Oct 2026, Archaeology Magazine, Oct 2026, VnExpress
Religion · Social Science

No revival, no collapse: Pew finds American religion holding steady, while the youngest adults attend services more than those a few years older

+

A Pew-Knight Initiative report released Oct. 28 takes on two competing stories about religion in America: that it is in steep decline, and that Gen Z is leading a revival. Pew’s data, including its 2026 National Public Opinion Reference Survey of 5,511 adults, supports neither. Since 2020, religious practice has “remained stable.” Roughly seven in ten adults still identify with a religious tradition, daily prayer is in the upper 40s percent, and attendance has recovered from its pandemic low. The more interesting findings are about age. Adults born from 2004 to 2008, roughly 18 to 22, attend at least monthly at a rate of 41%. That is higher than the 33% national figure and well above the 27% for those born from 2000 to 2003. Adults in their mid-20s are the least religious group on nearly every measure.

Pew is careful about what this means. On affiliation, prayer and how important people say religion is, today’s youngest adults are no more religious than people the same age were in 2014. The age subgroups are also small. One finding is new: among the youngest adults, men now report more religious observance than women, a reversal Pew has not seen before. Religion News Service led with the attendance numbers, and Catholic outlets framed the report as a challenge to the “revival” story. Pew’s own conclusion is that it is too soon to tell whether this is a blip or the start of a trend, since religious change is usually measured over decades.

Sources: Pew Research Center, Oct 28, Religion News Service, Oct 28, Catholic World Report / CNA, Oct 28, OSV News via Catholic Sun, Oct 30
History · Cities & Death

London’s railway for the dead: how overflowing Victorian churchyards led to a private train line for coffins and mourners

+

In a JSTOR Daily piece published Oct. 29, Amelia Soth looks at one of Victorian London’s strangest solutions to a public health crisis. By the mid-19th century, the city’s churchyards were badly overcrowded, with graves dug on top of graves. A private firm, the London Necropolis Company, proposed moving the problem out of town. It built Brookwood Cemetery in Surrey and ran a dedicated railway to it. From a private terminus near Waterloo, trains carried the corpse and the mourners out of the city together. The line opened in November 1854 and ran until April 1941, carrying a little over 200,000 dead.

The details show the period’s attitudes. Carriages and waiting rooms were divided by class and by religion, so the dead were segregated just as the living were. The company also drew critics. Soth highlights one who worried about the impropriety of a for-profit company handling the dead, an early version of arguments about the funeral industry that continue today. The piece is a good example of what JSTOR Daily does well, taking an odd footnote and using it to show how a growing industrial city rethought death, sanitation and the market at the same time.

Sources: JSTOR Daily (Soth), Oct 29, Background: London Necropolis Railway
