Press Freedom · Courts

The DOJ withdraws its subpoenas of three New York Times reporters after a judge threatens sanctions — as Trump insists “we’re after leakers”

+

The Justice Department has withdrawn subpoenas that sought grand-jury testimony and phone and text records from three New York Times reporters — and some of their family members — in a leak investigation tied to the Times’ reporting on security vulnerabilities in the Qatari-donated Boeing 747-8 being converted into a new Air Force One. The subpoenas were issued July 10; U.S. District Judge Arun Subramanian of the Southern District of New York halted their enforcement, signaled he would quash them, and raised the prospect of sanctions over “incorrect statements or misstatements” — including that a DOJ official had, in a sworn application, “falsely claimed that the investigation wasn’t public and failed to disclose that the targets were reporters.” The department called the missteps legal errors “which we own,” the product of “trying to move quickly,” and reversed course once sanctions were floated.

The matter stayed live Friday, July 24. Court filings revealed that Google had been subpoenaed on July 16 for “subscriber information associated with a phone number”; Google challenged the accompanying nondisclosure order on First Amendment grounds because the probe was already public, and Judge Subramanian granted the request and vacated the gag order. He also issued a Friday order demanding DOJ hand over its internal communications about seeking that nondisclosure order — a sign the case is not closed. Trump defended the effort from the Oval Office: “We’re not after journalists. We’re after leakers.” Press-freedom groups condemned the original subpoenas — the Reporters Committee’s Bruce Brown called them “a brazen and careless government overreach,” and the Committee to Protect Journalists described “an extraordinary escalation” with a “chilling effect.”

The Left-Leaning Take

The left casts this as the administration weaponizing the Justice Department against journalism — and getting caught. Slate argued Trump’s DOJ “targets the NYT for doing its job,” and Common Dreams amplified advocates’ “fight and you win” framing of the retreat. The emphasis: the subpoenas rested on misrepresentations to a court, were designed to intimidate the press and chill sources, and collapsed only because a judge threatened sanctions — not because the administration respects press freedom, as Trump’s Friday defense underscored.

The Right-Leaning Take

The right splits rather than speaking with one voice. Free-speech-oriented conservatives and libertarians (FIRE) welcomed the withdrawal as a First Amendment win while warning the episode still left a “chilling effect.” Pro-Trump Republicans (Rep. Elise Stefanik) backed the leak investigation and the underlying subpoenas, aligning with Trump’s “leakers, not journalists” framing and the national-security stakes of a leak about the new jet. (No in-window National Review or WSJ editorial-board piece surfaced — the mainstream-right editorial view is provisional; a Bulwark-style defense of the press would be intra-right dissent, not the pro-Trump line.)

Sources: Axios, Washington Post, PBS NewsHour, CNBC, Courthouse News, Slate
Trade · Now In Effect

Trump reimposes near-global tariffs under a forced-labor law — and is sued within hours

+

Overnight into Friday, July 24, the administration imposed new tariffs of 10% and 12.5% on goods from roughly 60 trading partners — including the European Union and China — using Section 301 of the Trade Act, alleging those economies failed to curb imports made with forced labor. The action was timed to take effect as a temporary 10% global tariff expired, and the White House says the covered partners account for about 99% of U.S. imports (Reuters counted 59 countries plus the EU). It is billed as the first step in rebuilding Trump’s near-global tariff wall after the Supreme Court in February 2026 struck down his earlier “reciprocal” duties, which had rested on the IEEPA national-emergencies law. China and Japan face the higher 12.5% tier; the UK — not a target here — said the move would have no negative effect, citing improved terms including zero tariffs on whisky and medical technology. A further Section 301 “excess capacity” investigation targeting 16 partners is still to come.

Within hours of the tariffs taking effect, two U.S. small businesses sued in the Court of International Trade in New York, arguing the policy exceeds the president’s authority to tax imports and that Section 301 requires the detailed, country-specific findings the administration did not make. Trading partners disputed the justification — Brazil accused Washington of “manipulat[ing] an issue of great importance to human rights and the struggles of workers worldwide.” Market reaction was muted, with bond yields edging up on inflation risk while attention stayed on the Middle East. Analysts were split: Section 301 is a sturdier statutory footing than the untested IEEPA basis, but many (including the Peterson Institute) called the forced-labor rationale legally “flimsy” and likely to draw a prolonged court fight.

The Left-Leaning Take

Left and center-left commentary treated the forced-labor rationale as a transparent pretext for protectionism. The Washington Post editorial board called it “clearly a pretext,” noting a genuine anti-forced-labor policy would not hit Japan, South Korea, Switzerland and New Zealand with the same duties as China; Talking Points Memo called the tariffs set under “sham pretenses.” House Ways and Means ranking Democrat Richard Neal said the “forced labor justification is too convenient to be taken seriously” and should not be “cheapened into a pretext… built on dubious legal theories and personal grievances.”

The Right-Leaning Take

The libertarian right is hostile. Reason argued the tariffs are “likely illegal too,” calling the forced-labor action “obviously flimsy” and a misuse of a “targeted, country-specific and practice-specific remedial authority” that is “not a freestanding authorization” to broadly tax imports — echoing the constitutional point that the tariff power belongs to Congress. Reason conceded a court might defer to the president’s determinations, but its posture is skeptical and anti-tariff. (No in-window WSJ editorial or National Review piece specifically on these tariffs surfaced — broader free-trade-right opposition is provisional.)

War Powers · Congress vs. the President

The bombing of Iran nears a 13th night as Congress’s rebuke stalls — Trump says Tehran needs “more of the same”

+

The U.S. air campaign against Iran ran for roughly its 13th consecutive night into July 24–25, after a late-June/early-July ceasefire collapsed; Iran’s health ministry reported 59 people killed and 666 injured since June 27. Trump said the two sides remain in contact and that Tehran is “getting more serious,” but signaled he is not ready to accept a truce — mediators floated a 10-day ceasefire that Washington downplayed, with Trump saying “they need more of the same.” He described two exit paths: “a military exit where we just keep going” or “a smarter strategy” of a deal. Spillover widened: the U.S. military said it disabled a tanker trying to bypass its blockade of Iranian ports, oil topped $100 a barrel, and Saudi Arabia struck Iran-backed Houthi targets in Yemen after a Red Sea attack on a Saudi vessel.

The war reignited a constitutional fight in Congress. On Thursday, July 23, the House passed, for a second time, a nonbinding concurrent resolution 214–208 calling on Trump to end hostilities, with four Republicans — Warren Davidson (Ohio), Brian Fitzpatrick (Pa.), Tom Barrett (Mich.) and Thomas Massie (Ky.) — joining Democrats; NPR attributed the measure to Rep. Pramila Jayapal (D-Wash.). The Senate, 49–47, fell short of discharging a binding joint resolution from Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.), with Sen. Susan Collins (R-Maine) again crossing over. Most Republicans embraced the administration’s argument that the weeks-long ceasefire “reset” the War Powers Resolution’s 90-day clock by creating a new conflict — a theory Massie rejected, accusing the administration of “breaking the law.” A Politico poll cited a 13-point drop since May in MAGA voters who say the war is worth its economic cost.

The Left-Leaning Take

The left frames the war as an unauthorized, spiraling blunder. Left-leaning outlets (AlterNet) argue that with “amateurs in charge and experts purged, Trump has no way out,” and Democratic military veterans have become the loudest critics — Rep. Sara Jacobs called it “one of, if not the biggest, blunders in American foreign policy.” The core charge: Trump has plunged the U.S. into its largest conflict since Iraq and Afghanistan with no congressional authorization, and his “reset-the-clock” theory is a dodge to evade the War Powers Resolution.

The Right-Leaning Take

The right is genuinely divided. National Review (“A War Restrained by the People”) frames the story around Trump’s ambitions being checked by an unenthusiastic public — a majority disapprove of his handling of Iran — reading as restraint rather than cheerleading. Anti-interventionist and libertarian conservatives (Massie, Reason-adjacent voices) side with the war-powers critique outright, while the pro-Trump majority defends the campaign on the “new conflict” clock theory and argues Congress cannot micromanage an active operation. (The Bulwark, as anti-Trump intra-right dissent, would align with the critics; no specific in-window piece confirmed — provisional.)

Europe · Russia’s War on Ukraine

A Russian ballistic missile hits a Ukrainian defense expo near Kyiv, killing about 10 — and Moscow says it aimed for it

+

A Russian daytime ballistic-missile strike on July 24 hit a defense-industry exhibition in the Kyiv region, killing at least 10 people and wounding roughly 100, according to Ukrainian officials and President Volodymyr Zelensky. Russia’s Defense Ministry took the unusual step of publicly claiming it deliberately targeted the event — an exhibition showcasing Ukrainian and foreign-made drones. The strike came the same day Zelensky said he was in talks with a U.S. company to jointly produce Patriot air-defense systems. Russia fired a mix of cruise and ballistic missiles (reports cite Oniks cruise missiles plus ballistic missiles, one intercepted) alongside a broader overnight barrage of roughly 180 drones.

Zelensky said he convened his top commanders — including Commander-in-Chief Mykhailo Drapatyi and acting Defense Minister Yevhenii Khmara — to set “response measures,” and warned that Russia is preparing a major missile attack within 48 hours and a “significant” mobilization wave in the fall. Ukrainian strikes on Russia’s Kirov region killed six, and Ukrainian drones hit warehouses and energy infrastructure in Belgorod overnight into July 25. The significance: Russia openly claiming a deliberate strike on a defense expo — even as Kyiv courts U.S. Patriot co-production — signals both an intent to hit Ukraine’s arms-production base and the deadlock in any peace track; it was the deadliest single reported Kyiv-region strike in the window.

Middle East · Gaza

A global food monitor says Gaza’s famine has eased but stays fragile — with two-thirds at risk of acute hunger by year’s end

+

The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) — the leading global authority on food insecurity — reported that the famine conditions declared in parts of Gaza last year have eased, crediting reduced fighting and more Israeli-permitted aid, but warned the gains are fragile and that conditions remain at “crisis” levels. It projected that more than two-thirds of Gaza’s population could face acute hunger by year-end if humanitarian agencies keep cutting aid flows amid funding shortfalls.

On July 24, Israel issued displacement orders and then struck Gaza, killing at least two Palestinians, with air strikes destroying homes in the Bureij and Jabalia refugee camps and the Zeitoun and Tal al-Hawa neighborhoods of Gaza City, per Al Jazeera. Gaza’s health ministry says Israeli strikes and shelling have killed more than 1,185 people since last October’s U.S.-brokered ceasefire. The significance: this is the first authoritative signal that Gaza’s famine has receded, but the IPC’s warning of renewed mass hunger — driven by aid-funding cuts rather than access alone — reframes the crisis and raises pressure on donors and on the durability of the shaky ceasefire.

Sources: Washington Post (partial — paywalled), AP via Detroit News, Al Jazeera

Space · Interstellar Visitor

China’s Tianwen-1 delivers a rare Mars-side close-up of interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS

+

China’s Tianwen-1 Mars orbiter has returned rare images of 3I/ATLAS — only the third confirmed interstellar object ever to pass through our solar system, after 1I/‘Oumuamua (2017) and 2I/Borisov (2019). According to the China National Space Administration, the spacecraft’s high-resolution camera imaged the comet from roughly 30 million kilometers away as it raced past Mars, clearly resolving a nucleus wrapped in a glowing coma several thousand kilometers across, plus a developing tail. The observations span three epochs between Sept. 30 and Oct. 3, 2025, and the sequence shows visible changes in coma and tail morphology driven by rapidly shifting viewing geometry.

The scientific value lies in the vantage point: because Tianwen-1 sat well out of the comet’s orbital plane and far closer than any Earth-based telescope, it captured a perspective essentially impossible from the ground — helping constrain the object’s structure and how material born around another star behaves as it heats near the Sun. 3I/ATLAS was first spotted on May 7, 2025, and remains one of the most closely watched objects in astronomy.

Sources: ScienceDaily, Space.com, SciTechDaily, Universe Today
Technology · Semiconductors

Intel weighs SK Hynix as an operating partner for its stalled $28 billion Ohio chip fab

+

Intel is seeking an outside partner to help operate its long-delayed Ohio semiconductor campus, and South Korea’s SK Hynix — the world’s leading maker of high-bandwidth memory (HBM) for AI accelerators — is among the candidates under evaluation, according to reporting from Semafor (July 22), TrendForce (July 23) and DIGITIMES (July 24). The talks are said to be at a very early stage with no formal negotiations, and the arrangement being floated is an operating partnership rather than an outright sale; it follows SK Hynix publicly denying a South Korean media report that it was negotiating to acquire the site.

The context is Intel’s stumbling foundry buildout: the company broke ground in Ohio in 2022 and pledged $28 billion, but missed its 2025 opening target and has pushed first output to around 2030. Bringing in a partner — especially a memory heavyweight riding the AI-driven HBM boom — would help Intel defray the capital burden as it continues a painful restructuring. Biomedicine · Drug Approval

The FDA approves centanafadine (Simtriyo), the first new-class ADHD drug in decades

+

On July 24, 2026 — its target decision date — the FDA approved Otsuka Pharmaceutical’s centanafadine (brand name Simtriyo) for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder in adults and children aged 6 and older. It is the first FDA-approved norepinephrine-dopamine-serotonin reuptake inhibitor (NDSRI) for ADHD — a genuinely new mechanistic class among the small roster of non-stimulant options — delivered as a once-daily, extended-release capsule that blocks reuptake of all three neurotransmitters.

Approval rests on four pivotal Phase 3 trials showing statistically significant symptom improvement versus placebo, with an earlier onset (benefit by week 1, sustained through week 6) more characteristic of stimulants — notable because existing non-stimulants typically act slowly. Otsuka also reported positive Phase 3b data in June in adults with ADHD and comorbid anxiety. The drug had been granted Priority Review after its application was accepted in January 2026.

Sources: Psychiatric Times, Medical Daily, Otsuka
Energy · Electrification

GM finishes folding ChargePoint into its Energy Pass app, reaching about 60% of U.S. Level 2 chargers

+

On July 24, 2026, General Motors said it has finished onboarding ChargePoint’s U.S. network into its Energy Pass charging platform, letting Chevrolet, GMC and Cadillac EV drivers find, activate and pay for charging — including Plug & Charge sessions — directly from their vehicle-brand app, without separate charging accounts. GM says the addition brings Energy Pass to roughly 60% of all U.S. Level 2 (AC) chargers; when EVgo is folded in later in 2026, the platform is expected to reach nearly 70% of U.S. DC fast chargers plus about 60% of public Level 2 plugs.

The move consolidates a fragmented U.S. public-charging experience — long cited as a barrier to EV adoption — into a single automaker-native interface, and follows GM and ChargePoint adding hundreds of new DC fast-charging locations over the past year across Michigan, California, Florida and upstate New York.
