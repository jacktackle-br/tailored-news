AI · The Safety Fight Goes Political

Amodei, Altman and Musk call to slow AI down — and Trump says no: ‘Whoever wins AI wins’

+

A weekend fight over the pace of artificial intelligence spilled into open politics. In a roughly 3,800-word essay published Saturday, Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei argued that development of the most advanced systems must be slowed to keep AI from slipping beyond human control — a call endorsed, per Bloomberg, by OpenAI’s Sam Altman and xAI’s Elon Musk. The Washington Post reported that leaders at Anthropic, OpenAI and Google have discussed standing up a new AI-safety body. The alarm intensified after an Anthropic researcher quit last week warning the technology could “kill us all by the end of the decade.”

President Trump dismissed the appeals on Sunday, saying a slowdown was unnecessary and would surrender America’s edge over China: “We’re leading China in AI… whoever wins AI wins.” House Speaker Mike Johnson echoed the competitiveness framing, while congressional Democrats pointed to public unease about jobs and safety. (CEOs’ characterizations of catastrophic risk are contested forecasts, not established fact; note that Anthropic is the maker of this brief’s underlying model.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a president waving away an extraordinary warning from the very people building the technology, choosing a China arms-race frame over the public’s stake in guardrails, labor protections and accountability. If the builders themselves are pleading for a brake, the argument runs, the answer isn’t “go faster” — it’s democratic oversight before the harms, not after. (Directional; opinion desks not individually reachable this cycle.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream directional right largely backs Trump: ceding the frontier to Beijing would be strategic malpractice, and a federally blessed “safety body” risks regulatory capture by incumbents pulling up the ladder. Libertarians (Reason) are doubly wary — skeptical of doom narratives and of the government controls they’d justify — while some on the anti-Trump right (The Bulwark, a dissent, not the mainstream view) fault the administration for treating existential-risk warnings as a punchline. (Directional.)

Courts · Visas

A Boston judge blocks the DHS visa clampdown on foreign students and journalists a day before it took effect

+

U.S. District Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV in Boston — a George W. Bush appointee — on Monday blocked a Department of Homeland Security rule that would have capped how long foreign students and journalists may stay in the United States without seeking an extension, ruling for a coalition of unions and advocacy groups one day before the rule was to take effect. The rule would have limited F student and J exchange visas to four years and cut I journalist visas — now often open-ended — to 240 days.

Saylor called the policy “irrational,” its justification “exceptionally weak,” and its national-security rationale a claim that “borders on the absurd,” warning the damage to higher education and the economy would be “catastrophic.” He ordered the start date postponed indefinitely while the case proceeds. (Facts from the ruling and wire/legal-press coverage; the administration is expected to appeal.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads this as another court catching an immigration crackdown built on pretext — a rule that would hollow out universities and chill foreign correspondents, dressed up in security language a judge found nearly laughable. It fits a pattern, the argument goes, of policy by decree that can’t survive contact with the administrative record. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream directional right argues the executive has broad, legitimate authority to set the terms of temporary visas and to end open-ended stays that invite abuse, and bristles at a district judge overriding it nationwide. A more institutionalist strand (The Dispatch) still cautions that losing on “arbitrary and capricious” grounds points to sloppy rulemaking, not judicial overreach. (Directional.)

NATO · Russia’s War

Russian drones strike near the Polish border, and Tusk warns the escalation could reach NATO soil

+

Poland pledged to fortify its frontier with Ukraine after a wave of Russian drone strikes near the border — two of them landing within a few kilometers of Polish territory. Prime Minister Donald Tusk warned that “these coming weeks and months will be a time of very intensified actions on the Russian side, and… we cannot rule out that this escalation will also affect our territory,” adding he would consult NATO allies on a possible joint response. NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte said the strikes would push the alliance to increase its support for Ukraine, calling them a sign of Russian “desperation.” The escalation extends the pattern in this morning’s brief — Ukraine’s long-range strikes on Russian refineries and the Trump–Zelensky rift — but shifts the risk onto NATO’s eastern edge, where a stray or deliberate strike on member soil would test the alliance’s Article 5 tripwire. (Border-incident details rest partly on Polish government accounts and remain provisional.)

Gaza · The Fraying Truce

The UN sounds the alarm as strikes across Gaza kill civilians and UN staff despite the ceasefire

+

The UN humanitarian office (OCHA) voiced “deep concern” on Monday over Israeli strikes hitting densely populated areas across Gaza and continuing to kill and injure civilians, nearly a year into the fragile ceasefire that took hold on Oct. 10, 2025. The UN said six UN staff and civilians were killed in a strike on a school sheltering displaced families, and UNICEF reported at least nine children killed over the past week. By OCHA’s tally, local authorities have recorded more than 1,300 deaths since the truce began, with military operations reported across Gaza over the weekend.

Israel says its operations target militants and disputes elements of the casualty accounts; the competing figures come largely from UN agencies and Gaza’s local authorities and cannot be independently verified. The strikes land amid sharpening Israeli domestic politics — Netanyahu’s Likud moved to petition an election panel to bar Arab-majority parties — underscoring how strained the post-war order remains even with the guns nominally silent. (Casualty tallies are attributed claims, not independently confirmed.)

Sources: UN News, Al Jazeera, The Times of Israel
Mediterranean · Migration

A migrant boat from Libya sinks south of Crete, leaving one dead and about 25 missing

+

A boat carrying migrants from Libya toward Greece took on water and sank roughly 75 km (47 miles) south of the island of Gavdos, near Crete, leaving one dead and about 25 people missing, Greek authorities said Monday. Survivors reported 77 aboard when it went down; 51 were plucked from the sea by two vessels in the area, and a search continued with patrol boats, a helicopter, a drone and commercial ships, assisted by the EU border agency Frontex, as high winds hampered the effort.

The wreck is the latest on the increasingly used Libya–Crete corridor, a roughly 320 km stretch that has become a favored route for asylum seekers as other Mediterranean crossings tighten — and a recurring flashpoint in Europe’s unresolved argument over migration and rescue at sea. (Figures are survivor and coast-guard accounts and may change as the search continues.)

Courts · FEMA

A federal judge blocks the plan to halve FEMA, calling the staffing math ‘pulled from thin air’

+

In a decision issued late Friday, U.S. District Judge Susan Illston in San Francisco ruled that the Department of Homeland Security acted unlawfully when it moved to cut the Federal Emergency Management Agency’s workforce by roughly half. The judge called the plan — which aimed to shed more than 11,000 FEMA employees by the end of September — “arbitrary and capricious,” writing that “the FEMA staffing plan number appears as if pulled from thin air.”

Illston found the effort violated the Post-Katrina Emergency Management Reform Act, which bars DHS from “substantially or significantly” reducing FEMA’s authorities, responsibilities or functions, and said the department had usurped the agency’s control over its own personnel. She ordered the two sides to agree on the “scope of relief” by Oct. 9; absent a deal, the court will impose one. The ruling lands mid-hurricane-season, amid the administration’s broader drive to shrink and reshape the disaster agency. (Facts from the ruling and wire/Tier-1 coverage; the administration is expected to contest the outcome.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads this as a court halting a reckless gutting of the one agency Americans rely on after floods and hurricanes — an ideological headcount cut dressed up as efficiency, with lives on the line. It fits a pattern of the executive trying to hollow out statutory functions Congress explicitly protected. (Directional; opinion pages not individually reachable this cycle.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream directional right defends the drive to streamline a bloated, often-criticized FEMA and bristles at yet another district judge second-guessing executive management. A more institutionalist note (The Dispatch) concedes the point of process: if a statute limits how far you can cut, you follow it — and losing in court on “arbitrary and capricious” grounds is an own-goal. (Directional.)

Sources: Washington Post, The Hill, Detroit News, UPI
Justice Dept. · The ‘Weaponization’ Fund

The DOJ moves to revive the $1.78 billion fund Blanche once pronounced ‘dead’

+

The Justice Department notified a federal court on Friday that it will appeal to the 11th Circuit, challenging U.S. District Judge Kathleen Williams’ July ruling that gutted a settlement built around Trump-family litigation against the IRS and Treasury. Williams had found the family’s roughly $10 billion suit “improper,” brought only to legitimize a deal under which DOJ would stand up a $1.776 billion fund to compensate the president’s allies — among them supporters convicted in the Jan. 6 Capitol attack.

Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche had told senators during his confirmation fight that the “anti-weaponization” fund was dead; the appeal revives it. Separately, a sweeping audit-immunity arrangement covering the president, his sons and the Trump Organization remains in place — an arrangement critics say could erase millions in back taxes. (Facts from wire and legal-press reporting; case posture and figures are as reported and provisional pending the appeal.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left calls this self-dealing in the open: a president suing his own government to conjure a taxpayer-funded payout for loyalists — Jan. 6 defendants included — while pocketing personal tax immunity, and a confirmation promise now revealed as expedient. The word it reaches for is corruption. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream directional right frames the fund as redress for real Biden-era “weaponization” of the DOJ and IRS. But this is a story with unusual intra-right unease: fiscal conservatives and libertarians (Reason) balk at a nearly $2 billion slush fund with no oversight, and the anti-Trump right (The Bulwark — a dissent, not the mainstream view) treats reviving a fund Blanche swore off as a bad-faith tell. (Directional.)

Iran War · Chokepoints

Drones shut Saudi Arabia’s East–West pipeline and the Houthis seize the Bab el-Mandeb, squeezing the world’s oil arteries

+

Saudi Arabia shut its 1,200 km East–West (Petroline) pipeline — the kingdom’s principal route for moving crude to the Red Sea and bypassing the Strait of Hormuz — after a drone attack triggered fires late last week. The drones reportedly flew from Iraq; President Trump blamed Iran, an attribution no other government, including Riyadh, has publicly made. To the south, Iran-backed Houthi forces announced they had completed the seizure of three strategic islands at the mouth of the Bab el-Mandeb — the strait carrying roughly 10% of global trade — along with the port of Mocha.

With Hormuz effectively closed to Western-allied shipping since May and an IRGC toll regime charging transiting vessels, Tehran’s axis is now positioned astride both chokepoints flanking the Arabian Peninsula. U.S. strikes on Iranian tankers have continued (wire tallies put the count around eight in ten days), and Oman-brokered Iran–Gulf talks on managing Hormuz traffic were postponed after Sunday’s deadly strike on an Iranian cargo ship off Qeshm. At the IAEA’s annual conference in Vienna, Iran accused Washington of blocking its nuclear chief’s travel and summoned the Austrian envoy. (Battlefield and attribution claims rest partly on combatant and government accounts and are provisional.)

Tech · AI Infrastructure

The AI build-out turns into a power play: Nvidia backs OpenAI’s Ohio data centers with up to $105 billion

+

Nvidia is providing up to $105 billion in financing for a vast new OpenAI data center in Ohio, backing an initial 4.25 gigawatts of computing capacity with an option to reach 8 GW. The chipmaker is also investing $1.5 billion in SoftBank-backed developer SB Energy, which will build power to support roughly 10 GW and pour more than $4.2 billion into regional grid infrastructure, with capacity phasing in from 2028. It sits inside a broader OpenAI–Nvidia plan to deploy 10 GW of systems for as much as $100 billion.

The deals fuse three things once handled separately — chips, electricity and capital — into a single race to stand up AI compute, and they underscore how power availability, not silicon, is becoming the binding constraint. Skeptics warn the arrangements look circular: a chipmaker financing a customer’s purchase of its own chips, raising questions about how much of the demand is real. (Figures are the companies’ own disclosures.)

Sources: CNBC, Nvidia Newsroom, Fortune
Energy · Geothermal

Google signs the largest enhanced-geothermal deal on record to feed a Utah data center

+

Fervo Energy and Google have signed a 396 MW power-purchase agreement — the largest enhanced-geothermal (EGS) PPA on record — to advance Fervo’s Cape Station project in Utah, due online in 2028. Google holds an option to expand its offtake by about 600 MW by June 2030, which would bring the total to nearly 1 gigawatt, and plans to use the carbon-free power for a potential Utah data center.

The deal is a milestone for “next-generation” geothermal, which borrows horizontal-drilling and fracturing techniques from shale to tap heat in hot dry rock almost anywhere — offering the round-the-clock clean power that solar and wind can’t supply alone. It builds on Fervo’s 2023 Nevada pilot, Project Red, and lands squarely inside the same AI-driven scramble for firm electricity now reshaping the grid. (Terms are the companies’ disclosures.)

Science · Neuroscience

A hidden layer of Alzheimer’s: the genome’s 3D folding is scrambled in vulnerable brain cells

+

Writing in Science, researchers from Carnegie Mellon, the University of Pittsburgh and the University of Washington report that the three-dimensional organization of DNA is disrupted in several types of brain cells affected by Alzheimer’s disease, altering how key genes are switched on and off. Mapping the changes across intact brain tissue, they found the genome’s reorganization tracked with shifts in gene activity and in how brain cells were arranged.

The team combined single-cell sequencing, spatial mapping of tissue, and a new deep-learning model — Hicformer — that predicts gene activity from DNA sequence and genome-folding patterns. The work exposes a previously underexplored layer of the disease and could open fresh routes to understanding and, eventually, treating it. (Reported around Sept. 12–13; a research finding awaiting replication and clinical translation.)

Sources: ScienceDaily, Carnegie Mellon, Technology Networks
Science · Foundations of Physics

A century in, the largest-ever survey finds physicists still can’t agree what quantum mechanics means

+

Nature’s largest-ever survey of physicists on the foundations of their field finds strikingly little consensus on the deepest questions: whether a particle can truly be in two places at once, whether there are many universes, and whether the mathematical objects underpinning the theory correspond to anything real. The most popular answer — the Copenhagen interpretation — drew just 36%, and among those who specialize in quantum foundations, only about 20% favored it.

The split runs along a basic fault line between “realists,” who want physics to describe a visualizable world, and those holding an “epistemic” view that treats the theory as being only about knowledge and predictions. A companion result from the same survey effort even puts the Standard Model of cosmology under fresh scrutiny — a reminder that a hundred years on, the science that runs modern technology still rests on contested philosophical ground. (Reported around Sept. 12; a survey of expert opinion, not an experimental result.)

Sources: ScienceDaily, SciTechDaily, Phys.org
