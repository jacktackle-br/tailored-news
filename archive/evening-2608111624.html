The White House · National Security

The story that broke today: Trump was secretly spirited off Air Force One in a catering truck over an Iranian assassination threat — and the White House told the public and the press he was still aboard the decoy jet

+

A Washington Post report published Monday night and matched Tuesday by CNN, NBC, CBS and others revealed that when President Trump left the NATO summit in Ankara last month — on July 8 — he boarded the aging Boeing 747 Air Force One in front of cameras, then was quietly moved back down to the tarmac inside an airport catering truck and transferred to a smaller U.S. Air Force C-32A, which flew him to RAF Mildenhall in Britain. The 747 took off as a decoy with staff and the traveling press aboard, shades drawn, believing the president was with them; the White House publicly stated he was on Air Force One. The extraordinary ruse was prompted by Israeli intelligence warning that Iran was plotting to shoot down his plane, reportedly with shoulder-fired or anti-aircraft missiles, in the days around Ayatollah Ali Khamenei’s July burial.

Key caveats are already contested. Two U.S. officials told Israel’s Channel 12 the intelligence reflected general Iranian chatter about killing the president rather than a specific, operational plot, and Turkish officials have suggested the Israeli warning may have been engineered to derail the U.S.–Iran diplomacy underway at the time. Senior aides, including Secretary of State Marco Rubio, are reported to have stayed on the decoy aircraft. The maneuver concealed the president’s true location from the public and many senior officials for hours. (Facts from The Washington Post, CNN, NBC, CBS and Al Jazeera via in-window search summaries; the WaPo scoop itself was paywalled/blocked in-window — partial.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The critique centers on the deception, not the security. CNN’s analysis (“Trump’s secret plane swap is more problematic than it might seem”) argues the real problem is that the White House affirmatively told the public a falsehood about the president’s whereabouts and used the traveling press corps as unwitting decoys — a transparency and accountability breach, whatever the threat. At least one Democratic senator called the episode “downright scary” (Newsweek), pressing for answers on who authorized the public misdirection. (Directional; no in-window Vox / Slate / Mother Jones / Guardian column was isolated.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream-right framing is vindication: the Secret Service took “extraordinary measures” against a genuine Iranian assassination threat and got the president out safely, evidence the hard line on Tehran is warranted (Fox News). Operational secrecy, on this view, is exactly what you’d want and briefly misleading the public about a live security movement is defensible. A skeptical strand — echoing the Turkish claim that the intel may have been hyped — questions whether Israel’s warning was as solid as advertised. (Directional; no clean in-window National Review / Dispatch / WSJ-editorial column surfaced.)

Colombia · Disaster

Colombia’s strongest earthquake this century: the toll from the magnitude-7.4 Chocó quake climbs past 220 through the day, with more than 2,700 reported missing

+

The magnitude-7.4 earthquake that struck western Colombia on Monday — epicentered near San José del Palmar in the department of Chocó, roughly 400 km (250 miles) west of Bogotá, and felt in Ecuador and Panama — is now Colombia’s most powerful quake this century, the country’s Geological Service says. The confirmed death toll climbed through Tuesday from about 132 on Monday to at least 224 by evening (CNN and ABC put it above 200), with more than 2,700 people reported missing, dozens of buildings collapsed and over 1,500 homes damaged. More than 70 aftershocks have been recorded. Hard-hit cities include Cali, Pereira, Quibdó and Manizales.

The disaster is an early crisis for President Abelardo de la Espriella, who said the government had mobilized the “entire military and police apparatus” and declared a national emergency. Rescue is complicated by geography: much of Chocó is reachable only by boat or plane, and crews have not yet reached the epicenter area, so the true toll is expected to rise as more remote communities are surveyed. Figures were moving fast in-window and vary by source. (Facts from CNN, Al Jazeera, NBC, ABC and WaPo via in-window search summaries.)

Sources: CNN, Al Jazeera, NBC News, Washington Post

Indo-Pacific · Nuclear

Washington publicly rebukes Beijing over its Pacific missile test, saying China gave the U.S. no advance notice — deepening a regional divide over militarization

+

The United States on Tuesday sharpened its criticism of China over last month’s launch of a nuclear-capable ballistic missile into the Pacific — a roughly 7,300-km flight that landed in the South Pacific and marked, by several analysts’ accounts, the first time China has fired such a missile into international open waters. Washington complained that Beijing gave insufficient warning: Australia, Japan and New Zealand were notified in advance, but the U.S. was not, and American officials framed the test as evidence of an “opaque” and rapid nuclear buildup at odds with nonproliferation efforts (Bloomberg, State Department).

The diplomatic friction is deepening a Pacific divide over militarization. Regional governments that had championed an “Ocean of Peace” framework find that ambition tested by great-power missile launches through their waters, and the U.S.–China exchange over notification norms underscores the absence of a launch-notification agreement between the two nuclear powers. The test itself was July 6; the fresh development is the escalating war of words and its regional fallout. (Facts from Bloomberg, the U.S. State Department, NPR and The Diplomat via in-window search summaries.)

Public Health · Vaccine Policy

Trump signs an executive order slashing the list of recommended childhood vaccines and reviving the debunked vaccine-autism claim — cutting the diseases every child is advised to be immunized against to 11 and splitting the MMR shot, with RFK Jr. at his side

+

On Monday, Aug. 10, President Trump signed an executive order at the White House directing the federal government to cut the number of routinely recommended childhood vaccines and to restructure the childhood immunization schedule. Branded “Gold Standard Childhood Vaccination Recommendations,” the order calls for children to be vaccinated against 11 diseases, down from what most outlets describe as 17–18 (the baseline figure varies by source); it directs that the combined measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) shot be split into separate injections, that children where possible be vaccinated in separate visits, and it tiers some shots as recommended for all children versus only higher-risk children. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., NIH Director Jay Bhattacharya and deputy chief of staff Stephen Miller attended the signing. Trump tied the order explicitly to autism, saying the condition should be included “in particular” and claiming that today’s high autism rates “did not exist” decades ago, when children received fewer shots.

Public-health experts and reporting note the autism claim is false: the rise in diagnoses is attributed largely to broader awareness and expanded diagnostic criteria, and large studies — a Danish cohort of 657,461 children and a U.S. study of 95,727 — found no link between MMR and autism, while the CDC says combining vaccines is safe. The order lands amid a 35-year-high measles caseload in the United States. Physicians’ groups pushed back immediately, and because courts have previously blocked the administration from unilaterally rewriting the vaccine schedule, the order is expected to draw fresh legal challenges. (Facts from NPR, CNN, CBS, STAT and CNBC; some pages were reachable only in summary form in-window, and the “11 diseases, down from 17 vs 18” discrepancy is flagged.)

The Left-Leaning Take

In-window left commentary (The New Republic, Aug. 10) casts the order as policy built on “right-wing conspiracies and misinformation” — RFK Jr. and Trump institutionalizing anti-vaccine pseudoscience at the very moment measles is surging, an active danger to children dressed up as “health freedom.” Slate’s health coverage runs parallel, urging that career scientists and FDA approvals be shielded from Kennedy’s influence. (Directional beyond the cited pieces; Vox / Mother Jones / Guardian columns were not reachable in-window.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The cleanest in-window response from the right is skepticism, not defense. The Dispatch (Grayson Logue, “Donald Trump Is the Real Vaccine Skeptic”) argues Trump himself — not just Kennedy — is now driving the vaccine skepticism. The Bulwark (anti-Trump right; used here only to mark the intra-right split) reports Trump told aides Kennedy “will be a failure” if he doesn’t deliver on the autism claim. The nearest thing to a defense is the libertarian lane (Reason), sympathetic to a leaner “shared clinical decision-making” schedule as a correction to COVID-era over-recommendation. (No clean pro-order National Review / WSJ editorial surfaced in-window.)

Sources: NPR, CNN, CBS News, STAT, The Dispatch, The New Republic

Turkey · Kurdish Peace Process

Turkey’s parliament passes a conditional pardon for thousands of PKK fighters — a milestone in the disarmament process — but pointedly excludes jailed founder Abdullah Öcalan

+

On Monday, Aug. 10, Turkey’s parliament voted 468–88 to approve a 12-article law creating a conditional pardon and reintegration framework for thousands of Kurdistan Workers’ Party (PKK) members — a milestone in the peace initiative launched after the PKK announced last year it would end its decades-long armed campaign and dissolve itself, responding to a call from its imprisoned leader, Abdullah Öcalan. The law only takes effect once Turkish authorities verify the PKK has fully disarmed, and it sets out procedures for disarmament and rehabilitation.

Crucially, the law excludes Öcalan himself, whose release the PKK has made a central demand — a gap that could complicate implementation. Fighters have signaled they will not lay down weapons and return to Turkey unless they can “engage in democratic politics on the basis of freedom of thought and freedom of association.” The vote is nonetheless a significant step toward ending one of the region’s longest-running insurgencies, a conflict that has killed tens of thousands since the 1980s, with implications for Turkey’s Kurdish politics and for Kurdish groups in Syria and Iraq. (Facts from NPR, NBC, France 24 and AP via in-window search summaries.)

Sources: NPR, NBC News, France 24, Las Vegas Sun / AP
Russia–Ukraine · Deep Strikes

Ukrainian drones hit the Taneco refinery in Tatarstan and a petrochemical plant deep in Siberia — a strike on Nizhnekamsk kills 13 — in some of the war’s deepest attacks yet

+

Overnight into Monday, Aug. 10, Ukraine carried out some of its deepest strikes yet against Russia’s energy sector. Drones hit the large Taneco oil refinery — design capacity more than 300,000 barrels a day — in Nizhnekamsk, Tatarstan, about 900 km (560 miles) east of Moscow, setting off a fire. The strikes fit Ukraine’s escalating campaign to degrade Russia’s oil-refining and export revenue and its military-industrial base. The high civilian death toll in a strike aimed at industrial targets is notable and is likely to feature in Russian messaging. Climate · Extreme Weather

Western Europe just logged its hottest June–July on record — 2.79°C above average — as Copernicus flags the heat’s “exceptional persistence”

+

The EU’s Copernicus Climate Change Service reported on Aug. 10 that western Europe experienced its hottest combined June–July period on record, with an average temperature of 21.62°C — about 2.79°C above the seasonal average and surpassing the previous record set in 2022. Drawing on the ERA5 reanalysis model, Copernicus said 33 countries recorded their hottest July since records began, and flagged the “exceptional persistence” of the heat; soil-moisture levels were “significantly lower” than in the July 2022 drought.

Copernicus also noted that July 2026 saw the highest global ocean-surface temperatures for the month, with hot, dry conditions fueling wildfires across France, Greece, the Balkans and the Alpine region, while the UK logged its driest July in nearly two centuries. The caveat: these are observational and reanalysis statistics from an authoritative EU monitor, not a peer-reviewed attribution study — the climate-change framing is Copernicus’s contextual interpretation. Science · Planetary Science

A Martian meteorite from a never-before-sampled region fills a 1.8-billion-year gap in the planet’s geologic record

+

Scientists led by Boston College have dated a Martian meteorite, Northwest Africa (NWA) 13441 — found in Algeria in 2019 — to 1.27 billion years old, and determined it originated from a previously unsampled, pristine region of Mars. The finding, published in the peer-reviewed journal Geochimica et Cosmochimica Acta and announced via EurekAlert around Aug. 10, matters because most Martian meteorites are either younger than about 600 million years or older than about 2.4 billion years, leaving roughly a 1.8-billion-year blind spot in the record of the planet’s magmatic and volcanic activity.

NWA 13441 falls squarely inside that gap, giving researchers a rare window into the composition and evolution of Mars’s interior during a poorly understood middle era. The caveat is built in: it is a single meteorite whose precise ejection site on Mars is unknown — powerful, but one sample. (Peer-reviewed. Science · Quantum Physics

Physicists generate entangled photons from sunlight for the first time — no laser required

+

An international team from the Max Planck Institute for the Science of Light, the Max Planck Center for Extreme and Quantum Photonics and the University of Ottawa reported generating quantum-entangled photon pairs directly from sunlight, published in the peer-reviewed journal Optica (release around Aug. 7). Using spontaneous parametric down-conversion in a nonlinear crystal — a process normally pumped by a laser — the group built an all-glass solar concentrator with a window-sized Fresnel lens that funnels ambient sunlight through a cone-shaped glass coupler into a thin optical fiber.

The outdoor experiment produced entangled photons with roughly 94% fidelity to an ideal entangled state, and the team argues the entanglement quality is comparable to laser-driven approaches once spectral bandwidth is accounted for. The work challenges the assumption that coherent lasers are indispensable, pointing toward lower-energy, more sustainable photonic quantum sources. The caveats: it is a proof-of-concept, the performance is bandwidth-normalized, and photon-pair rates from sunlight remain a practical constraint. (Peer-reviewed. Pages unreachable in-window; from Optica, Max Planck and ScienceDaily summaries.)

Sources: Optica, Max Planck Institute, ScienceDaily, The Quantum Insider
