The Iran War · Blockade

Trump reimposes the Iran blockade and declares a 20% Hormuz “toll” as U.S. strikes enter a third straight night; a primetime address is set for Thursday

+

The war took a sharp new turn Monday. Beyond the fresh exchange of strikes reported this morning, President Trump announced on Truth Social that the United States is reinstating its naval blockade of Iranian ports and will style itself “THE GUARDIAN OF THE HORMUZ STRAIT” — declaring that the waterway “is OPEN, and will remain OPEN, with or without Iran.” The centerpiece is a demand that the U.S. be “reimbursed, at the rate of 20% on all cargo shipped” transiting the strait, plus direct reimbursement from wealthy allies he named — Israel, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Bahrain and Kuwait — arguing “we can’t be expected to do that for nothing.” The blockade is to take effect Tuesday afternoon (reported around 4 p.m. ET).

Militarily, CENTCOM began a third consecutive night of strikes at Trump’s direction; Trump publicly floated Iran’s “Pickaxe Mountain” nuclear site as a “possible target,” vowing to “hit them very hard tonight.” The White House said Trump will deliver a primetime address to the nation Thursday at 9 p.m. ET. Markets extended their slide-and-spike: Brent crude pushed above $83 a barrel by Monday evening, and ship-tracker Kpler reported Hormuz crossings down more than half from the prior week. The toll drew immediate legal pushback — the UN’s International Maritime Organization said there is no legal basis for mandatory tolls, and a U.S. Naval War College maritime-law expert called the world’s right of transit unimpeded. Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, allowed that whoever secures passage “should be compensated” but called 20% “too much,” insisting Iran “will remain the GUARDIAN of the Strait … FOREVER.”

The Left-Leaning Take

Slate framed the toll as “an extortion racket on a global scale,” and MSNBC’s Maddow blog cast the “guardian”/protection-money framing as naked corruption dressed up as strategy — the president turning a war he cannot exit into a revenue scheme while charging allies for their own defense. The broader left reads it as lawlessness (the IMO and maritime-law experts agree there is no legal basis) layered atop an open-ended escalation with no defined endgame.

The Right-Leaning Take

The hawkish right welcomes the harder line — a real blockade and a credible threat to Iran’s nuclear infrastructure is the posture National Review and others had wanted. But the “toll” itself is splitting the coalition: libertarian (Reason-style) and fiscal conservatives balk at a protection-racket premise of dubious legality and feasibility, echoing a former State Department envoy who called 20% “extortionate” and doubted the U.S. can even deliver safe passage. Anti-Trump-right voices go further, calling it lawless improvisation — intra-right friction, not the mainstream-hawk line. (Dedicated in-window NR/Dispatch/WSJ editorial columns on the toll were thin at press time; nothing invented.)

Graham Vacancy · The Pick

McMaster names Graham’s sister, Darline Graham Nordone, to the Senate seat — hours after Trump recommended her

+

The interim question posed this morning was answered Monday, and not as expected. Rather than Rep. Joe Wilson, Gov. Henry McMaster appointed Darline Graham Nordone — the late senator’s sister — to serve out Lindsey Graham’s term, a choice that came hours after President Trump publicly recommended her. Nordone, a commissioner with the South Carolina Commission for the Blind who has never held elected office, will become the state’s first female U.S. senator; she is expected to be sworn in Wednesday and will serve until the new Congress convenes January 3. The mechanics reported this morning still hold — an August 11 special primary, with a one-week candidate filing window opening July 21 — so the appointment settles only the caretaker seat, not the November contest.

The Left-Leaning Take

The left flags the obvious: a president hand-picking the sister of a loyal ally, installed by a compliant governor, is nepotism made plain — and critics note Nordone “has no political record,” asking whether an appointee with no stated positions is prepared to cast votes for five million South Carolinians even temporarily. The dynastic optics, they argue, fit a broader pattern of loyalty-first Trump-era governance.

The Right-Leaning Take

The right treats it as a graceful, low-drama caretaker choice that honors Graham’s memory and, crucially, keeps a fragile Senate majority intact without igniting a premature primary. Sen. Tim Scott quickly endorsed her, saying “there is no one better who understands Lindsey’s love for family, our state, and our country.” Fox framed the move as the GOP moving fast to protect the seat; the real fight, in this telling, is deferred to August.

Ukraine · Politics / War

Zelensky ousts PM Svyrydenko in a wartime reshuffle and asks allies for 300 Patriot interceptors ahead of winter

+

President Volodymyr Zelensky announced a sweeping government reshuffle, and Prime Minister Yulia Svyrydenko stepped down on Sunday just five days short of a full year in office, along with the heads of some law-enforcement agencies. Zelensky said “Ukraine is changing its political strategy,” with each priority foreign-policy track to be run by a dedicated, experienced figure able to deliver on leader-level deals. The shake-up landed as Zelensky worked the “Coalition of the Willing” on Monday, appealing for a winter air-defense package of 300 Patriot interceptor missiles as Russia keeps hammering the grid — nearly 70,000 customers were left without power around Chernihiv, and drones struck Odesa. On the front, Russia is concentrating on the Sloviansk and Pokrovsk axes. The reshuffle is best read as Zelensky personalizing wartime diplomacy — assigning named envoys to Washington, Europe and the battlefield economy — rather than a change of course, even as the loss of his chief Trump-whisperer at the very moment Washington is consumed by Iran carries obvious risk for Kyiv’s access.

The Gulf · Regional Order

+

The most exposed players in the reignited war are the Gulf monarchies. Iran’s weekend retaliation blanketed Qatar, the UAE, Bahrain, Kuwait, Jordan and Oman with missiles and drones — hitting Qatar, a ceasefire mediator that had been spared since April, and the UAE, untouched since May. Trump’s demand that these same governments help “reimburse” the U.S. for securing Hormuz — and his 20% cargo toll, which the IMO says has no legal basis — sharpens their dilemma: they host the American bases drawing Iranian fire, depend on the strait for their oil revenue, yet are being asked to fund a blockade that invites still more retaliation. Analysts note the outward show of Gulf unity masks real divergence over how far to align with Washington, and the episode is reshaping the region’s security order in real time — a strategic bind with no clean exit for Riyadh, Abu Dhabi or Doha. The Iran War · Escalation

U.S. and Iran trade a fresh overnight round of strikes — and dueling accounts of whether the Strait of Hormuz is open

+

The U.S. and Iran exchanged another wave of strikes overnight into Monday, July 13, the latest turn in a tit-for-tat cycle that reignited on July 7 and has now shattered the memorandum of understanding the two governments signed last month to halt hostilities. U.S. Central Command said American forces hit dozens of targets across multiple locations — Iranian air-defense systems, coastal radar sites, and missile and drone capabilities — and said it deployed one-way attack sea drones for the first time in the campaign. Iranian state media said Tehran’s retaliation targeted U.S. military bases in Kuwait, Bahrain, Jordan, Oman and Qatar; Jordan said it intercepted several missiles and Oman summoned Iran’s ambassador. The round follows Saturday night’s larger assault, in which CENTCOM said it struck roughly 140 targets, part of what it now tallies as more than 300 hit across the week.

The central dispute remains the Strait of Hormuz. Iran’s IRGC and its strait authority say passage is “not possible” and declared the waterway closed after firing a warning shot at a vessel; CENTCOM and President Trump insist it “is open to all vessels” and that U.S. forces are escorting traffic to preserve freedom of navigation. Ship-tracking data cited by several outlets showed transits reduced to a trickle over the weekend — a partial corroboration of Iran’s claim even as Washington denies a closure. Trump, who declared the ceasefire “over,” has floated reimposing a naval blockade while saying talks would continue. Markets moved on the risk: outlets put Monday Brent crude near $78 a barrel (roughly +2.8%), with WTI around $73; treat the precise figures, which varied by outlet and hour, as unsettled.

The broader antiwar left stresses that the conflict is unpopular and that the administration has offered no coherent strategy beyond escalation. Congress · The SAVE Act Standoff

Trump lets a landmark bipartisan housing bill become law without his signature, “in protest” of the Senate’s failure to pass his voter-ID measure

+

President Trump refused to sign the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act — described as the largest housing-affordability bill in decades, which passed the House 358–32 and the Senate 85–5 — letting it become law automatically over the weekend without his signature. Trump withheld his signature “IN PROTEST” (his words, on Truth Social) over the Senate’s inability to pass the SAVE America Act, which would require documentary proof of citizenship to register and photo ID to vote. He dismissed the housing bill as a “big yawn” and “so unimportant compared to the SAVE America Act.”

The SAVE Act faces steep obstacles: the Senate parliamentarian ruled it does not qualify for budget reconciliation, and with Democrats opposed and Republicans unwilling to end the filibuster, GOP leaders say they lack the votes. Speaker Mike Johnson has said the House will try to move it through an “arduous process.” Because the housing bill became law regardless, the gesture is being widely characterized as symbolic.

The Left-Leaning Take

The New Republic (“Trump Has Pointless Temper Tantrum Over Housing Bill”) and MSNBC’s opinion desk cast the move as a self-destructive tantrum that forfeits a genuine bipartisan win to prop up what they call a “repressive anti-voting bill.” The framing: Trump sabotaged a popular, broadly supported law over an unrelated voter-suppression measure that has no path to passage anyway.

The Right-Leaning Take

Notably, the sharpest right-leaning critique comes from the Wall Street Journal editorial board, which mocked the maneuver as an incoherent legislative “mish-mosh” and noted the election bill “has no chance of passing either way” — so the protest achieves nothing while costing a win. That is intra-right friction: the institutionalist WSJ page breaking with Trump’s tactics, not defending them. Fox News framed the move more neutrally as an “unusual step.”

Pacific · China / Defense

New Zealand opens talks on joining the Australia–Fiji “Ocean of Peace” pact as Pacific states close ranks after China’s nuclear-capable missile test

+

The fallout from China’s July 6 missile test continues to reshape regional security. Beijing test-fired a nuclear-capable ballistic missile — identified by Taiwan as a submarine-launched JL-2 — into the South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone, landing near Tuvalu and Nauru only hours after Australia and Fiji signed a mutual-defense treaty that commits each to “act to meet the common danger” if the other is attacked. Fiji thereby became just the fourth country to hold a treaty alliance with Australia, after the U.S., New Zealand and Papua New Guinea; the pact, dubbed the “Ocean of Peace” alliance and described by some analysts as a nascent “Pacific NATO,” is to be backed by roughly A$1 billion over a decade and is open to other Pacific nations.

The new development Monday came from Wellington. Foreign Minister Winston Peters — freshly returned from Singapore and Japan, where he raised the launch — confirmed New Zealand is in exploratory talks about joining, while stressing any decision must go to Cabinet. He was pointed on Beijing’s likely objections: China might call joining “an act of provocation,” but “we’re not going to change our view of the… front yard in which we live,” adding that normalizing such missile tests “as regular activity is not acceptable.” Australia has welcomed the prospect, and Pacific leaders including Tonga have joined the condemnation — signals that China’s show of force may be accelerating, rather than deterring, the alignment it sought to discourage.

Sources: RNZ, CNBC, Radio Free Asia, Newsweek

Energy · Storage

BYD lands an 11.3 GWh battery contract for the world’s largest solar-plus-storage plant, locking Chinese firms into the flagship project

+

BYD Energy Storage has been awarded a contract to supply 11.275 GWh of its battery systems to Masdar’s round-the-clock (RTC) renewable project in Abu Dhabi, developed with the Emirates Water and Electricity Company. Space · Planetary Science

Most of the Moon’s water is likely locked chemically inside its deep interior, a new analysis concludes

+

Beyond the ice trapped in permanently shadowed polar craters, the bulk of the Moon’s water is probably bound up chemically within minerals in its deep interior, according to work highlighted around July 13. Drawing on decades of lunar-sample analysis — apatite, volcanic glasses, melt inclusions and “nominally anhydrous minerals” such as plagioclase — planetary scientists conclude the Moon is far wetter internally than the long-held “bone-dry” picture that prevailed until LCROSS detected polar water ice in 2009.

This water is not free-flowing but locked into rock, delivered and retained since the era of the lunar magma ocean. Estimates of the original magma-ocean water content still span a wide range (from roughly 10 ppm up to 100–1,000 ppm), but by one calculation, if all the interior water were extracted it would cover the entire lunar surface in an ocean about a yard deep — with implications for how the Moon formed and for future in-situ resource use. Climate · Forests

Oak trees keep pulling in carbon long after they stop growing, hinting climate models overestimate forest carbon storage

+

A new study finds that oak trees continue photosynthesizing and absorbing CO2 well into late summer even after their annual wood growth has shut down — roughly 36% of a year’s carbon assimilation happens after growth has stopped. Analyzing oaks at 137 sites across the eastern U.S. and California to build daily records of photosynthesis, carbon uptake and wood production, the researchers found the two processes are “decoupled”: more photosynthesis does not automatically translate into more wood.

That challenges a core assumption baked into many climate models — that rising carbon uptake means more carbon locked away in tree biomass. If the extra carbon isn’t being converted into durable wood, forests may store less carbon than projected as CO2 levels climb, weakening a key assumed natural brake on warming. The work was published in Science Advances. (Published about July 8, just outside the 24-hour window, but a genuine on-remit finding.)
