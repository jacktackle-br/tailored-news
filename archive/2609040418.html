Redistricting Wars · Courts Punt to Voters

Two state supreme courts hand Trump’s gerrymander push a double setback, sending GOP-drawn maps to a referendum

+

On Thursday, Sept. 3, the Missouri Supreme Court unanimously blocked the Republican-drawn congressional map — redrawn last year at President Trump’s urging to turn the state’s 6–2 GOP delegation into a likely 7–1 by dismantling Rep. Emanuel Cleaver’s Kansas City-area district — from being used in the Nov. 3 general election, and ordered a statewide referendum on it. The court held that the 2022 map remains in effect and the new districts cannot be used unless voters affirmatively approve them; organizers had submitted more than 300,000 signatures after Secretary of State Denny Hoskins (R) refused to certify the petition. Attorney General Catherine Hanaway said the state will appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court.

The same day, Maryland’s top court cleared a Democratic-backed constitutional amendment for the November ballot, reversing two lower-court rulings while ordering the ballot summary rewritten. If approved, it would let Democratic lawmakers redraw Maryland’s map for 2028, potentially eliminating the state’s lone GOP-held seat. The twin rulings mark a fresh turn in a mid-decade map fight both parties are now waging in courts and at the ballot box: Republicans have enacted new maps in states including Texas, Florida, Missouri, Ohio, North Carolina and Tennessee, with Democrats countering in California and elsewhere. (Per NBC News, UPI and Missouri Independent reporting; the Missouri appeal is pending.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads it as a democracy win: grassroots voters with 305,000-plus signatures beat back a Trump-directed gerrymander and a GOP secretary of state who tried to block the petition at every turn. The framing is that Trump ordered red states to rig maps mid-decade to hold the House without facing voters, and that citizen referenda and courts are the last line of defense — with Maryland showing Democrats finally willing to answer in kind. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Center-right opinion is split and wary. The WSJ editorial board has warned the GOP’s mid-decade map free-for-all — launched at Trump’s insistence — “could backfire in a big way,” calling it a legal hash the party leapt into without looking; National Review-aligned writers defend the maps as a lawful partisan response to Democratic gerrymanders. The anti-Trump right casts the pressure campaign on state legislatures as overreach that invites a mutually destructive arms race. (The anti-Trump reading is intra-right disagreement, not the institutional-right position; directional.)

Sources: NBC News (Missouri), NBC News (Maryland), UPI, Missouri Independent
Economy · Jobs Day Before the Midterms

The August jobs report lands as an economic reality check, with forecasts pointing to another weak month and unemployment ticking up

+

The Bureau of Labor Statistics released its August employment report at 8:30 a.m. ET Friday, Sept. 4 — the last major jobs read before the Nov. 3 midterms, and one Bloomberg billed as an “economic reality check.” Consensus pointed to another soft month: Dow Jones-surveyed economists projected roughly +53,000 payrolls (forecasts ranged from about +20,000 at Citi to +65,000), with the unemployment rate expected to hold at 4.1% or tick to 4.2%. Private-sector ADP data had already flagged softness, showing +38,000 in August, down from +46,000 in July. The report follows a genuinely weak summer: July payrolls unexpectedly fell 23,000, and prior months were revised down by a combined ~103,000.

Analysts attribute the “low-hire, low-fire” market to slower wage growth, tariff uncertainty, and an immigration crackdown — including TPS terminations — that has shrunk the labor pool, with labor-force participation down about 1.6 million since Trump’s term began. On rates, Fed Governor Christopher Waller signaled he would be “inclined” to hold at the current 3.5%–3.75% range, with officials framing inflation as the bigger risk. (This brief was written before the BLS print was corroborated across Tier-1 desks; one figure circulating in search matched the 2025 report and appears stale, so no headline number is asserted here — the framing is expectations, not the final print.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left argues the weak hiring is no mystery but a policy choice: mass deportations and TPS revocations are strangling labor supply while tariffs raise costs and freeze business hiring. Trump inherited a growing economy and stalled it, they say, and voters heading into November are feeling it in gas prices and a frozen job market. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Free-market and center-right voices — the WSJ editorial board, Reason-style libertarians — pin the drag on Trump’s own tariffs and immigration restrictions as self-inflicted wounds to growth and labor supply that can’t be papered over by public-sector and health-care job gains. The administration’s counter, echoed by pro-Trump outlets, is to blame the Fed for not cutting and to call the market “low-fire” rather than in crisis. (Directional; no clean in-window defense of the numbers themselves emerged.)

Sources: CNBC, NBC News, Bloomberg, Washington Post (opinion)
Courts · Birthright Citizenship

A federal judge blocks Trump’s latest birthright-citizenship order as ‘almost certainly unconstitutional’

+

U.S. District Judge Deborah Boardman (D. Md.) issued a preliminary injunction on Sept. 2 blocking Trump’s August 2026 executive order narrowing birthright citizenship — the first judicial ruling against the new order, in CASA, Inc. v. Trump. The order would have denied automatic citizenship to U.S.-born children whose parents fall into carve-outs such as “alien enemy,” foreign-government employee, or “birth tourism.” In a 35-page opinion, Boardman wrote the order “is almost certainly unconstitutional as applied to the certified class for the simple reason that the Supreme Court in Barbara already decided that the children in the class are citizens at birth,” adding: “No Presidential Executive Order can undo what the Supreme Court has done.”

The ruling is the sequel to Trump v. Barbara (June 30, 2026), the 5–4 decision that struck down Trump’s original Day One order. Boardman certified a nationwide class of affected children and barred enforcement against them, while letting agencies keep drafting implementation guidance. Reporting agreement is broad across WaPo, NBC, CNN and CBS; SCOTUSblog and Reason’s Volokh Conspiracy note this is likely the first of several rulings, with the administration expected to appeal and tee up another Supreme Court fight. (Legal desks weighted heavily; the injunction is preliminary.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left views Boardman’s injunction as a necessary but fragile guardrail. The argument is that Trump is deliberately re-litigating settled constitutional ground with cosmetically “narrower” orders to wear down the courts and normalize stripping citizenship from U.S.-born children — and that a one-vote Supreme Court majority makes the 14th Amendment’s guarantee perilously contingent. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The libertarian and legal right largely concedes the constitutional point: Reason’s Volokh Conspiracy treats the injunction as the predictable first ruling against an order that runs straight into precedent, and National Review argues the durable fix for “birth tourism” is legislative, not executive. The through-line is impatience with orders that generate courtroom losses rather than durable policy; little in-window enthusiasm for the order’s legal theory surfaced. (Directional.)

Sources: Washington Post, NBC News, SCOTUSblog, Reason (Volokh)

Europe · Ukraine Diplomacy

Belgium digs in as four EU states try to force a frozen-Russian-asset ‘reparations loan’ back onto the table

+

The push to tap Russia’s roughly €210 billion in immobilized assets — most held at the Brussels clearing house Euroclear — is back in play. The Netherlands, Poland, Spain and Sweden circulated a letter urging Brussels to reopen the debate, and EU foreign ministers took an initial pass at an informal meeting in Ireland on Sept. 1–2. The four argue the €90 billion loan EU leaders agreed last December for 2026–27 falls short, with Kyiv facing a defense-funding gap put at €23.1 billion. On Sept. 3, Belgium’s foreign minister reaffirmed that its opposition is unchanged, warning Euroclear could be left alone facing Russian lawsuits and reciprocal seizures; Brussels points Kyiv toward next year’s European loan instead. The Commission had floated a reparations loan of up to €165 billion, repayable only if Russia ever pays war reparations — the plan that collapsed in December over Belgian objections.

The wrangling runs against a backdrop of intensified strikes: Ukrainian officials said Russia attacked overnight Sept. 3–4 with an X-31P missile and 121 drones, roughly half of them jet-powered Shaheds, part of a week-plus of near-nightly barrages on Kyiv. Authorities reported at least 6 killed and 56 injured nationwide over 24 hours, with 13 hurt in the capital. (Casualty figures per Ukrainian sources and still being revised.)

Nepal · Disaster

A Himalayan glacial collapse leaves more than 1,250 dead and thousands missing as the toll keeps climbing

+

The death toll from the Aug. 26 disaster on the China–Nepal border continued to rise through Sept. 3–4. A massive rock-and-ice avalanche — geologists estimate up to ~200 million cubic meters of debris, possibly the largest rock-ice event on record — triggered flash floods and debris flows that destroyed the Gyirong border checkpoint and struck settlements along a 72 km stretch of the Trishuli River. Latest counts put Nepal’s dead at roughly 1,259 with about 5,083 missing, plus 21 dead and 541 missing across the border in Tibet; nearly 12,000 people have been rescued. Nepal’s foreign ministry reported 590 foreign nationals from 39 countries among the missing, and some victims’ bodies were carried as far as 240 km into India.

Outlet figures diverge by date as the toll moves: earlier tallies from UPI (Sept. 2) cited 1,114 dead and 3,916 missing, while NBC had reported the dead topping 900 with the missing count nearly doubling — the most recent counts now exceed 1,250 dead. Chinese authorities also flagged a newly formed glacial lake at high risk of a further breach. (Figures per Nepali and Chinese authorities and the UN; still being revised.)

Sources: CNN, UN News, Al Jazeera
Israel/Gaza · Politics

Ben Gvir unveils a ‘Disengagement 710’ platform to push 1.86 million Palestinians out of Gaza

+

On Sept. 3, far-right National Security Minister Itamar Ben Gvir unveiled a party platform he calls “Disengagement 710,” aimed at removing the vast majority of Gaza’s Palestinian population through what he terms “voluntary emigration.” The staged plan sets targets of 250,000 leaving in the first year, 1.11 million within three years and 1.86 million within seven, and would create a dedicated government “Emigration Ministry” — which his Otzma Yehudit party frames as a top demand for joining any next coalition. The party promises emigrants an “absorption package” of travel, initial housing, vocational training and up to 24 months of support, plus payments to host countries per Gazan taken in.

The rollout comes as Israel heads toward an Oct. 27 election, so coverage frames it as a campaign platform rather than government policy; rights advocates and Palestinians characterize forced-displacement schemes as ethnic cleansing under international law. Israeli media the same day also noted separate US-mediated Israel–Lebanon developments, including Israel’s handover of Lebanese detainees. (Per Israeli outlets; the plan is a party platform, not enacted policy.)

Technology · Autonomy

Tesla launches the Cybercab robotaxi in Austin — a two-seat car with no steering wheel or pedals

+

Tesla unveiled and began deploying its purpose-built Cybercab robotaxi at an invite-only event in Austin on Sept. 3, folding the two-seat autonomous vehicle into the paid robotaxi service it already runs in parts of Austin, Dallas, Houston and several Florida cities. The Cybercab has no steering wheel or pedals, relies entirely on Tesla’s camera-only “AI4” self-driving computer (nine cameras, no lidar or radar), and features butterfly doors, a low, wheelchair-friendly seat height and braille door handles.

The rollout is a product deployment rather than a regulatory milestone: only about 45 Cybercabs are currently authorized to operate in Texas, and the event was not tied to any NHTSA approval. Tesla also opened a public interest form for companies wanting to buy Cybercab fleets. The camera-only approach continues to distinguish Tesla from market leader Waymo, which uses a multi-sensor lidar-plus-radar-plus-camera stack. (Per launch coverage; independent safety and performance data to follow.)

Sources: Forbes, Motor1, Yahoo Finance
Energy & Climate · Grid

PG&E widens its vehicle-to-grid program, stacking up to ~$17,000 in incentives to turn an EV into a home battery

+

California utility PG&E significantly expanded its Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) program this week, adding bidirectional-charging-capable models — the Nissan Leaf (2018–2025), Volvo EX90, Kia EV9 and EV6, and three GM vehicles including the 2027 Chevrolet Bolt and two Cadillacs — paired with DCbel and Wallbox Quasar 2 chargers. V2X lets a compatible EV act as a mobile battery, powering a home during an outage or exporting to the grid at peak demand.

Residential customers can receive $2,500 upfront ($3,000 in disadvantaged communities), a $1,500 Early Adopter Incentive for the first 250 enrollees, and up to $13,000 more through a California Energy Commission grant — stacking to roughly $17,000, with enrollment open until June 30, 2027. The move is part of a broader push to use EV fleets as distributed grid storage as California manages solar-heavy midday surpluses and evening peaks. (Program details per PG&E and trade coverage.)

Science · Planetary Science

A giant 10-sided ‘decagon’ is spotted around Saturn’s south pole — the first regular polar jet pattern seen in its southern hemisphere

+

Astronomers have confirmed a ten-sided polygonal wave encircling Saturn’s south pole, described in a study published in Science Advances and widely reported Sept. 3. It is the southern-hemisphere counterpart to Saturn’s long-famous north-polar hexagon, and the first time a large, regular-sided jet-stream pattern has been documented in the planet’s south. Hubble caught faint hints of the structure in 2023; it sharpened into a fully defined decagon in images taken in August–September 2025.

Notably, NASA’s Cassini spacecraft, which orbited Saturn until 2017, saw no sign of such a long-lived southern formation, and the south pole was tilted away from Earth between roughly 2012 and 2023 — so the feature is either new or newly observable. The wave appears thick enough to span multiple atmospheric layers, suggesting it is more than a cloud-top pattern, and takes about 800 days to complete one rotation. (Peer-reviewed; the wave’s origin remains under study.)

Sources: NASA Science / Hubble, ScienceDaily, Sci.News
Science · Biomedicine

The FDA approves Ionis’s Zanvastro, the first disease-modifying therapy for fatal Alexander disease

+

The FDA on Sept. 3 approved Zanvastro, from Ionis Pharmaceuticals, the first drug to treat Alexander disease — a rare and fatal neurological disorder — in both children and adults. It is a disease-modifying therapy, the first approved for the condition, rather than a treatment aimed only at symptoms.

Approval rested on a pivotal trial in which walking speed, a standard measure of motor function in neurologic disease, remained stable in treated patients while those in the control group saw a 33% decline. Alexander disease is caused by mutations affecting the GFAP protein in the brain’s astrocytes and has had no targeted therapy until now. (Per STAT News; single-source at the time of writing.)

Sources: STAT News
