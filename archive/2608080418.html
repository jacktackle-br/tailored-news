Justice Department · Confirmed

The Senate confirms Todd Blanche as attorney general, 50–49, in a 4:31 a.m. vote — Cassidy the decisive “yes,” Collins and Murkowski the only GOP defectors

+

The weeks-long standoff ended overnight. In a session that ran past 4 a.m. before the Senate’s recess, the chamber confirmed Todd Blanche — President Trump’s former personal criminal-defense lawyer, who had been running the Justice Department as acting attorney general since April — as attorney general by 50–49, gaveled down at 4:31 a.m. Saturday, Aug. 8. The vote was largely party-line: Sens. Susan Collins (R-Maine) and Lisa Murkowski (R-Alaska) were the only two Republicans to join every Democrat in opposition. Collins cited a Justice Department growing “increasingly political”; Murkowski said she was not convinced Blanche would “check the worst impulses of this administration.” Blanche becomes the second Senate-confirmed attorney general of Trump’s second term, succeeding the fired Pam Bondi.

The nomination had nearly collapsed over a roughly $1.8 billion DOJ “anti-weaponization fund” tied to the settlement of Trump’s suit against the IRS — a deal critics said would also shield Trump and his family from future audits. GOP holdouts John Cornyn (Texas) and Thom Tillis (N.C.) had demanded a formal written rescission, which Blanche executed on Aug. 3; Sen. Bill Cassidy (R-La.) supplied the decisive vote Friday, calling Blanche imperfect but the best option the White House would offer. Judiciary ranking Democrat Dick Durbin called the confirmation “a serious mistake,” and analysts note the rescission’s durable legal effect — and the fate of the audit-immunity language — remains unresolved.

The Left-Leaning Take

Confirming Trump’s own lawyer institutionalizes a weaponized DOJ, the left argues. Slate has pressed the abortion angle, reporting Blanche told anti-abortion groups he wanted to make Dobbs “permanent in every single state,” would use DOJ to curb telehealth abortion, and appeared to embrace a Comstock Act reading that functions as a de facto national ban. The broader charge: a nominee who sees himself as the president’s attorney, not the public’s. (Directional synthesis leaning on Slate; Mother Jones/Vox/Guardian opinion were not cleanly reachable in-window.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The right was strikingly divided rather than triumphant. National Review’s Bench Memos treated the “anti-weaponization” fund as a genuine separation-of-powers and rule-of-law problem, not a cause for celebration, and Reason’s Volokh Conspiracy argued flatly that Blanche “should not be the attorney general” even as the votes lined up. The affirmative case rested largely on Trump-aligned senators and House Judiciary Republicans. Intra-right dissent (anti-Trump right, Bulwark): confirming him is “a gift to Senate Democrats,” itemizing conflicts of interest and DOJ purges — labeled anti-Trump right, not the mainstream position.

Sources: NPR, NBC News, CNN, Axios, CNBC, Slate, National Review, Reason (Volokh), The Bulwark
Congress · Russia & Iran Sanctions

The Senate passes the “Lindsey O. Graham” Russia and Iran sanctions bill, 86–11 — sending secondary sanctions and up-to-100% tariff powers to a House still in recess

+

On Friday, Aug. 7, the Senate passed the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 by 86–11. The bill layers primary and secondary sanctions on Russia — targeting its oil “shadow fleet,” officials, oligarchs and financial institutions — and authorizes the president to impose tariffs of up to 100% on the largest buyers of Russian oil and gas, a lever aimed squarely at China and India. It also extends and strengthens Iran sanctions. The measure is named for the late Sen. Lindsey Graham (R-S.C.), who died unexpectedly on July 11 after finalizing the White House deal that enabled passage; Sen. Richard Blumenthal (D-Conn.) was the lead cosponsor.

The 11 “no” votes crossed ideological lines: Sen. Rand Paul (R-Ky.) was the lone Republican opposed, arguing in a Fox News op-ed that the tariffs would “make American families poorer,” joined by Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vt.) and progressives including Elizabeth Warren and Mazie Hirono, who objected that the bill hands Trump sweeping unilateral tariff authority. It now goes to the House, which is in recess until September. Trump has signaled support but wants the Iran component structured as tariffs rather than sanctions — “that’s what Lindsey wanted” — a demand that could complicate final passage even with Speaker Mike Johnson on board.

The Left-Leaning Take

The left’s objection surfaced inside the vote rather than in located commentary: Sanders, Warren, Hirono and other progressives backed sanctioning Russia but balked at the breadth of tariff and sanctions power the bill delegates to Trump, warning it effectively ratifies open-ended executive economic authority. (No in-window Mother Jones/Vox/Guardian/Slate piece on the bill was reachable here.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The sharpest right-side critique is libertarian and populist: Reason frames the bill as the last gasp of neoconservative interventionism, and Rand Paul’s tariffs-are-a-tax argument channels the MAGA-adjacent economic populism that increasingly resists foreign-policy hawkishness. The Daily Caller pointedly branded it a “neocon-backed” measure. The lopsided 86–11 tally, though, shows the hawkish establishment right — and most Democrats — still firmly behind pressuring Moscow and Tehran.

Sources: NBC News, CBS News, CNBC, NPR, The Hill, Reason, Daily Caller

Middle East · The Iran War Widens

The Iran war spills across the Gulf — Houthi and Iraqi-militia salvos pound Saudi and Yemeni targets as Riyadh braces for “coordinated attacks,” days after the Mecca defense pact

+

The conflict widened from a U.S.–Iran/Israel–Iran axis into a Gulf-wide proxy fight on Friday, Aug. 7. Iran-backed Houthis launched at least a dozen fresh missile attacks; drone and ballistic strikes on Saudi-led coalition and Yemeni government sites killed dozens of soldiers, and shelling in Marib and Hadramout provinces killed civilians and wounded more. A Saudi official said Riyadh was braced for “multiple coordinated attacks” by the Houthis and Iraqi militias, potentially against civilian and energy infrastructure. One de-escalation wrinkle: the Islamic Resistance in Iraq said it would postpone its response to recent U.S.–Saudi strikes on militia positions, citing mediation by Badr Organization chief Hadi al-Ameri.

The timing sharpens the stakes of the trilateral Saudi–Pakistan–Turkey mutual-defense treaty signed in Mecca on Friday, which treats an attack on any signatory as an attack on all: a large strike on Saudi Arabia could now, in principle, draw in nuclear-armed Pakistan and NATO member Turkey. Against that backdrop, reporting indicates Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. Dan Caine has privately urged the White House to find an “off-ramp,” warning that further escalation options could backfire. Europe · Russia’s War on Ukraine

Russian drones kill a 3-year-old boy and his grandparents near Kyiv — as Zelensky makes the first-ever Ukrainian presidential visit to Russia-friendly Serbia and the U.S. abruptly pulls its top Europe corps commander

+

Overnight into Saturday, Aug. 8, a Russian drone-and-missile barrage struck the Brovary district outside Kyiv, killing a 3-year-old boy and his grandparents in the village of Puhivka when their home caught fire; the boy’s parents, teenage brother and a neighbor were wounded. Outlets differ on the toll — three killed and seven injured (Al Jazeera, Kyiv Independent, Irish Times) versus four killed (ABC) — and Ukraine’s air force said it downed 135 of 151 drones launched, meaning roughly 16 got through, underscoring the interceptor-shortage theme that has dominated recent coverage. President Zelensky said Russia also used six ballistic missiles against civilian infrastructure.

On the diplomatic front, Zelensky made the first-ever visit by a Ukrainian president to Serbia on Saturday, meeting President Aleksandar Vučić — a notable outreach, given Belgrade is one of the few European capitals still maintaining friendly ties with Moscow. Separately, the U.S. Army abruptly removed Lt. Gen. Charles Costanza, commander of V Corps, which helps coordinate American support to Ukraine and the NATO eastern flank, roughly two months before his tour was to end. No official reason was given; U.S. outlets report sources framing it as a conduct matter rather than a political firing, though it lands amid a broader shake-up of senior officers under Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. Maj. Gen. Thomas Feltey, already Senate-confirmed, is slated to take the corps.

Persian Gulf · Strait of Hormuz

An Iran–Oman framework to reroute Hormuz shipping awaits Tehran’s “final approval” — but reopening the strait still hinges on the U.S. lifting its naval blockade

+

Iran and Oman have agreed on the coordinates for new commercial shipping lanes through the Strait of Hormuz — the passage for roughly a fifth to a quarter of the world’s seaborne oil — and as of Aug. 8 Iranian lawmakers were awaiting “final approval” of the framework. Under the mechanism, commercial vessels would enter the Gulf via an Iranian-controlled route and exit via an Oman-controlled one, with temporary routes near Iran’s Larak Island closed; the two states are also discussing a joint coordination center to manage traffic and collect vessel data. A deputy foreign minister called it “a new model” for the strait, a break from the arrangements of the past six decades.

The critical condition remains: Iran’s foreign ministry stresses the strait will not automatically reopen — that depends on Washington removing its naval blockade and halting strikes on Iranian infrastructure. A CNN analysis framed the emerging deal as one Trump does not want, because it hands Iran and Oman — not the United States — control over the terms of access, functioning as much as a bargaining demand as a done deal. It sits in uneasy counterpoint to the day’s Gulf escalation: diplomacy on the water, widening proxy war on land.

Sources: Bloomberg, CNN (analysis), Fortune, Washington Times

Technology · Semiconductors

Tesla and SpaceX unveil “Terafab,” a $16.8B chip megafab in Texas to feed Optimus, Cybercab and space-based data centers

+

SpaceX and Tesla jointly announced (Aug. 6, dominating Friday’s coverage) a semiconductor manufacturing complex called Terafab in Grimes County, Texas, north of Houston. The disclosed first phase is a $16.8 billion capital investment split between the two companies, with further expansion phases said to be under consideration. The companies describe a site spanning more than 100 million square feet that would vertically integrate fabrication, packaging and testing of advanced logic and memory chips, employ at least about 3,000 people, and draw a $30 million Texas Enterprise Fund grant.

The chips are earmarked for Tesla’s Optimus humanoid robots and Cybercab, plus SpaceX computing and “space-based data center” systems; the companies claim combined future demand exceeding 1 terawatt of compute, far above current global supply. Caveats: the square-footage, jobs, incentive and demand figures are company-stated projections, not independently verified, and no firm first-production date was given. This is a commitment and a groundbreaking, not an operating fab — and vertical mega-fabs have a long history of slipping timelines.

Sources: TechCrunch, Quartz, Yahoo Finance, MarketScale
Energy · Fuel Cells & Hydrogen

A single silver nanocatalyst switches reaction sites depending on the job — a design rule that could sharpen solid-oxide cells for power and hydrogen

+

A joint Korean team — Seoul National University, KAIST and the Korea Basic Science Institute — reported (Aug. 8) that a single silver nanocatalyst changes which reaction site it uses depending on whether a solid-oxide cell is generating electricity or producing hydrogen. By quantifying oxygen-exchange behavior on solid-oxide-cell electrodes, the researchers propose this switching as a design principle for higher-performance next-generation cells — devices central to efficient hydrogen production and clean power generation. The work is peer-reviewed, published in Energy & Environmental Science.

The caveat is scope: this is a mechanistic, laboratory-scale materials finding, not a deployed technology or an energy-market development. Its value is in the design rule it offers — a clearer picture of how catalysts behave under reversible operation — which could, over time, feed into more durable and efficient fuel cells and electrolyzers.

Sources: EurekAlert (SNU–KAIST–KBSI), Phys.org
Science · Materials Physics

UCLA physicists steer heat like a beam of light through a crystal at room temperature — a trick that could route heat around delicate chips

+

A team led by UCLA’s Yongjie Hu demonstrated “phonon focusing” — heat moving through a crystal in focused, wave-like rays rather than spreading diffusely — at room temperature, a phenomenon previously seen only at cryogenic temperatures. Working with boron arsenide, a semiconductor prized for its very high thermal conductivity, the researchers found the heat-flow patterns change predictably with crystal orientation, with different crystal planes producing distinct sixfold, eightfold and fourfold focusing patterns. The study is peer-reviewed, published in Nature Physics.

The practical promise is thermal management: if engineers can direct heat along planned routes with nanoscale precision — steering it around the most sensitive parts of next-generation processors and quantum devices — rather than letting it spread and then trying to pump it out, it could ease one of the hardest constraints in advanced computing. For now it is a laboratory demonstration in a specialized material, not a manufacturing technique.

Sources: Nature Physics, ScienceDaily
Space · Lunar Debris

A spent SpaceX rocket stage slams into the Moon’s far side — and telescopes catch sodium and lithium glowing in the impact plume

+

A discarded Falcon 9 upper stage — left over from a January 2025 launch that carried Firefly Aerospace’s Blue Ghost 1 lunar lander — made an uncontrolled impact on the Moon’s far side, near Einstein Crater, at about 2:35 a.m. EDT on Wednesday, Aug. 5, striking at roughly 5,400 mph. NASA estimated the collision would carve a fresh crater on the order of 60 feet across and 12 feet deep. The stage was never commanded to maneuver away after its fuel ran out, leaving it to be pulled in over months.

The European Southern Observatory’s Very Large Telescope in Chile detected the strike and confirmed spectral lines of sodium and lithium gas in the impact plume, which lingered for five to ten minutes — and follow-up crater imaging from lunar orbiters began circulating this week. The event, unintentional but entirely predictable, sharpened a running debate about human-made debris on the lunar surface, now estimated to top 200 tons.

Sources: NPR, CNN, TIME, Forbes
