The Shutdown · The Data Goes Dark

No jobs report, and firings in limbo: on day three the shutdown starts erasing the numbers Washington runs on

+

The first hard economic casualty of the lapse arrived this evening: the Bureau of Labor Statistics did not release the monthly jobs report scheduled for today, the marquee reading on hiring and unemployment, after the shutdown furloughed nearly all of its staff and froze its surveys. The blackout leaves the Federal Reserve — weighing another rate cut this month — and financial markets partly flying blind, and it is likely only the first of a cascade of delayed releases (inflation and GDP figures follow) if the impasse drags on. Critics noted the report was effectively finished before funding lapsed, casting the non-release as a choice as much as a consequence.

That landed atop the fight already consuming the capital. Budget director Russell Vought has told House Republicans that permanent reduction-in-force notices would hit targeted agencies “within one to two days,” aimed at programs “not consistent with the President’s priorities” — even as a federal judge’s order temporarily pausing earlier RIFs remains in force and lawyers warn the firings run into the Antideficiency Act. The Senate, out until next week, left no vote scheduled; Majority Leader John Thune is still hunting five more Democrats, while Minority Leader Chuck Schumer called the layoff drive “deliberate chaos” the White House chose rather than was forced into. (Fast-moving; the RIF order is temporary and the data schedule depends on when the government reopens.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left (Vox, Slate, Mother Jones, Guardian opinion) reads the day as sabotage dressed as consequence: an administration that could publish a finished jobs report and decline to fire anyone is instead darkening the data and threatening mass layoffs to inflict pain and pin it on Democrats. Withholding the numbers, they argue, conveniently blinds everyone to an economy the White House would rather not show — and a judge has already called the firings likely illegal. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right (WSJ editorial, National Review, The Dispatch) keeps the frame on cause: Democrats are filibustering a clean stopgap over an unrelated health-subsidy demand, so the missing report and furlough pain are their doing — reopen first, debate the rest after. Libertarians (Reason) call shutdowns a terrible budgeting tool for every side and are skeptical the executive can lawfully fire workers off a funding lapse. Anti-Trump conservatives at The Bulwark warn that weaponizing statistics and the civil service sets a precedent any future president will abuse — an intra-right dissent, not the party line. (Directional.)

Sources: The Hill, CEPR, NBC News, PBS NewsHour, Lawfare
Health Care · The Core of the Impasse

No talks until you reopen: with the ACA subsidy cliff weeks away, both sides dig in and millions face a premium shock

+

Beneath the day’s theatrics, the substance of the standoff hardened. President Trump and Republican leaders are holding to a single precondition — reopen the government first, then negotiate — while Democrats insist any stopgap must extend the enhanced Affordable Care Act premium tax credits that expire at year’s end. The clock is unforgiving: open enrollment begins November 1, and insurers have already set 2026 rates assuming the subsidies lapse. Schumer warns some households could see costs jump by hundreds of dollars a month — by one estimate as much as $400 — if nothing changes.

Republicans have floated alternatives rather than a straight extension, including redirecting the money to individuals through health savings accounts or direct payments, and Trump has framed his objection partly as refusing to subsidize coverage for undocumented immigrants — a characterization Democrats dispute. For now neither chamber has a deal, high-level negotiations have barely begun, and each side is betting the other blinks first as the enrollment deadline closes in. (The subsidy extension would be settled by the reopening legislation; premium estimates vary by household and plan.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left argues Democrats are fighting over something concrete and imminent — a double-digit premium spike hitting working families just as enrollment opens — and that “reopen, then we’ll see” is a promise to do nothing. Republicans control the government, they say, so a manufactured health-cost crisis is theirs to own. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right (WSJ editorial, National Review) counters that the enhanced credits were a temporary pandemic-era expansion that ballooned in cost, and that the minority is holding basic government funding hostage to make them permanent. Reformers prefer HSAs or targeted help over open-ended subsidies to insurers; libertarians (Reason) see both parties papering over a health system neither will actually fix. (Directional.)

Sources: NOTUS, The Hill, The Hill (HSA plan), PBS NewsHour

‘Stop the bombing’: Trump orders Israel to halt strikes after Hamas’s partial yes, and the deal moves to the fine print in Cairo

Ukraine · Russia’s Winter Campaign

+

Russia pressed an intensifying assault on the Ukrainian capital today, striking one of Kyiv’s key bridges and damaging the power lines that feed the city’s transit, injuring at least two people in the latest of a run of attacks on civilian infrastructure. The exchange underscores how the war has settled into a contest of deep strikes on each other’s energy and logistics, even as unexplained drone incursions over several European airports and borders keep NATO capitals on edge about where the conflict’s edges now lie.

Europe · France

A ‘Save France’ budget on a knife’s edge: Lecornu stakes his young government on €54 billion in cuts

+

France’s fifth prime minister in under two years moved from survival to a reckoning. Having only just assembled a government, Sébastien Lecornu has put forward a draft 2026 budget built on roughly €54 billion in spending cuts and new revenue, aiming to pull the deficit from about 5.4% of GDP toward 5% next year as national debt climbs past a record 119% of output. The plan reaches into politically radioactive territory — health spending, pensions and family benefits — the kind of austerity that has already toppled two of his predecessors.

The arithmetic in the National Assembly is as brutal as the fiscal arithmetic. With no majority and both Marine Le Pen’s National Rally and the left-wing France Unbowed threatening censure, Lecornu’s government could fall the moment it tries to force the budget through, potentially via the constitution’s contested Article 49.3. The standoff is a test not just of one premier but of whether the euro zone’s second-largest economy can still pass a budget at all — a question markets and Brussels are watching closely. (The budget faces weeks of parliamentary battle; no-confidence threats were not yet scheduled votes at publication.)

The Shutdown · Day Three

Into the weekend, still deadlocked: the Senate fails again, and the fight turns to whether workers will ever be paid

+

The shutdown rolled into its first weekend with no exit in sight. On its third day the Senate again failed to advance either path out: the House-passed Republican stopgap (H.R. 5371) fell 54–44, and a competing Democratic measure (S. 2882) failed 46–52 — both well short of the 60 votes needed to break a filibuster. Only Democrats Catherine Cortez Masto and John Fetterman crossed to back the GOP bill, while Republican Rand Paul and independent Angus King voted the other way. Majority Leader John Thune said he still believes he can peel off the five more Democrats he needs; Minority Leader Chuck Schumer’s caucus is holding out for a stopgap that extends the expiring Affordable Care Act subsidies, the core of the impasse.

A new flashpoint opened over money owed. The administration signalled that furloughed federal employees may not automatically be entitled to back pay once the lapse ends — the budget office’s shutdown guidance dropped citations of the 2019 Federal Employee Fair Treatment Act, arguing Congress must affirmatively authorize the payments in any reopening deal. The threat landed on a workforce already rattled: budget director Russell Vought had declared “the RIFs have begun,” and permanent reduction-in-force notices went out before U.S. District Judge Susan Illston granted a temporary restraining order pausing them as “likely” illegal. Roughly 750,000 workers are furloughed, with many more working unpaid; even some Republicans, among them Sen. Thom Tillis, called the no-back-pay posture “bad strategy.” (Fast-moving; the back-pay question is contested and would be settled by the reopening legislation, and the RIF order is temporary.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads the back-pay gambit as an escalation of the same coercion: first permanent firings during a funding lapse, now a threat to stiff workers on wages they are owed, all to break the minority over health subsidies for millions. A judge already halted the firings as lawless, they argue; Democrats should hold the line rather than reward hostage-taking of the civil service. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right says the math is simple: the House passed a clean stopgap and Democrats keep voting to keep the government shut over an unrelated demand. The WSJ editorial line is to reopen first and debate the ACA on its own. Libertarians (Reason) call shutdowns a poor tool for anyone. Anti-Trump conservatives at The Bulwark warn that dangling back pay to punish civil servants is norm-breaking a future president will abuse — an intra-right dissent, not the party line. (Directional.)

Courts · Troops in American Cities

To the Supreme Court: after losing in Chicago, the administration asks the justices to clear the way for the National Guard

+

The fight over soldiers on American streets reached the nation’s highest court. After a federal appeals panel refused to lift a district judge’s order blocking deployment — writing that “political opposition is not rebellion” and finding “insufficient evidence of a rebellion or danger of rebellion in Illinois” — the administration filed an emergency application at the Supreme Court seeking to immediately send National Guard troops into the Chicago area. It is the first time the justices have been drawn into the president’s drive to put troops in U.S. cities over their governors’ objections. The deployments are tied to Operation Midway Blitz, an intensified immigration surge that has drawn large protests outside the Broadview processing center.

The broader legal map stays tangled. In the parallel Portland case, the Ninth Circuit voted to rehear the dispute before an 11-judge en banc panel, keeping troops out of that city for now; roughly 200 federalized Texas Guard members remain barred from the Chicago area. Illinois officials urged the justices to stay out, while Gov. JB Pritzker cast the lower-court rulings as a win for the state. Every case turns on the same unresolved question: whether a president may federalize and deploy a state’s Guard domestically over its governor’s objection, absent an insurrection — and a single order from the Court could set the terms for all of them at once. (Active litigation; the Court had not ruled on the application as of this writing.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a president who lost in the trial and circuit courts now betting that a friendly Supreme Court majority will hand him a domestic army. The lower-court language — no rebellion, no justification — is the whole case, they argue; letting the troops in anyway would bless using the military against cities for their politics. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right argues the president needs the Court to settle a real question: whether he can protect federal personnel and enforce federal law when local officials obstruct it. But federalism-minded and libertarian conservatives (Reason) warn the right against cheering a precedent that would let a future Democratic president federalize Guard units over Republican governors’ objections — a power better left unclaimed. (Directional.)

Europe · Czech Republic

Babiš storms back: populist ANO wins the Czech election, pointing Prague toward a eurosceptic turn

+

Czech voters returned the billionaire populist Andrej Babiš to the center of power. His ANO movement won the parliamentary election with about 34.5% and roughly 80 of 200 seats, well ahead of Prime Minister Petr Fiala’s center-right Spolu alliance on about 23%, which conceded. Technology · The AI Buildout

Chips, gigawatts and circular deals: OpenAI’s spending spree redraws the AI hardware map — and strains the grid

+

The scramble for AI compute reached a new scale. OpenAI struck a deal with AMD to buy enormous quantities of AI chips — roughly 6 gigawatts of capacity over coming years — and secured warrants that could make it owner of up to about 10% of the chipmaker, with the first silicon due to switch on in 2026. It lands atop a Nvidia-backed financing commitment reported in the $100 billion range and a roughly $300 billion cloud pact with Oracle, knitting chipmakers, cloud providers and the model-maker into interlocking, mutually financing arrangements that critics call “circular.”

The binding constraint behind all of it is power. The new data centers are now measured in gigawatts rather than megawatts: one flagship site — built on a former Cold-War uranium-enrichment plant in Ohio — could grow toward 8 GW, with partners pledging to build 10 GW or more of new generation to feed it. That appetite is colliding with grids and ratepayers already feeling the squeeze, and sharpening the question hanging over the whole boom: whether demand for AI will ultimately justify the hundreds of billions being committed to serve it. Energy · The Transition

Records on both sides of the ledger: clean power keeps setting highs — yet 2025’s energy emissions still rose

+

The energy transition is accelerating and falling short at the same time. New analysis finds global energy-related emissions rose in 2025 even as renewables were added at a record clip — because surging electricity demand, from data centers to electrified transport and heat, has been outrunning the pace of clean build-out. The headline numbers for solar, wind and batteries keep breaking records; the total carbon line has not yet turned down.

The build-out is real nonetheless. The United States is on track for a record year of new electric capacity in 2025, roughly 64 GW, dominated by solar (about 33 GW) and battery storage (about 18 GW), with wind trailing. Elsewhere, grids are hitting milestones — Australia’s main market set fresh renewable-share records this year, with rooftop solar at times supplying more than half of demand. The gap between record clean additions and still-rising emissions is the defining tension of the decade’s power story. (Preliminary industry and agency estimates, routinely revised.)

Space · Planetary Defense

A close shave, spotted only afterward: asteroid 2025 TF skimmed past Earth before any telescope saw it

+

A small asteroid made one of the closest approaches to Earth ever recorded — and nobody noticed until it was gone. Asteroid 2025 TF passed just about 410 kilometres (roughly 255 miles) over Antarctica on Oct. 1, lower than the orbit of the International Space Station, before it was discovered some six hours later by the Bok Telescope at Kitt Peak National Observatory in Arizona. Estimated at only a couple of metres across and moving near 21 km/s, it ranks among the three closest known flybys on record.

A rock that size would most likely have broken up harmlessly in the atmosphere, so the story is less about danger than about detection. Objects this small, approaching from certain angles, can slip past the sky surveys entirely until after closest approach — a reminder of the blind spots that planetary-defense programs and new wide-field instruments are racing to close. (Orbit and size are preliminary estimates refined from follow-up observations.)

Biomedicine · Drug Repurposing

Old drugs, new target: a computational hunt points cancer medicines at Alzheimer’s

+

Researchers at the Gladstone Institutes and UCSF have been pursuing an unusual shortcut to an Alzheimer’s treatment: rather than design a new molecule, they mapped how the disease rewires gene expression in the brain and then searched a library of already-approved drugs for ones that push those genetic signatures in the opposite direction. The screen flagged a combination of cancer drugs, which in laboratory models reversed disease-associated changes — and, in a parallel analysis of millions of patient records, was linked to lower rates of Alzheimer’s among people who had taken them.

Drug repurposing is attractive because approved medicines already have safety data, which can shave years off the path to a trial. The usual cautions apply: an association in records and an effect in mice are not the same as a benefit in patients, and Alzheimer’s has a long history of promising leads that faltered in the clinic. But the strategy — letting the disease’s own molecular fingerprint nominate candidates — is a template now being applied well beyond this one condition. (Early-stage and partly observational; prospective trials would be needed to establish any clinical benefit.)

Sources: Gladstone Institutes, FierceBiotech
