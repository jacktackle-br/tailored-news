Congress · War Powers

As the Iran war reignites, Washington’s fight over who authorized it flares again — and oil does the arguing

+

Thursday’s renewed U.S. strikes on Iran (see Global Affairs) pushed the domestic fight over the war’s legality back to the surface. Trump has not sought congressional authorization for the campaign, now nearly five months old, and has asked Congress for roughly $67 billion in supplemental funding to sustain it and replenish munitions and Patriot interceptors. In late-July votes, Congress split along largely symbolic lines: the House adopted a War Powers Resolution 214–208 directing Trump to end hostilities — with Republican Reps. Thomas Massie (Ky.), Brian Fitzpatrick (Pa.), Tom Barrett (Mich.) and Warren Davidson (Ohio) crossing over — while the Senate’s companion measure failed 47–49. Neither would reach Trump’s desk or bind him.

The political pressure is compounded by the economics: Brent crude jumped nearly 8% (settling around $90.74) on the latest escalation, with some reporting oil topping $100 intraday amid the Strait of Hormuz deadlock. Sen. Bill Cassidy (R-La.) had a public shouting match with Trump and demanded — and got — a classified briefing, later flipping a vote he said he’d used as “leverage to get some information.” Notably, both the CIA and the Defense Intelligence Agency have assessed that the current bombing is unlikely to shift Iran’s negotiating posture, sharpening the question of what the campaign is for.

The Left-Leaning Take

The left casts this as an unauthorized, open-ended war the public never debated: a president bombing a country for months without a vote, asking for $67 billion, while intelligence agencies say it won’t even work. Progressive voices amplify the war-powers rebukes as the constitutional line in the sand and point to spiking gas prices as the tangible cost of an escalation with no defined endgame. The recurring frame: Congress is abdicating, and only a handful of members — from both parties — are willing to reclaim the power to declare war.

The Right-Leaning Take

Most congressional Republicans are standing with Trump, framing the strikes as necessary to keep Iran from a nuclear weapon and treating the war-powers votes as partisan theater. But the restraint wing is real and growing: Reason-style libertarians and Rep. Massie argue the Constitution requires Congress to authorize war, full stop. On the anti-Trump right (label: intra-right disagreement, not the mainstream-right position), The Bulwark has gone furthest, blasting a war it describes as “led by idiots and incompetents” — part of a widening rupture in Trump’s own coalition over the Iran campaign.

Europe · Russia’s War on Ukraine

A Russian missile crosses into Poland and NATO scrambles jets, as a mass barrage kills at least 10 across Ukraine

+

Russia launched one of its largest aerial attacks in weeks in the early hours of Thursday, July 30 — dozens of ballistic and cruise missiles and hundreds of drones aimed across Ukraine. The Poland incursion, even if unintentional, is the kind of incident NATO planners have long feared could widen the war, and is likely to intensify pressure over Western air-defense supply.

The Senate Judiciary Committee on Wednesday night, July 29, abruptly postponed the vote — scheduled for Thursday, July 30 — on Todd Blanche’s nomination to be U.S. attorney general, a striking setback with the Senate days from its August recess. Blanche is already serving as acting AG. The trigger was Sen. The dispute centers on Trump’s roughly $10 billion suit against the IRS and Treasury over the leak of his tax returns during his first term. DOJ said in June it was “not moving forward” with the fund amid bipartisan outrage, but officials say the audit-immunity piece remains in place; Cornyn wants Blanche to certify in writing that the fund is dead and cannot be revived. Adding legal weight, a federal judge earlier in July ruled the underlying suit a “bad faith” attempt to manipulate the courts, found the immunity order has “no basis in law,” and barred the administration from citing the settlement in future proceedings.

Technology · Quantum Computing

A silicon quantum chip runs its own error correction — from inside the fridge

+

HRL Laboratories — the Malibu R&D lab jointly owned by Boeing and GM, and now being acquired by IBM — reported in Nature on July 29 an 18-qubit silicon spin quantum processor that operates autonomously, running gate sequences and error detection from a custom cryogenic-CMOS control chip that sits inside the cryostat at 4 kelvin. That replaces the racks of room-temperature control electronics today’s quantum machines depend on. The device integrates a 54-quantum-dot array (configurable for up to 18 exchange-only qubits) fabricated on 200-millimeter isotopically enriched silicon-germanium wafers — the same kind of standard semiconductor line used for conventional chips.

Reported performance included average single-qubit gate errors of about 1.7×10⁻⁴ and two-qubit (CNOT) entangling-gate errors of 3.5×10⁻³, with the best reproducible two-qubit errors near 9×10⁻⁴. Silicon spin qubits have long trailed superconducting and trapped-ion approaches, but their compatibility with existing chip fabs makes them attractive for scaling up, which is the significance Nature’s accompanying coverage emphasized. The result also lands days after IBM moved to acquire HRL — a deal expected to close by the end of the third quarter of 2026 — and amid a broader wave of spin-qubit progress elsewhere in the field.

Science · Archaeology

LiDAR peels back the Amazon canopy to reveal a lost civilization — the “Aquiry” — that may have numbered in the millions

+

A study published July 29 in Nature used airborne LiDAR — laser scanning that sees through forest canopy — across roughly 4,500 square kilometers (about 1,740 square miles) of southwestern Amazonia and revealed 396 previously unknown geoglyphs and earthworks (432 total in the dataset). The builders, a culture the authors call the “Aquiry,” carved geometric ceremonial and political centers into the landscape between about 600 BCE and 850 CE. Researchers estimate the Aquiry territory spanned some 183,000 square kilometers — under 3% of Greater Amazonia — yet may have supported 1.25 to 3 million people at the start of the first millennium.

Extrapolating the density of the finds to the full territory, the team offers a “cautious” estimate of more than 20,000 earthwork ceremonial centers still hidden across the region — recasting the pre-Columbian Amazon not as pristine wilderness but as a heavily engineered, densely populated landscape. One researcher said the work “completely transformed my understanding” of the region. The collaboration included researchers affiliated with the University of Helsinki.

Sources: Science (AAAS), Live Science, ScienceAlert, Phys.org, University of Helsinki
Science · Space

NASA pulls the Roman Space Telescope’s launch forward to August 30 — roughly eight months early

+

At a mission-preview news conference on July 29, NASA confirmed a firm launch target of August 30, 2026, for the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center — roughly eight months ahead of the original “no later than May 2027” schedule. The observatory had just been fueled. Roman is bound for the Sun–Earth Lagrange Point 2, about 1.5 million kilometers out, with roughly 90 days of commissioning before science operations begin in early 2027.

The science case is scale: Roman carries a Hubble-quality mirror but a field of view about 100 times wider, and is expected to discover on the order of 100,000 exoplanets in its primary mission via gravitational microlensing — potentially more than every telescope in history combined — while mapping dark energy and dark matter across large swaths of sky. Outlets differ on whether the acceleration is “eight” or “nine” months; the baseline was a no-later-than-May-2027 commitment.

Sources: NASA Roman blog, SpacePolicyOnline, The Planetary Society, WKNO FM, NASA
