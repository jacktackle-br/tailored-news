Energy Prices · Russia Sanctions

Trump strikes a diesel deal with Putin and Treasury waives sanctions on Russian diesel, weeks before the midterms

+

After a phone call with Vladimir Putin on Friday, President Trump said on Truth Social that Russia would “immediately” supply more than 300,000 tons of diesel to US and world markets. He said 500,000 tons would follow in November and about 1 million tons after that, and up to 3 million more could come later depending on the state of Russia’s refineries. The same day, Treasury issued a general license that temporarily waives sanctions on Russian-origin diesel through April 2027. “Lower prices for Americans, especially our Great Farmers, Ranchers, and Truckers, is my Greatest Priority,” Trump wrote. The Kremlin said Putin told Trump Russia was ready to supply oil to US and world markets. AP and NPR described the move as a sharp reversal of US pressure on Moscow. Diesel prices have surged because of the Iran war, disruption at Hormuz, and Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries, which pushed Moscow to ban diesel exports.

Energy analysts quoted by CNN and the Washington Post said the volumes are small compared with US demand and unlikely to move pump prices much. The White House has not said who pays for the fuel or when it arrives. Democratic Sens. Schumer, Shaheen and Warren called the deal a betrayal of Ukraine and US allies. Sen. Blumenthal said it was “directly contrary to Congress’s intent” in the bipartisan sanctions law Trump signed last month, which directs the administration to target the top importers of Russian oil and gas. President Zelensky called it “a weak decision by strong partners” (see Foreign Affairs).

First, the deal hands Putin sanctions relief and a diplomatic win while US envoys are mediating the war. The Pentagon · Capital Punishment

Vance breaks with Hegseth over livestreaming the Fort Hood shooter’s firing-squad execution as the backlash crosses party lines

+

On Friday, Vice President JD Vance said, “If it’s ultimately live-streamed, I’m not going to be watching it,” and added, “I don’t know that that’s actually going to happen.” Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth had said, “We’ll make sure that people are able to watch it.” Earlier this week the Pentagon said the Dec. 3 firing-squad execution of Nidal Malik Hasan would be livestreamed. Hasan is the former Army psychiatrist who killed 13 people at Fort Hood in 2009. The execution is set for 1 p.m. at Fort Hood, the hour of the attack. Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell said Trump approved the plan. Asked on Friday, Trump said only that “they’re making a determination.” It would be the first US military execution since 1961, and NPR notes it would be the first public US execution since the 1930s.

Republican Sens. Thom Tillis, who called the plan “beyond disgusting,” and John Curtis joined Democrats including Sens. Chris Murphy and Jon Ossoff in objecting. Rep. Nancy Mace backed it. The head of the US Conference of Catholic Bishops called it a “barbaric promotion of a culture of death,” and the UN human rights office said it would be “tantamount to a public execution.” AP reports that Army execution rules do not address broadcasting. Experts said the legal route to block the stream is narrow, though a habeas petition from Hasan could delay the execution itself.

The Left-Leaning Take

Slate argues it is a calculated publicity stunt: Hegseth picked a condemned man almost no one will defend, so that objecting to the spectacle looks like sympathy for a mass murderer. Mother Jones calls it a state-produced “snuff film,” and says that turning a lawful sentence into content debases both the military and the audience.

The Right-Leaning Take

Much of the right objects too. National Review Corner posts call it “barbarism” and say a terrorist’s death should not be made a show or a martyrdom. One argues Army regulations, such as the bar on witnesses under 18, cut against public viewing. The Dispatch’s Boiling Frogs newsletter treats it as postliberalism normalizing ruthlessness. Reason also covered the plan. From the anti-Trump right, The Bulwark’s Bill Kristol calls it spectacle rather than deterrence, meant to turn citizens into voyeurs. (Several pieces undated; exact publication days not verified.)

Russia & Ukraine · Diplomacy

Russian glide bombs kill 12 in Zaporizhzhia a day after Washington eases diesel sanctions, as Zelensky calls the deal “a weak decision by strong partners”

+

On Saturday, Russian glide bombs hit apartment buildings in Zaporizhzhia, killing 12 people, including three children, and wounding at least 14, according to AP. The toll rose from an early count of eight. AP reports that Russian strikes have killed more than 60 people across Ukraine in four days. “Today, Russia expressed its thanks for the lifting of sanctions on diesel exports by dropping five glide bombs,” President Zelensky wrote. He was referring to Friday’s US deal for Russian diesel and the Treasury waiver of sanctions on it through April 2027 (see Politics). He said sanctions relief granted outside a lasting de-escalation agreement is “an obvious weakness.”

The deal came while a senior Ukrainian delegation, including Kyrylo Budanov and Rustem Umerov, was meeting US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner in Miami, with European and NATO officials present. Witkoff called the talks “constructive,” but no agreements were announced, including on a proposed ceasefire covering energy infrastructure. Zelensky expects a briefing on Monday. Kremlin envoy Kirill Dmitriev welcomed the deal and said US-Russian cooperation on energy “will benefit the world.”

Analysis: Washington is easing energy pressure on Moscow for domestic price reasons while its own envoys try to mediate. That undercuts the leverage of Kyiv’s campaign against Russian refineries, and it raises doubts about whether penalties under last month’s sanctions law will be enforced. Outlets disagree on whether Russia can even deliver: the Washington Post and analysts cited by CNN question it, given refinery damage and Moscow’s own diesel-export ban. Foreign Policy’s Adam Tooze puts the deal in the context of a global diesel squeeze driven by both the Iran and Ukraine wars.

Latin America · Disaster

A magnitude 7.6–7.7 earthquake and a 6.6 aftershock rock Panama; Mulino declares a “major disaster”

+

On Friday morning, a powerful, shallow earthquake struck near Panama’s southern Pacific coast, about 10 km deep according to the USGS. It shook buildings in Panama City and set off tsunami warnings for Panama, Colombia, Ecuador, Nicaragua and Guatemala, which were later lifted. About two and a half hours later, a magnitude 6.6 quake struck near the coast, and officials counted more than 140 aftershocks. Damage is concentrated in the central provinces of Veraguas, Herrera, Los Santos and Coclé. It includes partial collapse at the Penonomé cathedral, a reported church collapse in Los Santos, and large cracks in roads. President José Raúl Mulino said no deaths had been documented as of Friday evening, warned that could change, and declared a state of “major disaster.” A Los Angeles County search-and-rescue team has been deployed.

Analysis: This is one of the strongest quakes in Panama’s modern record, and the low toll so far is notable. Assessments in rural provinces are still incomplete, so casualty figures may be revised. No effect on the Canal has been reported. Outlets differ on the size of the main shock: the Washington Post and France 24 report 7.6, and NBC, CBS and later CNN updates report 7.7, reflecting revisions to the preliminary estimate. They also differ on injuries: Mulino reported none documented, while other officials described several minor injuries.

Middle East · Aviation Security

UAE prosecutors say a flydubai co-pilot planned a 9/11-style suicide attack on Israel’s Ben-Gurion Airport

+

On Friday, UAE Attorney General Hamad Saif al-Shamsi released preliminary findings on flydubai flight FZ1073 from Dubai to Tel Aviv on Sept. 30. He said the 29-year-old co-pilot had planned a “premeditated” attack inspired by 9/11: to seize the plane and crash it into Ben-Gurion’s passenger terminal, causing “the greatest possible number of human casualties.” Prosecutors say he had flown two earlier Tel Aviv rotations to study the route. Over Saudi airspace, they say, he attacked the captain with a crash axe, and the aircraft dropped about 16,000 ft before he was overpowered and the crew landed in Saudi Arabia. The UAE did not name him. Reuters, citing people familiar with the matter, identified him as an Omani national.

Analysis: The incident is not new. What is new is the first official finding that it was a deliberate mass-casualty plot. That puts airline vetting, and the Gulf–Israel air links that have kept running during the Iran war, under scrutiny. Accounts conflict on vetting: the UAE says he passed every check with no sign of extremist views, while CBS sources describe a security lapse. He told investigators he acted alone, and US officials told NBC early signs point to a lone actor. Some officials have suggested Iranian links, but no evidence has been offered.

Sources: Washington Post, Oct 9, Reuters (via U.S. News), Oct 9, CNN, Oct 9, The National, Oct 9

Climate · Hurricanes & Energy

Isaias makes landfall near Destin as a Category 2, leaving more than 800,000 without power as about 72% of Gulf oil output sits shut in

+

Hurricane Isaias made landfall near Destin, Florida, around 8 p.m. ET Friday as a Category 2 storm with 105 mph sustained winds, according to the National Hurricane Center. It was the first hurricane of an unusually quiet Atlantic season. A 116 mph gust was recorded at Santa Rosa Sound. A flash-flood emergency was declared for Fort Walton Beach and Destin. The storm weakened quickly inland to a post-tropical cyclone, and the NHC’s final advisory early Saturday sent its remnants toward the Tennessee Valley. Parts of the Alabama–Florida line got more than a foot of rain. North Georgia, including metro Atlanta, faces a Level 3-of-4 flood risk. As of Saturday morning, more than 800,000 customers across the Southeast had no power, about 315,000 of them in Alabama. No deaths from this storm had been confirmed in the reporting reviewed.

On the energy side, producers shut in about 71.5% of US Gulf oil output and evacuated crews from 129 platforms. Refinery risk centered on Chevron’s Pascagoula plant and a refinery in Mobile, while the large Louisiana refineries were expected to escape the worst. Analysts expect quick restarts if facilities are undamaged. The disruption hits a fuel market already strained by the Iran war, the same squeeze behind Friday’s US–Russia diesel deal.

Tech · AI Safety

Three fired OpenAI safety researchers say they were pushed out for speaking up; OpenAI says they broke information rules

+

Former OpenAI safety researchers Mikita Balesni, Tomek Korbak and Jasmine Wang say the company fired them last week on pretexts, as punishment for being outspoken about safety or for working with outside researchers. Korbak says he was told his firing related to how he communicated with METR, the evaluation group OpenAI had partnered with to investigate an incident involving Hugging Face. The three say the firings have left former colleagues “afraid to speak,” and they warn the company may walk back a recent safety commitment.

OpenAI told NPR that a “thorough investigation” found the three repeatedly violated policies on sensitive information, and that “these decisions were not about raising safety concerns or speaking out.” CNN first reported the dispute on Thursday. Friday’s NPR and Al Jazeera reports added the researchers’ public accounts and the company’s response.

Electrification · EVs

Tesla drops the “Full Self-Driving” name in Europe after German pushback, rebranding it “Tesla Assisted Driving”

+

Tesla has renamed its driver-assistance software in Europe from “Full Self-Driving” to “Tesla Assisted Driving,” Electrek reported Friday. The change follows objections in Germany, where regulators and consumer groups have long argued the FSD name overstates what the system does. In the US, Tesla keeps the “FSD (Supervised)” label.

The rebrand is a concession on marketing as Tesla tries to get its assisted-driving features approved and deployed in Europe. Reports conflict on where approval by Germany’s federal motor-transport authority (KBA) stands, so how far the rollout has progressed is unclear. WNBA · Semifinals

Second-year Valkyries dethrone the Aces in overtime to sweep into the WNBA Finals

+

On Friday, the Golden State Valkyries beat the defending champion Las Vegas Aces 87–77 in overtime to finish a semifinal sweep. In only their second season, they are the fastest expansion team to reach the WNBA Finals. Golden State came back from 13 points down with a 17–0 third-quarter run. Gabby Williams’ steal set up Kayla Thornton’s tying layup with 1:50 left in regulation. The Valkyries then outscored Vegas 16–6 in overtime, helped by a Williams step-back jumper with 54.7 seconds left. Thornton finished with 18 points and Williams with 16, eight of them in overtime.

The No. 2 Valkyries host the No. 4 Atlanta Dream in the best-of-seven Finals starting Oct. 17. Neither franchise has won a title. In baseball, Friday was an off day. The Guardians–White Sox ALDS goes to a deciding Game 5 in Cleveland on Saturday night, and the winner opens the ALCS at Tampa Bay on Monday.
