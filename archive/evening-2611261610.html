White House · Economy · Data Integrity

In a Thanksgiving post, Trump calls his own Commerce Department’s pre-election GDP numbers “fake” and demands an investigation, a day after growth was revised up

+

On Thanksgiving morning, President Trump posted a roughly 460-word message on Truth Social from Mar-a-Lago. In it, he accused the Commerce Department of putting out deliberately wrong economic numbers before the Nov. 3 midterms and said he wanted an investigation. The post followed the Bureau of Economic Analysis’s Nov. 25 revision of third-quarter annualized GDP growth to 4.3%, up from the 3.4% advance estimate. Trump wished a happy Thanksgiving to those who issued “FAKE” numbers and corrected them “three weeks late,” calling it “a massive, and purposeful, error.” According to the AP, he offered no evidence that the revision was politically motivated. The BEA revises GDP on a fixed schedule of three estimates per quarter, regardless of the political calendar.

The same post attacked political opponents and immigrants, including Somali-Americans, and warned about Muslim religious law. Commerce Department officials did not immediately comment, and the White House said nothing beyond the post. One unusual feature of this complaint is that the revision made the economy look stronger. Trump’s earlier attacks on federal statistics were aimed at numbers that looked weak. The post comes as Republicans prepare to give up control of both chambers in January after heavy midterm losses.

The Left-Leaning Take

No in-window piece on the post turned up at Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian Opinion. The critique that comes through in news coverage, including Bloomberg’s description of the post as seizing on a routine revision to allege election interference without evidence, is that calling scheduled statistical revisions “purposeful” damages trust in federal data. Critics also say it continues the president’s pattern of blaming the midterm defeat on institutions instead of voters.

The Right-Leaning Take

No in-window reaction turned up at National Review, The Dispatch or the WSJ editorial page. The president’s own argument is that an initial estimate understating growth by nearly a full point, released just before the election, hurt Republicans and deserves scrutiny. No conservative outlet in the coverage reviewed has endorsed the claim that the error was deliberate.

National Security · DOJ · Export Controls

Reuters: the Justice Department never formally investigated Jensen Huang before Trump pushed for his return to Nvidia, and Trump says a pardon is “always an option” ahead of next week’s House grilling

+

On Wednesday, Reuters reported, citing three people familiar with the matter, that the Justice Department did not open a formal investigation into Jensen Huang before President Trump pushed for his reinstatement as Nvidia’s CEO. The question was whether Huang had been compromised through a relationship with a woman intelligence officials allege is a Chinese operative. According to Reuters, acting Attorney General Todd Blanche concluded there was no evidence Huang had broken the law based on the department’s criminal probes of two Nvidia customers, Megaspeed International and Super Micro Computer, which are suspected of smuggling advanced chips to China. That account conflicts with Trump’s claim on CNBC days earlier that DOJ “did a pretty good investigation” and Huang was “exonerated.” Vice President JD Vance said on Wednesday that he had been told Huang was under no investigation. At a news conference, Blanche repeatedly declined to say how the administration had determined that Huang posed no national-security risk.

Separately, Trump told the New York Post that a pardon for Huang is “always an option, I guess, but I don’t think it’ll be necessary.” He also urged Huang not to invoke the Fifth Amendment. Huang is scheduled for a closed-door interview with House committees on Tuesday, Dec. 1, followed by a public appearance reported for Dec. 3. Concern spans both parties. Sen. Elizabeth Warren says the reinstatement makes it more urgent to pass bipartisan export-control bills, and Republicans Pete Ricketts and John Kennedy have also raised questions. Kennedy said he expects Huang to plead the Fifth. Lawmakers are weighing whether to attach chip-tracking and export limits to the year-end defense bill. Megaspeed denies wrongdoing, and Beijing calls the spy allegations “smears.”

The Left-Leaning Take

No in-window piece turned up at Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian Opinion. The Democratic case is on the record. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer has promised to “wield their full power to investigate,” and Warren says Huang “still owe[s] Congress answers.” CNN’s news analysis points to questions about whether Trump’s investment accounts, which have frequently traded Nvidia stock, shaped his intervention. Democrats argue that floating a pardon before testimony undercuts congressional oversight.

The Right-Leaning Take

No in-window piece turned up at National Review, The Dispatch, Reason or the WSJ editorial page, and the right is split. Trump argues that Huang is an irreplaceable talent who could otherwise be lured to a Chinese firm. China hawks such as Ricketts and Kennedy want answers on national security. Sen. Dave McCormick has warned against Washington telling companies how to run their business, which he said “sounds a lot more like China than America.”

Courts · Immigration · 14th Amendment

A second federal judge blocks Trump’s August birthright-citizenship order, certifying a nationwide class and refusing to pause the ruling for appeal

+

On Wednesday, U.S. District Judge Christopher R. Cooper in Washington issued a preliminary injunction against President Trump’s August 2026 executive order on birthright citizenship. That order directed agencies to stop recognizing the citizenship of U.S.-born children in several new categories. Reported categories include children of foreign-government or international-organization employees without diplomatic immunity, births arranged through commercial surrogacy or “birth tourism,” and children of parents labeled suspected gang members or “alien enemies.” Cooper certified a class of children “who are or will be unlawfully denied citizenship under the Order.” He barred the State Department, the Department of Homeland Security and the Social Security Administration from applying the order to them, and he denied the government’s request for a stay pending appeal.

Cooper wrote that the Supreme Court had already made clear that “the President cannot carve out new exceptions to birthright citizenship for children born within the United States and subject to its laws.” That was a reference to the Court’s 6–3 decision on June 30 that struck down Trump’s January 2025 order. Judge Deborah Boardman in Maryland had already blocked the August order for a separate class in September, so the administration would have to overturn both rulings before enforcing it. The White House called Cooper an “activist judge” and said the order is consistent with the Supreme Court’s holdings. The Justice Department is expected to seek emergency relief from the D.C. Circuit. Reports differ on whether the ruling came late Wednesday or early Thursday.

The Left-Leaning Take

No in-window piece on the ruling turned up at Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian Opinion. On the record, the ACLU, which brought one of the challenges, calls the order a second attempt to get around a settled constitutional question. In its words, the Supreme Court “has twice rejected efforts to gut birthright citizenship, in 1898 and again earlier this year.” Critics on the left see the narrower categories as a test of whether lower courts will hold the line against piecemeal exceptions.

The Right-Leaning Take

No in-window reaction turned up at National Review, The Dispatch or the WSJ editorial page. The administration’s position, echoed in conservative outlets’ news coverage, is that the order stays within the narrow historical exceptions the Supreme Court recognized rather than reviving the 2025 approach. On the libertarian side, the Cato Institute’s David Bier has said since August that the new orders were “blatantly ignoring the Supreme Court’s decision,” which shows the right is not united on the policy.

Sanctions · Russia · Congress

A Boehly-led group signs a roughly $10 billion share-swap deal for Lukoil’s foreign assets with a 15% U.S. government stake, and the top House Foreign Affairs Democrat demands that it stop

+

A consortium led by Todd Boehly’s Eldridge signed an agreement on Wednesday to acquire Lukoil International, the non-Russian arm of the sanctioned Russian oil company. The deal is structured as a share swap worth about $10 billion: restricted shares in Lukoil’s parent company and other Russian firms would be exchanged for the overseas portfolio. That portfolio spans operations in 11 countries producing about 350,000 barrels a day, and sources value it at roughly $16 billion. The U.S. International Development Finance Corporation (DFC) would take about a 15% equity stake without putting in capital up front, which reports call a first for the agency. Assets in Kazakhstan are excluded. The deal needs a license from Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control, the same approval that an earlier Carlyle deal never received, and Boehly said closing could take up to a year.

Rep. Gregory Meeks, the ranking Democrat on the House Foreign Affairs Committee, called on the administration to halt the DFC’s role “immediately.” He argued that Congress barred the DFC from investing in Russia less than a year ago and that the structure gets around that ban. Critics also question whether a share swap creates value for a sanctioned company. They note that the shares would sit in an Emirati custodial account, while Treasury’s published sale conditions call for proceeds owed to Lukoil to stay blocked. The DFC and Boehly say the deal moves critical energy infrastructure out of Russian control.

The Left-Leaning Take

No in-window piece on the deal turned up at the left-leaning opinion outlets. The Democratic case, made by Meeks, is that the administration is using a government-backed vehicle to do what Congress explicitly forbade, and to the benefit of a well-connected buyer, a Trump supporter, as Ukrainian outlets have stressed. It ties into a broader Democratic argument about favoritism in post-sanctions energy deals.

The Right-Leaning Take

RUSI’s Petras Katinas told CNN that it shows Moscow being forced to give up overseas assets, and the DFC presents U.S. ownership as a strategic gain. Coverage also notes bipartisan unease about the DFC’s involvement, so the deal does not fall cleanly along party lines.

Tech · Space · AI Infrastructure

FCC gives SpaceX conditional approval for up to 1 million satellites for orbital AI data centers, over objections from Amazon, Viasat and astronomers

+

On Wednesday, the Federal Communications Commission gave SpaceX conditional approval to deploy and operate a constellation of up to 1 million satellites for an orbital data-center business. The idea is to run AI computing in space, powered by sunlight and cooled by radiating heat into space. Amazon, Viasat and the American Astronomical Society objected during the proceeding, raising concerns about orbital debris, radio interference and harm to astronomical observation.

The approval is an early regulatory step and does not mean orbital data centers are close. Launch cadence, heat rejection and the cost of hardware in orbit remain unsolved at that scale. Still, the scale is notable: the authorized number is many times the total of active satellites in orbit today. The decision also adds to the industry’s search for power beyond strained terrestrial grids, a week after several multi-hundred-megawatt AI infrastructure deals on the ground.

Climate · Earth Science · Nature

Two centuries of peatland drainage account for about 2.7% of human-caused warming, and keeping drained peat dry could nearly double that impact by 2100, a Nature study finds

+

A study published Wednesday in Nature by an MIT-led team, “An enduring climatic burden from past peatland drainage,” reconstructs the climate effect of draining peatlands worldwide since about 1800. Drained peat covers about 0.5% of Earth’s land and roughly 15% of all peatland area. The researchers find it accounts for about 2.7% of humanity’s warming influence since the Industrial Revolution. Drainage cuts methane emissions but releases far more carbon dioxide as the exposed peat decomposes, so the net effect is warming.

The researchers say that leaving currently drained peatlands as they are could nearly double their cumulative warming by 2100. Raising water tables cannot undo warming that has already happened, but it would avoid much of what the drained areas would otherwise add this century. That makes rewetting an inexpensive and often overlooked climate lever. MIT engineer César Terrer said a drained peatland “can keep emitting carbon dioxide for centuries.”
