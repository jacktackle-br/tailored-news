Public Health · Vaccines

Trump’s order to shrink the childhood vaccine schedule triggers a rare wave of Republican dissent — a GOP doctor-senator and a National Review contributor among those breaking ranks

+

The fallout is building around the executive order President Trump signed Monday, Aug. 10, alongside Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., directing the federal government to recommend fewer childhood immunizations. The order would cut the list of diseases children are advised to be vaccinated against from roughly 17–18 to 11, calls for the combined measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) shot to be split into three separate injections, and revives the long-debunked claim of a link between vaccines and autism; Kennedy is to chair a commission on vaccine safety. Public-health experts note the order isn’t even the lawful mechanism for setting the schedule — that authority sits with the CDC’s Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices — and the American Academy of Pediatrics and other medical groups say the safety and efficacy of the schedule has been established beyond question.

What makes this more than a familiar left-right fight is the pushback from inside the president’s own party, days before the fall school year. Sen. Bill Cassidy (R-La.), a physician, wrote: “I’m a doctor. This executive order is wrong. The President does not have the expertise to make these changes. Vaccines are overwhelmingly safe.” National Review contributor Dr. Pradheep Shanker called the order a political “epitaph.” (Facts from CNN, NPR, The Washington Post, NBC News, ABC News and CNBC.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The left sees an anti-science capitulation to Kennedy that endangers herd immunity for political gain — resurrecting a myth science buried decades ago, overriding the CDC’s expert process by fiat, and inviting measles outbreaks just as classrooms fill. The framing: this is what it looks like when a public-health agency is handed to a vaccine skeptic, with children as the test subjects. (Directional; no single in-window Vox / Mother Jones / Slate column was isolated.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The populist right cheers it as “medical freedom” — parental choice, distrust of a one-size-fits-all schedule and of the Fauci-era consensus, and a promise to follow the science wherever RFK Jr.’s review leads. But this is an unusually visible intra-right split, not the party line: Sen. Cassidy and a National Review contributor publicly broke with the order on the merits, warning it substitutes presidential say-so for clinical evidence and hands Democrats a midterm weapon. (Directional; the Cassidy/Shanker dissent is a labeled intra-right disagreement, not the mainstream-right position.)

Courts · International Law

Four US rights groups sue the Trump administration over its sanctions campaign against the International Criminal Court

+

Four leading American human-rights organizations — the American Friends Service Committee, the Center for Constitutional Rights, Human Rights Watch and the Open Society Institute — filed suit in the Southern District of New York on Tuesday, Aug. 11, arguing that the administration is unlawfully sanctioning people and groups affiliated with or assisting the International Criminal Court. The suit challenges Trump’s February 2025 executive order authorizing such sanctions, which the government has since used against the ICC prosecutor, eight ICC judges, the UN special rapporteur on the occupied Palestinian territories and three Palestinian rights groups. The plaintiffs contend the measures amount to an illegal, First Amendment–violating attack on international justice that bars them from lawfully engaging with the court.

It is the second major challenge to the order — three ICC judges have separately sued — and it lands amid the court’s war-crimes work touching Gaza and other conflicts, giving the case both a domestic free-speech dimension and a foreign-policy one. (Facts from The Washington Post, Human Rights Watch, The Hill and U.S. News; weighted toward primary legal filings.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The left reads this as Washington punishing lawyers and NGOs for the crime of pursuing accountability — using the sanctions machinery built for terrorists and cartels against human-rights defenders, and chilling constitutionally protected advocacy to shield allies from scrutiny. The through-line: a government that fears an independent court is a government worried about what the court would find. (Directional; drawn from the plaintiffs’ filings and statements, not an isolated opinion column.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right defends the sanctions as legitimate self-defense: the ICC has no jurisdiction over the United States or Israel, both non-members, and its pursuit of American and Israeli officials is a politicized overreach that a sovereign nation is entitled to resist. A libertarian strand (Reason) is queasier — wary of sanctions authorities sweeping up the speech and associations of U.S. citizens and NGOs — but the dominant conservative view is that protecting servicemembers from a foreign tribunal is the point. (Directional; no clean in-window National Review / WSJ-editorial column surfaced on the suit.)

Iran · Naval Blockade

A US Navy helicopter cripples a third blockade-runner with Hellfire missiles as an Iran–Oman deal to manage Hormuz reaches final drafting

+

US Central Command said a Navy MH-60 helicopter fired two Hellfire missiles into the engine room of the Panama-flagged cargo ship M/V Vela Nova in the Gulf of Oman on Tuesday, Aug. 11, after its civilian crew ignored repeated warnings to halt and turn back from an Iranian port. The strike disabled the vessel’s steering; a small fire broke out, and all 17 crew were evacuated to a nearby ship uninjured. CENTCOM said the Vela Nova is no longer heading to Iran and called it the third ship disabled since the US reimposed its naval blockade on July 14 — a campaign under which it says it has redirected 55 commercial vessels, disabled three and boarded two. Reporting notes it was the first such strike since Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth dismantled a Pentagon office dedicated to minimizing civilian casualties.

The escalation runs alongside quiet diplomacy: Iran and Oman are in the final drafting stage of a framework to manage traffic through the Strait of Hormuz — inbound ships using a northern lane in Iranian waters, outbound ships a southern lane in Omani waters, overseen by a joint coordination center. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi called a deal “very close,” but stressed that even a signed Oman pact would not by itself reopen the strait unless Washington honors a June memorandum by lifting the blockade and sanctions — the same standoff that has kept roughly a fifth of the world’s seaborne oil hostage to the crisis. (Facts from Stars and Stripes, CENTCOM, CNN, Bloomberg, Al Jazeera and Just Security.)

Sources: Stars and Stripes, Just Security, CNN, Bloomberg, Al Jazeera
Colombia · Disaster

Colombia’s magnitude-7.4 earthquake kills more than 200 as rescuers struggle to reach a devastated epicenter

+

The death toll from Monday’s magnitude-7.4 earthquake in western Colombia has climbed past 200 and is expected to keep rising. Colombian authorities put the confirmed toll at roughly 181 killed with about 2,595 injured, while a running CNN tally reached around 224; some 195 people are officially listed as missing, and civilian-run databases suggest far more. The epicenter, near San José del Palmar about 150 miles west of Bogotá, sits in a remote, underdeveloped region, and rescue crews have struggled to reach it — leaving the full scale of the destruction unclear days in.

The government has declared a state of emergency, and the disaster is testing Colombia’s new administration and its emergency services early in their tenure. Aftershocks, including a strong tremor felt in the capital, have compounded the danger and slowed the search for survivors trapped in collapsed structures across the affected zone. (Facts from NPR, CNN, Al Jazeera and TIME; casualty figures are as reported and were shifting through the day.)

Sources: NPR, CNN, Al Jazeera, TIME
Afghanistan · Education

Five years after the Taliban takeover, UNESCO says 2.4 million Afghan girls are shut out of secondary school

+

Marking the approach of the fifth anniversary of the Taliban’s August 2021 return to power, UNESCO said this week that an estimated 2.4 million Afghan girls remain excluded from secondary education — leaving Afghanistan the only country in the world to formally bar girls and women from schooling beyond the primary level. The agency noted that nearly one million girls were enrolled in secondary school by 2021, progress that two decades of expansion had achieved and that the Taliban’s bans have now reversed; a parallel prohibition on female teachers instructing boys has deepened teacher shortages and degraded education for everyone.

UNESCO condemned the restrictions and demanded the immediate restoration of girls’ and women’s right to education, while acknowledging that the community-based classes, literacy programs and psychosocial support it helps fund cannot substitute for formal schooling. The report lands as international leverage over Kabul remains thin and a generation of Afghan girls approaches adulthood having been denied a full education. (Facts from UNESCO via NBC News, Al Jazeera and The Washington Times.)

Sources: NBC News, Al Jazeera, The Washington Times

Elections · Primary Night Results

Democrats split down the middle in six-state primaries: progressive Peggy Flanagan romps to the Minnesota Senate nod, while a moderate edges out a democratic socialist in Wisconsin — and Trump’s endorsement record comes up mixed

+

Results from Tuesday’s primaries in six states — Alabama, Connecticut, Minnesota, Vermont, Wisconsin and South Carolina — came in overnight, and the headline is a Democratic Party still arguing over its direction. In Minnesota, progressive Lt. Gov. Peggy Flanagan won the Democratic Senate primary by roughly 20 points over moderate Rep. Angie Craig, to succeed retiring Sen. Tina Smith; Flanagan, backed by Smith, Bernie Sanders and Elizabeth Warren, would be the first Native American woman in the Senate if she wins in November. In the Minnesota governor’s race, Sen. Amy Klobuchar cruised to the Democratic nomination and will face Republican state House Speaker Lisa Demuth, who defeated Trump-endorsed MyPillow founder Mike Lindell. Rep. Ilhan Omar held off a renewed challenge from Don Samuels in MN-05 by about 16,000 votes.

In Wisconsin, the story flipped: Milwaukee County Executive David Crowley — a moderate who dropped out, then re-entered with outgoing Gov. Tony Evers’s endorsement — pulled off a narrow upset over democratic socialist state Rep. Francesca Hong, roughly 40.0% to 39.6%, after Hong had led late polls. Crowley will face Trump-endorsed Rep. Tom Tiffany, who won the GOP primary in a landslide. Alabama held a court-ordered second primary in four districts after the Republican legislature’s mid-cycle redraw — enabled by the Supreme Court’s Louisiana v. Callais ruling — erased one of the state’s two majority-Black districts, with several Trump-backed candidates advancing. The night’s throughline: progressives banked real wins (Flanagan, Omar) but fell just short of a marquee prize in a swing state, and Trump’s endorsement carried Tiffany while failing to lift Lindell. (Facts from NPR, CNN, NBC, WaPo and The Hill; exact margins in Wisconsin were still firming up as counting finished.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The left reads the night as proof of genuine progressive energy — a decisive Flanagan win and a Hong near-miss in Wisconsin show a base hungry for fighters who will confront Trump’s immigration crackdown and offer generational change. But party-aligned commentators also breathe relief at Crowley’s survival, arguing electability still matters in a true swing state, and cast Alabama’s second primary as the concrete damage of a Court-blessed racial gerrymander that dilutes Black voting power. (Directional synthesis from straight-news framing in NPR / CNN; no single in-window opinion column was isolated.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The right frames a Democratic Party lurching leftward — a progressive Senate nominee in a purple state and a socialist coming within a whisker of a governorship — as a general-election gift, and treats Callais as restoring race-neutral districting rather than gutting the Voting Rights Act. On endorsements, the mainstream read is that Trump’s clout still delivers where the party is united (Tiffany’s landslide), while Lindell’s loss to Demuth is spun as GOP primary voters being pragmatic about November. (Directional; no clean in-window National Review / Dispatch / WSJ-editorial column surfaced on the results.)

Courts · Voting Rights

A federal judge blocks Trump’s mail-voting order nationwide, barring the Postal Service from refusing to deliver ballots — a ruling that likely dooms the rules before November

+

On Tuesday, Aug. 11, U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani in Boston expanded an earlier injunction to a nationwide block on the U.S. Postal Service implementing a key provision of President Trump’s executive order overhauling mail-in voting ahead of the November midterms. The order had empowered USPS to adopt rules allowing it to refuse to deliver mail ballots in states that declined to coordinate with the agency on citizen-verification lists intended, the administration said, to prevent noncitizen voting. Talwani’s June ruling had covered only the roughly 23 mostly Democratic-led states (plus D.C.) that sued; the new order, in a suit brought by the League of Women Voters and other nonpartisan groups, extends the block to the whole country.

Talwani wrote that with fewer than 90 days before the midterms, the millions of citizens who rely on mail voting “require clarity as to how or whether they will vote in November.” It is the second legal defeat for the order — which the administration had asked the Supreme Court to let it enforce — and observers say it may effectively kill the policy for this cycle. The White House and USPS did not immediately comment. (Facts from Votebeat, Axios, Bloomberg, The Washington Post and The Hill; NPR was unreachable in-window.)

The Left-Leaning Take

Voting-rights advocates cast the ruling as a needed check on an administration “weaponizing” the federal government to sow chaos in elections. As an ACLU attorney put it, “The president does not get to rewrite the rules of voting” — the order, on this view, was a thinly disguised effort to disenfranchise mail voters under the pretext of a noncitizen-voting problem that data show is vanishingly rare. (Directional; drawn from advocacy statements quoted in the news reports, not an isolated opinion column.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right frames the fight around election integrity, arguing citizenship verification is common sense and that the ruling makes passage of the SAVE Act more urgent. A libertarian-leaning strand focuses instead on process — skeptical both of sweeping executive rewrites of election machinery and of a single district judge issuing nationwide injunctions, a recurring separation-of-powers complaint on the right. (Directional; no clean in-window National Review / WSJ-editorial column surfaced.)

Sources: Votebeat, Axios, Bloomberg, Washington Post, The Hill
Health Policy · Transgender Care

At Trump’s direction, CMS says Medicaid and CHIP will stop paying for gender-affirming care for minors — puberty blockers, hormones and surgery — starting in October

+

On Tuesday, Aug. 11, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services — led by Administrator Dr. Mehmet Oz and acting at Trump’s direction — announced that Medicaid and the Children’s Health Insurance Program (CHIP) will end funding for gender-affirming surgeries and hormone treatments, including puberty blockers, for people under 19. The change is set to take effect Oct. 13; mental-health treatment for transgender minors would still be covered, and patients already receiving hormone therapy would be phased out by April 2027. Trump said the government would not pay for “barbaric surgeries” on children, and Oz said America’s children “aren’t lab mice.”

The move is the latest in a string of measures targeting transgender Americans since Trump returned to office, most of which have drawn litigation: the Supreme Court has let the administration’s passport-sex policy stand and allowed states to enforce sports bans, while a lower court ruled against a declaration calling the treatments unsafe, and a challenge to a policy targeting hospitals is pending. The American Medical Association and most major medical groups support access to gender-affirming care, though the American Society of Plastic Surgeons said this year it found “insufficient evidence” that the benefits of such surgeries on minors outweigh the risks. LGBTQ legal groups vowed a fresh challenge. (Facts from STAT, AP via US News, Fox News and CBS.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The left sees a politically driven assault on a small, vulnerable population — the government inserting itself between families and their doctors, overriding the consensus of the AMA and pediatric bodies, and stripping poor trans kids of care the wealthy can still buy. It reads the “barbaric surgeries” rhetoric as demagoguery, given how rare surgery on minors actually is, and as one more front in a systematic campaign. (Directional; Vox / Mother Jones / Slate columns were not isolated in-window.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The right frames it as protecting children from irreversible interventions and refusing to make taxpayers fund them, pointing to European systems’ retrenchment and “insufficient evidence” findings as vindication of a precautionary line. A libertarian counter-current (Reason) is uneasier — wary of Washington dictating clinical decisions and of the parental-rights implications cutting both ways — but the dominant conservative reaction is approval. (Directional; no clean in-window National Review / Dispatch / WSJ-editorial piece surfaced.)

Korean Peninsula · Missiles

North Korea fires its second ballistic missile in a week toward the East Sea, protesting the imminent start of large US–South Korea military drills

+

North Korea launched a ballistic missile toward the East Sea (Sea of Japan) on Wednesday, Aug. 12, South Korea’s Joint Chiefs of Staff said, detecting the weapon from the Wonsan area on the east coast. The missile flew more than 700 km (435 miles); Japan’s Defense Ministry said it landed in waters outside Japan’s exclusive economic zone. It was Pyongyang’s second ballistic launch in under a week, following a short-range test on Aug. 6 that was the North’s first ballistic activity since late June.

The timing is pointed: South Korea and the United States are set to begin their annual large-scale Ulchi Freedom Shield exercises on Aug. 17, drills that Pyongyang routinely denounces as rehearsals for invasion. No damage or injuries were reported. The back-to-back launches suggest North Korea is signaling displeasure ahead of the exercises rather than testing a new capability, but they add friction to an already tense peninsula. (Facts from France 24, UPI, The Japan Times and Euronews.)

Technology · AI Infrastructure

Nvidia and six Wall Street giants launch financing platforms to steer more than $500 billion into AI data centers

+

Nvidia said on Aug. 10 that it had signed memorandums of understanding with six of Wall Street’s largest firms — Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR — to establish “compute financing platforms” designed to mobilize more than $500 billion of third-party capital for AI infrastructure over time. The idea is to treat compute — chips, servers, power and data centers — like commercial real estate or toll roads: an asset customers can borrow against to fund buildouts. CEO Jensen Huang told CNBC he approached only those six firms and none turned him down, calling his chips an “investable asset.” BlackRock’s Larry Fink likened the effort to the 1970s creation of mortgage-backed securities, the start of a “next future for financial engineering.”

The scale is striking, but so are the caveats: these are MOUs, not committed dollars, and the capital is to be deployed “over time.” The structure also intensifies a debate about circularity in the AI boom — Nvidia helping to finance the customers who buy its chips — and about whether hundreds of billions in debt-funded compute is a durable asset class or a bubble in the making. (Facts from Nvidia’s newsroom, CNN, CNBC and Axios.)

Energy · The Grid

Solar and batteries will make up nearly four-fifths of new US power capacity in 2026, the EIA projects, as storage additions leap again

+

U.S. developers plan to add about 86 gigawatts of new utility-scale generating capacity in 2026, and the mix is overwhelmingly clean, according to Energy Information Administration data. Solar accounts for roughly 51% of the planned additions — about 43 GW, a 60% jump over last year — battery storage 28%, and wind 14%. Utility-scale battery additions alone are projected at around 24 GW this year, up from a record 15 GW installed in 2025, as grid operators lean on storage to soak up midday solar surpluses and discharge into the evening peak.

The build-out is being driven by fast-falling battery costs (down sharply again in 2025) and surging electricity demand from data centers and electrification. Marquee projects include a 160 MW / 640 MWh system at California’s SMUD and large solar-plus-storage plants in Texas. The caveat: these are planned additions, not completed megawatts, and the pace faces headwinds from shifting federal clean-energy tax policy and interconnection queues. Astronomy · Black Holes

A star’s death on a galaxy’s outskirts betrays a hidden ‘wandering’ supermassive black hole — flagged by an AI trained to hunt them

+

A team led by Robert Stein of the University of Maryland and NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center reported catching a “wandering” supermassive black hole — roughly a million times the Sun’s mass — in the act of shredding a star that drifted too close, about 30,000 light-years from the center of its host galaxy. The event, TDE 2025abcr, was published Monday, Aug. 10, in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, and is described as only the second confirmed tidal disruption event detected outside a galaxy’s nucleus — strong evidence that such nomad black holes, likely stripped from smaller galaxies in past mergers, can be found from the ground with visible-light surveys.

The find leaned heavily on automation. The Zwicky Transient Facility flags on the order of 500,000 transient events every night — far more than humans can sift — so the researchers built an AI algorithm trained on the specific light signature of tidal disruptions and set it to search the whole sky, not just galactic centers. Launched in August 2025, the program flagged the tell-tale flare three months later, in November. The caveat is inherent to the result: it is peer-reviewed but hinges on a single object, and pinning down the black hole’s exact history will take more like it. (Peer-reviewed. Facts from Sky & Telescope, Sci.News, ScienceDaily and Science.)

Materials · Clean Energy

A new ‘stainless steel for hydrogen’ could replace titanium in seawater electrolyzers — and cut a key cost roughly 40-fold

+

Researchers at the University of Hong Kong, led by mechanical-engineering professor Mingxin Huang, reported a corrosion-resistant alloy they call “stainless steel for hydrogen” (SS-H2), engineered to survive the brutally corrosive conditions inside electrolyzers that split water into hydrogen. In a saltwater electrolyzer, the team says, the new steel performed comparably to the titanium structural components used in today’s industrial practice — but at a fraction of the cost, potentially cutting the price of those structural materials by around 40 times and making green hydrogen from seawater far more economical.

The trick is a “sequential dual-passivation” technique combining chromium- and manganese-based protective layers; the researchers note the manganese-based passivation is counter-intuitive and “cannot be explained” by current corrosion science, a puzzle in its own right. The usual caveat applies: this is a laboratory result (released around Aug. 11) that still has to survive the leap from experimental steel to durable industrial components before it changes the economics of hydrogen at scale. (Facts from ScienceDaily, TechSpot and Chemeurope.)

Sources: ScienceDaily, TechSpot, Chemeurope
