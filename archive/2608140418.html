National Security · US–Iran War

Iran strikes two more Emirati tankers in the Strait of Hormuz as the U.S. opens a fresh sanctions front and vows a blockade ‘indefinitely’

+

The roughly five-month U.S.–Iran war centered on the Strait of Hormuz escalated again on Thursday, Aug. 13, when the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC) said two of its vessels were attacked while transiting the strait. ADNOC reported no injuries and said the situation was “under control”; the UAE, and separately Qatar, accused Iran and condemned the strikes. It was the second incident involving ADNOC ships in under a week, following an Aug. 8 attack on an ADNOC tanker.

Rather than launching a fresh military wave, Washington tightened economic pressure: on Friday, the Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control sanctioned individuals, exchange houses and companies accused of helping Iran’s Shahr Bank move hundreds of millions of dollars clandestinely — described as Treasury’s eighth 2026 action against Iran’s “shadow-banking” network under the “maximum pressure” campaign. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the U.S. would apply measures that have “never been seen,” and War Secretary Pete Hegseth said the U.S. can maintain its naval blockade of Iranian ports “indefinitely.” Outlets note a sharp factual dispute: President Trump has claimed the U.S. has “total control” of Hormuz, while Iran’s Gulf authority insists the strait “remains blocked and will not be reopened until Iran’s conditions are accepted” — and CNBC reported strait traffic near three-month lows, cutting against the “total control” claim. (Facts from The National, CNBC, CBS News, Fox News and NPR.)

Health & Politics · Vaccine Policy

Backlash builds against Trump’s childhood-vaccine order as medical societies revolt and the GOP’s Bill Cassidy — a physician — calls it ‘wrong’

+

Fallout intensified this week from the “Gold Standard Childhood Vaccine Recommendations” executive order Trump signed Monday, Aug. 10, which directs cutting the number of universally recommended childhood shots (reporting frames the change as the list of diseases covered dropping from roughly 17–18 to 11), splitting the combined MMR vaccine into three separate single-disease shots, and downgrading flu, COVID, rotavirus and hepatitis A and B to “shared clinical decision-making,” with dengue advised only for high-risk groups. The order revives a vaccine–autism link that decades of studies have found does not exist (FactCheck.org and Tier-1 outlets flagged the claim as false). By Aug. 11–13, major medical organizations — the American Academy of Pediatrics, American College of Physicians, American Public Health Association and Infectious Diseases Society of America among them — publicly rejected the order, and the Florida AAP chapter said it would not comply. HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., present at the signing, praised Trump for “gold-standard science.”

The response cut across party lines. Sen. Bill Cassidy (R-La.), a physician who chairs the Senate HELP Committee, said: “This executive order is wrong. The President does not have the expertise to make these changes,” adding “Vaccines are overwhelmingly safe… Vaccines DO NOT cause autism,” and urged parents to follow their pediatrician over the order. The Wall Street Journal editorial board also warned against the measles/vaccine push. Analysts expect legal challenges; earlier in 2026 an AAP-led suit led a judge to block prior Kennedy vaccine-schedule changes, and splitting combined shots would in practice mean more separate visits for families. (Facts from NPR, NBC News, STAT News, TIME, PBS NewsHour and FactCheck.org.)

Trade & Economy · Tariff Enforcement

A White House ‘Great Transshipment Scam’ report accuses 40-plus nations — Canada and Mexico included — of helping China dodge tariffs, pegging losses as high as $26 billion

+

On Aug. 13–14 the White House Office of Trade and Manufacturing Policy, led by trade adviser Peter Navarro, released a roughly 25-page report titled “The Great Transshipment Scam,” alleging the U.S. loses about $19 billion to $26 billion a year in tariff revenue (the report cites a wider $10 billion–$100 billion range) to goods — largely Chinese-origin — that are rerouted, relabeled or minimally processed in third countries to secure lower U.S. duty rates. It identifies more than 40 countries at “elevated risk” of serving as hubs in what it calls a “global Shadow Transshipment Network,” naming Mexico and Canada on the U.S. land borders plus the EU, India, Japan, South Korea, Israel and Malaysia, and singling out Canada among China’s “biggest enablers.” It warns that Chinese goods routed through Mexico or Canada can improperly claim USMCA treatment and drop the China-specific duty to zero; separately, U.S. Customs and Border Protection said it is deploying AI tools — analyzing container markings, packaging patterns and X-ray imaging — to flag mismatches between declared and actual cargo.

Canadian trade officials signaled they could walk away from USMCA talks if Trump triggers threatened 50% tariffs. On the analysis, economist Daniel Drezner said Navarro is correct that U.S. tariffs triggered a spike in transshipment — but Navarro was widely mocked for treating tariff arbitrage as a scandal rather than the predictable result of the tariffs themselves. (Facts from BNN Bloomberg, Fortune, The Hill, Global News and Reason.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left/critic frame reads the report as Navarro discovering the obvious — high tariffs create evasion — then blaming allies for a problem the tariff regime itself manufactured, while U.S. consumers keep absorbing the cost of the trade war. Guardian polling cited in-window found majorities across parties say tariffs hurt consumers more than they help. (Directional; no in-window whitelisted opinion column was isolated.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Two right-of-center strands diverge. The protectionist/administration line treats transshipment as real Chinese cheating that justifies tougher enforcement and closing the USMCA loophole. The free-market/libertarian line (Reason) argues the tariffs are the problem — its late-July analysis pegged the cost at roughly $900 per U.S. household in 2026 — and the WSJ editorial board’s criticism of Trump’s tariff “fixation” reflects establishment-right skepticism of the whole enterprise. (Directional; no clean in-window National Review / Dispatch column surfaced.)

Middle East · West Bank

U.S. Ambassador Huckabee brands Israeli settlers ‘terrorists’ after a five-night siege of a Palestinian-American family, and the IDF moves in to end it

+

For five nights, dozens of Israeli settlers besieged three Palestinian homes on the edge of Qusra in the occupied West Bank, cutting water and electricity and pitching tents outside as the families’ supplies dwindled; one of the homes belongs to a Palestinian-American family, with a brother in Ohio watching via security cameras. On Aug. 13, U.S. Ambassador Mike Huckabee — a longtime ally of the settler movement — issued a rare, sharp rebuke on X, calling the settlers “terrorists” and the siege “a horrific act of terror meant to intimidate and harass this family,” and said the Israeli military acted at his request. The IDF entered Qusra, declared a closed military zone and dismantled settler outpost structures.

The response was chaotic: the military issued, then rescinded after publicity, an order for Palestinians to evacuate about 15 homes. Some families returned; at least one told The Times of Israel it still could not reach its house, and settlers remained elsewhere in the village. The story is notable less for the siege itself than for the messenger — “terrorist” is language U.S. officials almost never apply to Israeli settlers — signaling friction between Washington and Israel’s settler right even as Huckabee defended the IDF. It lands amid an increasingly disordered West Bank enforcement picture, with Defense Minister Israel Katz separately moving Aug. 14 to shift civilian enforcement from the IDF to the police. Americas · Colombia

Colombia’s earthquake death toll climbs past 265 as rescue enters its ‘final phase’ and thousands remain missing in remote Chocó

+

The death toll from the magnitude-7.4 earthquake that struck Colombia’s remote Chocó department on Aug. 10 kept rising through Aug. 13–14, with rescue operations entering what officials called the “final phase” as the critical 72-hour window closed. Figures diverge across outlets: ColombiaOne (Aug. 13) reported 265 dead and 496 missing; ABC News reported 281 dead and more than 12,000 homes destroyed; broader tallies cite more than 3,700 injured and figures for the missing ranging from roughly 2,700 up to about 4,100. The epicenter, near San José del Palmar, remains hard to reach, with damage extending to Cali, Pereira, Quibdó and Manizales, and shaking felt in Ecuador and Panama.

The disaster is an early test for Colombia’s new government. International aid is mobilizing — El Salvador pledged two planeloads (about 100 tons) of relief, and Mexico redeployed its “Topos” rescue brigade to Cali. The wide spread in official casualty and missing counts reflects the epicenter’s inaccessibility, and authorities warn the toll will keep climbing as crews reach collapsed structures. (Facts from CNN, ABC News, France 24 and NPR.)

Yemen · Civil War

The UN envoy warns Yemen faces its highest risk of full-scale war since the 2022 truce as a Houthi offensive hits the Taiz frontlines

+

On Thursday, Aug. 14, UN Special Envoy Hans Grundberg told the UN Security Council that Yemen faces its most serious threat of a return to large-scale war since the UN-brokered truce of 2022 — a truce that largely held but was never converted into a durable settlement. Grundberg cited intensified military activity along multiple frontlines, including recent Houthi attacks in Marib, Hadramawt and Mocha that caused military and civilian casualties, and warned that renewed Houthi strikes on commercial vessels in the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden risk pulling Yemen into a wider regional confrontation. He paired the warning with a stark humanitarian picture: more than half of Yemen’s population is acutely food-insecure, with roughly 6 million people at “Emergency” levels bordering on famine. On the ground the same day, government forces said they repelled a Houthi assault along the al-Mafalis front in southeastern Taiz province, with at least one government soldier killed.

Outlets trace the breakdown to a July 13 Saudi airstrike on the Sanaa airport runway that blocked an Iranian flight carrying a senior Houthi delegation; the Houthis responded with missile-and-drone barrages that killed dozens of government soldiers across Hadramout and Marib in early August, struck Saudi Arabia’s Abha airport, and declared a blockade on Saudi shipping — including an Aug. 12 Bab al-Mandeb ship attack that Yemen’s government said killed six. This is the internal Houthi-versus-Saudi-backed-government war, distinct from the U.S.–Iran naval dynamic in Hormuz. Reporting differs mainly on dating (Reuters filed Aug. 13; Al Jazeera and the UN readout place the briefing Aug. 14) and on precise casualty tallies, with government claims of “heavy” Houthi losses unverified by independent sources. (Facts from Reuters, UN Meetings Coverage, Al Jazeera and Asharq Al-Awsat.)

Climate · Oceans

How fast we warm — not just how much — may decide whether the Atlantic’s overturning circulation collapses, a new study finds

+

A study published Aug. 13, 2026 in Nature Climate Change by researchers at Utrecht University finds that the rate of global warming is a decisive factor in whether the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) — the current system that carries tropical heat northward and keeps Western Europe mild — tips into collapse. Using climate-model experiments that ramp CO₂ at different speeds, the team reports that under rapid warming the AMOC can collapse at as little as +2 °C, whereas under slow warming it stayed stable past +5 °C. They identify a critical pace of roughly 0.3 °C per decade — a rate the world is already approaching — arguing the system can “fail to track” a stable state when the forcing changes too quickly. The paper is titled “Failure to track a stable AMOC state under rapid climate change.”

The caveat is inherent to the method: these are model-based results, and real-world AMOC collapse thresholds remain highly uncertain — prior estimates put the temperature threshold near +4 °C with a wide 1.4–8 °C range — so the finding reframes the debate around the speed of warming rather than resolving it. (Facts from Nature Climate Change and Phys.org.)

Sources: Nature Climate Change, Phys.org
Science · Space

JWST spots a ‘black hole star’ at cosmic dawn — a possible key to the early universe’s giant black holes

+

Astronomers report MoM-BH*-1, described as a “black hole star”: a rapidly growing black hole, on the order of about 100,000 solar masses, so thoroughly wrapped in a dense, turbulent envelope of hydrogen gas that it radiates like an enormous star — roughly 100 billion times brighter than any single star can physically shine. It is seen as it was only about 660 million years after the Big Bang. The object was found in the JWST-based “Mirage or Miracle” survey and is presented as a candidate explanation for JWST’s mysterious “little red dots.” The work appeared in Nature (“A gas-enshrouded and gas-reddened black hole at cosmic dawn”), from an international team led by Rohan Naidu.

A note on timing: the paper posted Aug. 12 and drew heavy coverage Aug. 12–13, placing it at the older edge of the 24-hour window — included here as the week’s marquee science result. The caveat is that the interpretation — a “new class” of object, and its link to the little red dots — is a leading hypothesis rather than settled science; how common and how long-lived such gas cocoons are remains open. (Facts from Scientific American and the team’s Nature paper.)

Sources: Scientific American
