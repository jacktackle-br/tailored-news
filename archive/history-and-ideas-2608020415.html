Philosophy

Even including a thinker can be a way of erasing them

+

Published in Aeon on July 27, the essay “Epistemic convergence in the study of world philosophies” takes aim at a subtle failure mode buried inside the well-meaning project of globalising philosophy. The problem it names is “epistemic convergence”: the largely unconscious tendency of Western-trained scholars to read non-Western thinkers only through the parts that already resemble Kant, Hume or analytic logic — assimilating whatever fits a pre-existing European framework while quietly discarding the rest. The result, the essay argues, is that Africana, Chinese and Indian philosophies are admitted to the canon only after being flattened into “tamer versions of ourselves,” their more radical or genuinely alien commitments filed off. Borrowing and extending a concept from the philosopher Tommy J. Curry — the first Black professor of philosophy in Scotland, at Edinburgh — the piece frames this not as a scholarly nicety but as a continuation of structural exclusion by other means: reading colonised thinkers with an Anglo-European gaze distorts them precisely by “improving” them.

What makes the essay bracing is that it refuses the easy anti-canon posture and instead demands something harder of specialists — go to the original languages, sit with the historical and existential worlds these thinkers actually inhabited, and let their categories unsettle yours rather than confirming them. It reframes the tired “Western philosophy is parochial” debate into a concrete methodological ethics: true diversity in philosophy is not achieved by adding names to a syllabus but by resisting the gravitational pull to translate everything back into a European idiom. It is a self-implicating argument about how even inclusion, done lazily, becomes a form of erasure.

Sources: Aeon
Religion

A newly energized field insists Black Americans were never at the margins of Buddhism

+

In a Religion News Service feature dated July 24, “Long overlooked and newly energized, Black Buddhist studies challenges US Buddhist history,” scholars in an emerging field argue that the standard narrative of American Buddhism — white converts on one side, Asian immigrant communities on the other — has erased more than a century of Black practitioners, teachers and thinkers. Central to the reappraisal is a critique of the influential “Two Buddhisms” framework, which scholars such as Wakoh Shannon Hickey say quietly installs whiteness as the universal, normative centre of the American convert story. The piece is pegged to a concrete institutional moment: in early August, Princeton’s Black Buddhism Faculty Project (launched in 2023) hosts a four-day training workshop meant to build a teachable field in which scholars of African American religion and of Buddhism talk to each other in a sustained way for the first time.

The claim reaches well beyond academic turf. Figures like Rima Vesely-Flad — founder of the Initiative for Black Buddhist Studies and author of The Fire Inside: The Dharma of James Baldwin and Audre Lorde — are reading canonical Black writers as dharma teachers, arguing that liberation depends not only on mass movements but on inner well-being, embodiment and the skilful metabolising of rage. The deeper argument is not merely that Black Buddhists exist but that Buddhism is an American religious tradition braided into African American intellectual and spiritual life all along — a reframing that unsettles who gets to count as a “convert,” a “tradition,” and a founder, and a live example of a religious history being rewritten in real time.

Sources: Religion News Service, Interfaith America
History · Maritime Archaeology

A shipwreck full of champagne turns out to be a floating peace offering to a czar

+

Smithsonian Magazine reported on July 27 that a mid-19th-century wreck surveyed off the Swedish coast has turned out to be far stranger than an ordinary cargo loss. In 2024, Polish divers examining the roughly 18-metre vessel found a clay bottle stamped “Nassau” — the clue that began to unravel the story. The hold held hundreds of bottles of Louis Roederer champagne, cases of Selters mineral water from the German Duchy of Nassau, wines, porcelain tableware, decanters, state-of-the-art scientific instruments, and even rare meteorites — an inventory of Victorian-era luxury and prestige assembled with obvious care. The researcher Pawel Truszynski argues the cargo was a coordinated gift bound for Czar Alexander II of Russia in the immediate aftermath of the Crimean War.

What lifts this above a treasure-hunt headline is what the manifest reveals about how peace was actually made in the 19th century. The goods, Truszynski suggests, formed “a physical bridge of goodwill” — meant to reopen the commercial channels the war had severed and to cement tiny Nassau’s strategic alignment with the Russian empire. Diplomacy here is legible not in treaties but in bottles: champagne and mineral water as instruments of statecraft, meteorites and scientific instruments as flattery aimed at an emperor’s curiosity. The wreck is, in effect, a diplomatic overture frozen mid-delivery on the seafloor — a rare material snapshot of soft power in the age of the Concert of Europe.

Sources: Smithsonian Magazine
