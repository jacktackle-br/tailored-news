History · The American Founding

Two hundred fifty years ago this week the country quietly acquired its name — and it very nearly wasn’t ‘the United States of America’ at all

+

On September 9, 1776 — 250 years ago this week — the Second Continental Congress resolved that official documents should henceforth read “the United States” in place of “the United Colonies,” and Smithsonian’s history desk marked the semiquincentennial by revisiting how the nation stumbled into its name. The phrase itself was not coined that day: Thomas Jefferson had already reached for it in June, opening his draft of the Declaration with “A Declaration of the Representatives of the UNITED STATES OF AMERICA.” What Congress did in September was administrative — a change of usage — and yet it fixed, almost casually, the words a quarter of a billion people would one day live inside.

What makes the episode worth turning over is the road not taken. “United Colonies” carried the whiff of continued dependency; the poets preferred Columbia, the female personification of the new republic that survives in place names and a university; and, most improbably, some floated the United States of Alleghania, after the Allegheny mountains, as if the country might have been named for a ridge line rather than a hemisphere. National identity, the story reminds us, is partly a matter of contingency and copy-editing: the grandest of our self-descriptions began life as a bureaucratic emendation, and only the passage of 250 years has made it feel inevitable.

Social Science · Complexity & Networks

A new model offers an unsettling explanation for hyper-connected societies that keep fracturing: past barely four or five close ties, the math of friendship tips toward the echo chamber

+

Researchers at the Medical University of Vienna and the Complexity Science Hub Vienna — Stefan Thurner, Markus Hofer and Jan Korbel, writing in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences — built a strikingly simple model in which people continuously adjust both their opinions and their social ties to reduce social friction. It behaves calmly, converging on a broad consensus, so long as the average person keeps only a handful of close connections. But push the average past a low threshold — the splintering sets in at around four and a half close ties — and the whole network abruptly reorganizes into rival camps, each internally agreeable and mutually estranged.

The unsettling part is the correlation the authors then draw. Modern communication technology has roughly doubled the number of close relationships a typical person maintains, and that increase tracks the same decades in which many societies fractured into hostile political blocs. When the team tested the model against multi-year survey data — including Pew figures on ideology and friendship counts — the simulated pattern matched the real one. The counterintuitive moral is that connection is not the cure for division but, past a point, one of its engines: polarization emerges structurally, almost thermodynamically, from the sheer density of our ties, without needing villains or algorithms to explain it. It is, of course, a stylized model — but a provocative one for an age that keeps prescribing more connectivity as the remedy for the loneliness and discord that connectivity helped produce.

Sources: PsyPost, PNAS
Philosophy & Religion · History of Ideas

Are we living through a second ‘Axial Age’? A revived question asks whether AI and synthetic biology are stirring the same deep reordering of the self that once produced the Buddha, the Hebrew prophets and Socrates

+

The idea belongs to Karl Jaspers, the German-Swiss philosopher who named the “Axial Age” — that astonishing window, roughly 800 to 200 BCE, when civilizations with little contact between them independently turned from myth toward reflection, ethics and the inner life. In the space of a few centuries the world acquired Confucius and the Daoists in China, the Upanishads and the Buddha in India, Zoroaster in Persia, the Hebrew prophets in Israel and the first philosophers in Greece. This week the concept returns as the frame for the 2026 Berggruen Prize essay theme, developed at length in Noema: “A New Axial Age?”

The wager — advanced in essays by Otto Scharmer and a conversation with the complexity theorist Stuart Kauffman — is that our own convulsions in artificial intelligence, synthetic biology and genetic engineering are again unsettling the oldest questions of origin and destiny, and in doing so are reviving a religious and ethical imagination that a coolly technocratic age had assumed it was done with. The question rhymes, pleasingly, with last week’s reading on Susan Neiman and Steven Lukes, who leaned on the Axial Age as evidence that moral convergence recurs across human cultures. Whether ours is a genuine axial turn or merely a technological disruption dressed in cosmic language is exactly the provocation — and a worthy one to sit with, given how rarely we ask whether the tools remaking our world are also remaking what we take a human being to be.

Sources: Noema Magazine, Berggruen Institute
