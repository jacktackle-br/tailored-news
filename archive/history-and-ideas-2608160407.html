History · Medieval England & the Church

The letter that may have decided 1066 — not on the battlefield at Hastings, but in a papal schism a thousand miles away

+

For nearly a millennium the story of the Norman Conquest has hung on a broken promise: Harold Godwinson swore an oath to William of Normandy, broke it by taking the English crown, and forfeited the moral high ground when the pope blessed William’s invasion. A fragment of a lost letter by Pope Alexander II — surviving as excerpts in medieval collections of papal texts and freshly re-read by the historian Tom Licence of the University of East Anglia — complicates that tidy morality tale. In it, Alexander describes England as fallen under men “filled with the pride of their father Satan.” Licence argues the language is not generic invective but the specific rhetoric of the papal schism of the 1060s, when two rival popes — Alexander II and the antipope Honorius II — split the Church. His reading suggests Harold’s fatal error may have been backing the wrong pope, giving Alexander a political-religious motive to legitimize the conquest as something closer to a holy war than a mere enforcement of feudal oath-keeping.

What makes this a genuine reappraisal rather than a footnote is how it reframes the Conquest as an event shaped as much by continental Church politics as by anything that happened at Hastings — “the outcome may also have been shaped by decisions taken almost a thousand miles away in Rome,” as Licence puts it. The find arrives with pointed timing: it anchors his new biography Harold: Warrior King and lands as British institutions mount fresh attention on the Bayeux Tapestry, the very artifact whose triumphalist Norman narrative the letter quietly destabilizes.

Philosophy · Free Will & Metaphilosophy

Why the oldest argument in philosophy refuses to die — and what its stubbornness reveals about the limits of the method

+

Julian Baggini uses the free-will problem as a lens on a stranger, more unsettling question: why do some philosophical disputes never resolve, no matter how much brilliance is thrown at them? He begins with Hume’s optimistic diagnosis — that when an argument runs on interminably, “we may presume that there is some ambiguity in the expression, and that the disputants affix different ideas to the terms.” Clear up the definitions, Hume thought, and the deadlock dissolves; his own compatibilism, reconciling freedom with causal determinism, was meant to be that dissolution. But two centuries later P.F. Strawson opened his landmark “Freedom and Resentment” already resigned to failure, offering it “as a move towards reconciliation” while conceding it “is likely to seem wrongheaded to everyone.” The disagreements were as wide as ever.

Baggini’s essay is really about the limits of philosophical method. The debate persists, he suggests, not because philosophers are careless with words but because compatibilism keeps striking many as a “watered-down” substitute — the reconciliation buys peace only by quietly redefining the very thing people cared about. That points to a deeper divide over what we are even asking for when we ask whether we are free, a divide no amount of conceptual clarification seems able to close. It is a bracing meditation on the possibility that intractability itself is a permanent feature of certain human questions, rather than a temporary embarrassment awaiting the right clever move.

Natural Sciences · Biology of Aging

Aging as choreography, not decay — and why that reframing reopens one of biology’s oldest disputes

+

The reigning intuition about aging is entropic: cells wear out, damage accumulates, the machine breaks down. Junyue Cao’s work at Rockefeller University, profiled in Quanta, pushes toward a more provocative picture — that aging looks less like haphazard breakdown and more like a remodeling of the cell society, a coordinated redistribution of which cell types the body contains. Using a technique called EasySci-ATAC, Cao’s team profiled chromatin accessibility across roughly seven million cells from 21 mouse tissues at three ages, assembling what may be the most comprehensive epigenomic atlas of mammalian aging yet built. About a quarter of all cell types shift significantly with age; strikingly, many of these shifts are coordinated across different organs rather than occurring randomly and locally, and males and females often age quite differently at the cellular level.

The reframing matters philosophically as much as medically. If aging is partly a program — governed by epigenomic instructions that redistribute the body’s cellular makeup — then it is potentially legible, and perhaps steerable, in ways that pure wear-and-tear models never allowed. Cao is careful not to claim aging is simply “meant” to happen, but the atlas reopens one of biology’s oldest disputes: whether senescence is an unavoidable running-down or an orchestrated late act of the same developmental process that built us in the first place. It is a genuine conceptual reappraisal dressed as a data paper.
