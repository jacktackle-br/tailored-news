Courts · Elections

Supreme Court clears the way for Trump’s mail-in voting order ahead of the midterms, splitting 6–3

+

In an emergency-docket order Monday, Aug. 24, the Supreme Court divided 6–3 along ideological lines to lift a Massachusetts-based judge’s June injunction that had blocked key parts of President Trump’s executive order restricting mail-in voting. The relief was sought against a suit by California and 22 other Democratic-led states (23 in all). Per reporting, the order lets the Department of Homeland Security compile a federal “citizenship list” from federal databases and share it with states, directs the U.S. Postal Service to deliver mail ballots only to people on its voter-eligibility lists (and gives USPS rulemaking authority over state mail-ballot designs), and allows the attorney general to prioritize prosecution of state officials who issue ballots to ineligible voters.

Justices Sonia Sotomayor and Elena Kagan dissented, warning the administration has signaled it would use the new authority to investigate and prosecute state representatives; Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson wrote that the ruling “needlessly injects chaos and uncertainty into the upcoming midterm elections.” Crucially, the majority stressed the order is procedural: it postpones, rather than resolves, whether the president may direct state election administration, and does not endorse the executive branch’s authority to implement the directives. Legal experts cautioned the decision on its own is unlikely to change much for individual voters immediately. (Facts from NPR, CBS News, CNN, Axios and SCOTUSblog.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads this as the Court greasing the rails for voter suppression weeks before the midterms: federal “citizenship lists” and a Postal Service told to withhold ballots are, in this view, a machinery of disenfranchisement, and the threat to prosecute state election officials is naked intimidation of the people who run elections. Jackson’s “chaos” warning, they argue, is the point — uncertainty depresses turnout. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right frames citizen-only, verified mail voting as basic election integrity and welcomes the Court declining to let a single district judge freeze a national policy. A libertarian counter-current (Reason-style) is wary: conscripting the Postal Service and DHS into policing state-run elections is exactly the kind of federal overreach conservatives distrust, and the majority itself conceded it hasn’t decided whether any of it is lawful. (Directional.)

National Security · Iran

The Iran sanctions land: Bessent rolls out ‘Operation Economic Outcast,’ hitting nearly 60 targets and widening secondary sanctions to crypto, gold and aviation

+

The package previewed this morning arrived Monday afternoon. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent unveiled what he dubbed “Operation Economic Outcast,” sanctioning roughly 60 entities, individuals and vessels tied to illicit procurement of nuclear and missile technology, cyber operations and oil smuggling, and suspending several previously issued licenses that had permitted certain payments to Iran and Iranian access to U.S. cultural and academic institutions. Bessent said Washington is expanding the categories of secondary sanctions to reach digital assets, technology, gold, aviation and shipping, and pressed foreign governments and firms to cut economic ties with Tehran.

Bessent set no firm enforcement timeline, saying only that the administration does “not have infinite patience” and that Iran would “see the ramifications” quickly if it does not respond. Critics — and even some sympathetic outlets — noted the announcement was thin on specifics about direct action against major Chinese banks, the biggest buyers of Iranian crude. Tehran, meanwhile, advanced plans for Strait of Hormuz “transit fees” and repeated warnings it could seize non-compliant vessels. This is the concrete delivery of the “economic D-Day” the morning brief covered as a preview. (Facts from NBC News, NPR, Axios, CNN and CNBC.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a maximalist re-run: naming 60 targets and branding it “Operation Economic Outcast” is theater that, on past evidence, squeezes ordinary Iranians while hardliners adapt through smuggling and crypto. Refusing to say whether Chinese banks are actually in the crosshairs, they argue, exposes the bluff — and any move to police Hormuz shipping courts an oil shock that lands on U.S. drivers. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Hawks and free-marketeers (a WSJ-editorial / National Review-style view) applaud finally choking off Iran’s oil-and-money lifelines and extending secondary sanctions to the crypto and gold channels Tehran uses to evade them. The libertarian caution (Reason-style) stands: open-ended secondary sanctions strain allies and third countries, and threats to board or seize shipping risk dragging the U.S. into a Gulf shooting war. (Directional.)

Trade · U.S.–Canada

Trump escalates the Canada trade war, accusing Ottawa of ‘ripping off’ the U.S. as 50% tariffs bite and Carney readies retaliation

+

With U.S.–Canada trade talks having collapsed just before a Friday-midnight deadline, President Trump used Monday to sharpen the fight, accusing Canada of “ripping off the United States for years” and reprising his line that Canada “wants the benefits” of being a U.S. state. Fresh 50% duties took effect at midnight Friday on roughly $20 billion of Canadian products — including dairy, alcohol, cement and hockey equipment — and Trump has threatened to invoke Section 338 of the 1930 Tariff Act to pile on additional 50% duties. U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said Canada made last-minute demands that sank the deal.

Prime Minister Mark Carney called the new tariffs “a miscalculation,” said Washington “asked too much” and “offered too little,” and announced Canadian retaliatory tariffs beginning Sept. 8 targeting U.S. steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, and electronics. The breakdown is widely read as a test of the limits of Trump’s pressure-first trade strategy against a close ally and NATO partner. (Facts from NPR, NBC News, Axios, BBC and CBC.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left casts this as self-inflicted economic damage dressed as toughness: a needless trade war with a friendly neighbor that raises prices on American consumers and manufacturers, alienates a NATO ally, and delivers retaliation that will hit U.S. farmers and steelworkers — all in service of Trump’s annexation taunts. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Here much of the mainstream right breaks with Trump. The WSJ editorial board calls it “the dumbest trade war” that just “got dumber,” and National Review warns the tariffs and 51st-state talk make no economic or political sense and menace the sovereignty of a NATO ally. Reason argues the Section 338 tariffs are an illegal usurpation of Congress’s authority, since that provision was superseded by 1962 and 1974 trade law. A pro-Trump minority still frames it as leverage to win a better deal. (Directional.)

Middle East · Turkey–Israel

Turkey moves to have Interpol arrest Netanyahu, seeking ‘red notices’ over the Gaza aid-flotilla raid

+

Turkey has asked Interpol to issue “red notices” for Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and an Israeli army officer, part of an Istanbul case against 35 people over the interception of the Global Sumud Flotilla — a civilian convoy carrying humanitarian aid toward Gaza that Israeli forces stopped in international waters in mid-May, detaining some 429 activists from roughly 40 countries before deporting most to Istanbul. The Turkish charges include genocide, crimes against humanity, torture, property damage and hijacking. A red notice is a request that police worldwide locate and provisionally arrest a person pending possible extradition; Interpol screens such requests before deciding whether to issue one, and even if issued it obliges no country to act.

The step is largely symbolic in practical terms — Netanyahu already faces a November 2024 International Criminal Court arrest warrant over the Gaza war — but it sharpens the Turkey–Israel rupture at a fraught moment. It comes amid rising friction over Syria, where Ankara and Israel are jockeying for influence and where an Israeli strike on an Idlib airbase last week briefly froze the U.S.-mediated de-escalation track. The move also keeps international legal pressure on Israel’s Gaza conduct in the headlines, even as its real-world effect on Netanyahu’s travel is likely marginal. (Facts from The National, Times of Israel, Haaretz and Turkish Minute.)

Sources: The National, The Times of Israel, Haaretz, Turkish Minute
Africa · DR Congo

DR Congo and the M23-led alliance agree a roadmap for peace talks after five days of Swiss-brokered negotiations

+

The Democratic Republic of the Congo and the AFC/M23 rebel alliance have agreed on a “roadmap setting out sequenced steps and timelines for negotiations,” according to a joint statement following five days of talks in Switzerland (Aug. 17–21) that also drew in the United States, Qatar, Togo, Switzerland and the African Union Commission. The roadmap operationalizes a framework agreement the two sides signed in Doha last November. Both parties committed to a standardized method for reporting ceasefire violations and to a mechanism for resolving disputes over implementing the Doha deal; a first ceasefire-verification mission was due to reach Minembwe, in DRC’s South Kivu province, Monday.

The agreement is a modest but real step in a conflict that has displaced hundreds of thousands and drawn accusations of Rwandan backing for M23. It layers onto a parallel U.S.-brokered track between Kinshasa and Kigali, reflecting Washington’s and Doha’s converging interest in stabilizing the mineral-rich east. The caveat is familiar: fighting has not stopped — M23 alleges recent Congolese drone strikes with civilian casualties — and previous ceasefires in eastern Congo have repeatedly unraveled, so the roadmap’s value will hinge on whether the verification machinery actually holds. (Facts from Al Jazeera, Euronews, France 24 and Oman Observer.)

Sources: Al Jazeera, Euronews, France 24, Oman Observer

National Security · Iran

Bessent unveils Trump’s ‘economic D-Day’ sanctions on Iran, leaning on China and other oil buyers, as Tehran threatens to seize ships in the Strait of Hormuz

+

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent held a Monday, Aug. 24 news conference (reported for around 2 p.m. ET) to detail what he has called “the single greatest financial offensive ever marshalled against an adversary” — a sweeping sanctions package aimed at severing Iran’s remaining foreign-trade and financial lifelines. Previewing the move in a Financial Times op-ed, Bessent wrote, “Now we are entering the endgame. At dawn begins an economic D-Day,” and told CNBC the measures would be “the toughest sanctions in history,” warning foreign governments and firms, “you are either with us or against us.” The package leans heavily on secondary sanctions against third countries and entities that keep buying Iranian oil or moving Iranian money — above all China, which takes the bulk of Iran’s shipped crude. Asked whether Chinese banks could be targeted, Bessent said such conversations were “best to have in private.” The rollout follows the collapse of the June 17 US–Iran memorandum of understanding, which lapsed in mid-August, and comes with the broader conflict now in roughly its sixth month.

Tehran responded with defiance. Iran’s Persian Gulf transit authority warned that vessels violating its rules in the Strait of Hormuz could face “fines, seizure, or confiscation,” and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi dismissed the coming measures as a “desperate” ploy; a senior security official argued the pivot to economic warfare proves the US “failed militarily.” President Masoud Pezeshkian struck a softer note, saying Iran “cannot continue with war forever” and defending the June MOU — a sign of a split within Tehran’s leadership. Note: outlets agree on the timing and thrust of Bessent’s presser, but the specific designated entities, exemptions, enforcement dates and any direct action against major Chinese banks were not confirmed ahead of the announcement, and the exact 2 p.m. ET time appeared in some reports but not all. (Facts from CNBC, CNN, NBC News and NPR; some details vary by source.)

A “with us or against us” ultimatum aimed at allies and China, they argue, is coercion that could fracture coalitions and risk a Hormuz shipping crisis and oil-price spike that lands on American consumers — economic warfare as a substitute for the diplomatic deal the administration let lapse. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Hawkish and free-market conservatives (a WSJ-editorial / National Review-style view) broadly welcome hard leverage on Tehran — cutting off oil revenue and money flows as the right alternative to a weak deal, and applauding the willingness to hit Chinese buyers with secondary sanctions. A libertarian counter-current (Reason-style) warns that blockade-style enforcement and threats to board or seize shipping risk pulling the US into a Gulf shooting war and that sweeping secondary sanctions strain ties with allied trading partners. (Directional.)

Economy · Fed Independence

Trump’s renewed bid to oust Fed Governor Lisa Cook nears a decision point as her window to contest the allegations closes this week

+

The clock is running on President Trump’s second attempt to remove Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook. In a letter reported Aug. 5, the White House notified Cook that Trump was “considering” her removal and gave her 21 days — closing around Aug. 26 — to contest allegations, referred by Federal Housing Finance Agency director Bill Pulte, that she committed mortgage fraud in 2021 by listing two homes (in Ann Arbor, Mich., and Atlanta) as her “primary residence.” Cook denies wrongdoing and has never been criminally charged; her lawyers Abbe Lowell and Norm Eisen call the allegations “as baseless now as they were a year ago.” The renewed effort was structured to satisfy the Supreme Court’s late-June 5–4 ruling in Trump v. Cook, which blocked Trump’s first attempt because Cook had not received notice and a chance to respond — with Chief Justice Roberts noting nothing forbade trying again with proper process.

The dispute is widely framed as the most direct test yet of Federal Reserve independence: governors serve 14-year terms specifically to insulate monetary policy from political pressure, and a president’s ability to remove one over disputed, uncharged allegations would mark a significant shift. Note on freshness: this is a simmering, high-salience thread rather than a story that broke in the last 24 hours — the last hard development was the early-August letter, and the near-term catalyst is the roughly Aug. 26 response deadline and any subsequent removal action or court filing. Reporting also notes financial records that appear to complicate the administration’s fraud claims; the underlying facts remain contested. (Facts from The Washington Post (partial — paywalled), NPR, SCOTUSblog and Fortune.)

Science · Astrophysics

A captured star’s rapid spin can explain why some black holes’ repeating flares fade with each pass, a Syracuse-led team argues in The Astrophysical Journal

+

Astronomers studying “repeating partial tidal disruption events” — cases where a star repeatedly skims a supermassive black hole, is partially shredded, survives and flares each time — have proposed an answer to a nagging puzzle. Of roughly ten known repeating systems, about four produce flares that grow progressively dimmer with each orbit, a trend prior models could not reproduce. The new work argues the dimming is recovered if the star was already spinning very rapidly before capture, invoking the “Hills mechanism”: a tight, tidally locked binary is torn apart by the black hole, one star ejected and the other captured already rotating fast — which explains both the tight orbit and the fading flares.

The study, “The Role of Stellar Spin in Repeating Partial Tidal Disruption Events,” was led by Syracuse University doctoral student Ananya Bandopadhyay with associate professor Eric Coughlin and collaborators, and appears in the peer-reviewed Astrophysical Journal (release circulated Aug. 22, 2026). It is a theoretical/modeling result offering a candidate explanation, not a direct observation of stellar spin. (Facts from ScienceDaily, EurekAlert/Syracuse University and the arXiv preprint.)

Sources: ScienceDaily, EurekAlert (Syracuse), Syracuse University, arXiv preprint
Technology · AI in Biology

A UC San Diego team uses machine learning to decode the DNA ‘initiator,’ the on-switch present in about 60% of human genes

+

Researchers in James T. Kadonaga’s lab at UC San Diego report that AI models, trained by evaluating on the order of 500,000 DNA sequence variants via high-throughput sequencing, learned the base-sequence pattern of the “initiator” — the core-promoter element that marks where a gene’s instructions first begin to be expressed. The models can predict, for the first time, the presence or absence of an initiator across human genes, and found it in roughly 60% of them. The authors frame the advance as a step toward predicting the effects of harmful mutations — for instance, scanning for changes that could contribute to diseases such as cancer — and toward decoding the broader grammar of gene regulation.

The paper — “Machine learning analysis of the human initiator region reveals key features of different types of core promoters” — was published in the peer-reviewed journal Genes & Development, with the public release circulating Aug. 23, 2026.
