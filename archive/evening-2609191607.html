Press Freedom · First Amendment

Trump bars CNN, MS NOW and Politico from the White House — and reporters are turned away at the gate

+

In a Truth Social post Friday afternoon, Sept. 18, President Trump said he is “banning” CNN, MS NOW (the former MSNBC) and Politico from the White House, writing that the outlets “shouldn’t be able to constantly write or report FICTION and LIES” about him and his administration. He floated extending the move to The New York Times and The Washington Post, calling both “crummy.”

By Saturday, Sept. 19, the order had teeth: reporters from the three outlets said they were denied access to White House grounds, with MS NOW’s Akayla Gardner reporting on air that a Secret Service officer asked her to hand over a badge that had been “disabled.” Leading First Amendment organizations called any such ban unconstitutional and predicted the courts would reject it — echoing a 2018 case in which a judge ordered CNN’s Jim Acosta’s press pass restored. (The announcement and the Saturday denials are consistent across Tier-1 outlets; the legal question is unresolved and headed for court.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left calls it a naked authoritarian move: punishing news organizations for coverage the president dislikes is textbook viewpoint discrimination and a direct assault on the First Amendment. The fear is less about badge logistics than the precedent — a government that can pick which journalists cover it can manufacture its own reality. Courts, they expect, will strike it down. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The response splits the right. Some populist voices cheer a long-promised reckoning with outlets they see as reflexively hostile. But principled conservatives and libertarians (National Review, The Dispatch, Reason’s free-speech instinct) warn the ban is plainly unconstitutional and hands a future Democratic president the same weapon. The Bulwark (anti-Trump right, an intra-right dissent) casts it as strongman theater dressed up as media criticism. (Directional.)

Sources: CNN, CNBC, NBC News, NPR, Axios, Al Jazeera
Congress · Shutdown Countdown

Both stopgaps fail in the Senate as the House leaves town, pushing the odds toward an Oct. 1 shutdown

+

The funding fight sharpened. The ACA subsidies remain the knot: Democrats will not fund the government without an extension, Republicans will not attach one to a stopgap, and the calendar is now the adversary. (The failed votes are the day’s new development; whether a deal materializes before Oct. 1 is unresolved.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left frames the standoff as worth it: letting the ACA credits lapse means steep 2026 premium hikes for millions, so holding firm is protecting families, not hostage-taking. That the House left town rather than negotiate, they argue, tells you which party is treating a shutdown as acceptable collateral. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right says Republicans offered the obvious off-ramp — a clean CR at existing levels — and Democrats blew it up to force a partisan health-care demand, owning any shutdown that follows. Fiscal conservatives (Reason’s bent) remain wary of any stopgap that cements elevated spending on autopilot. (Directional.)

NATO · U.S. Force Posture

Pentagon is said to weigh pulling up to 40,000 troops from Europe — the biggest drawdown since the Cold War

+

Reporting on Sept. 18 indicated the Pentagon is weighing a withdrawal of as many as 40,000 U.S. troops from Europe — what would be the largest reduction of the American presence on the continent since the Cold War. It builds on last autumn’s decision to scale back forces on NATO’s eastern flank, including rotational troops in Romania, where roughly a thousand American soldiers remain. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has framed the shifts as a “deliberate process” to rebalance posture; Trump has described it as moving people around rather than a net cut, and the Pentagon denies any wholesale American exit from Europe.

The prospect has rattled allies and drawn objections from Republican defense hawks who chair the Armed Services committees, coming as Poland’s government warns of possible Russian provocations against Ukraine’s backers. It sits awkwardly beside the just-signed Graham sanctions law: Washington is tightening the economic screws on Moscow while signaling a lighter military footprint on the frontier facing it. (The 40,000 figure is attributed to reporting on internal deliberations, not a finalized order; officials stress no decision is set.)

Sources: Eastern Herald, Washington Post, CNN, Atlantic Council, Fox News

Foreign Policy · Sanctions Law

Trump signs the Graham Russia–Iran sanctions bill into law, gaining new power to hit buyers of Russian oil

+

On Friday, Sept. 18, President Trump signed H.R. 5334, the “Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026,” completing the fast passage of a bill named for the late South Carolina senator, who died in July 2026. It cleared the Senate 86–11 in August and the House 262–159 on Sept. 16. The law authorizes tariffs of up to 100% on the largest purchasers of Russian crude and gas — China and India chief among them — sanctions Russian officials, banks and the “shadow fleet” of tankers, and extends the Iran Sanctions Act by five years.

The White House framed the signing as handing the president fresh leverage to press Moscow toward ending the war in Ukraine and to keep economic pressure on Tehran during the ongoing US–Iran war. The catch remains the drafting: the tariff authority is discretionary — “may,” not “shall” — so whether Trump actually wields it against Beijing and New Delhi is now entirely his call. (The signing and vote counts are consistent across Tier-1 outlets; the practical effect turns on enforcement choices not yet made.)

The Left-Leaning Take

Directional-left commentary echoes the Democratic leadership’s “no”: the law doesn’t require Trump to sanction anyone, is riddled with discretionary loopholes, and mainly gifts the White House sweeping tariff powers that could raise Americans’ costs while doing little concrete for Ukraine. The worry is the blank check, not sympathy for Putin. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

National Review backs it as “the best option on the table” to squeeze Russia’s economy and honor Graham. Libertarian Reason (with Cato voices) is wary of the vast, ill-defined tariff authority as bad law and another Article I giveaway. The Bulwark (anti-Trump right, an intra-right dissent) mocks Republicans for voluntarily handing Trump a “tariff nuke.” (Directional.)

Sources: RFE/RL, CBS News, The National, US News, White House
Congress · Government Funding

Dueling stopgaps and an ACA-subsidy standoff as the Sept. 30 shutdown deadline nears

+

With current federal funding set to lapse at the end of Sept. 30, the two parties have staked out competing stopgaps. House Republicans unveiled a “clean” continuing resolution on Sept. 16 to keep the government open at existing spending levels through Nov. 21, free of policy riders. A day later, House and Senate Democrats countered with a CR running to Oct. 31 that would permanently extend the expiring Affordable Care Act premium tax credits, roll back Medicaid cuts from the 2025 reconciliation law, restore rescinded funds, and add security money for lawmakers. On Sept. 18 the Senate agreed to take up both proposals, each needing 60 votes.

The math is complicated by Speaker Mike Johnson’s decision to cut the House schedule short and send members home early to campaign, leaving a narrow window to reconcile the two bills before the deadline. Democrats, buoyed by favorable post-Labor Day battleground polling, see leverage in the ACA fight; Republicans insist a shutdown would be the minority’s doing. (The competing texts and the Sept. 18 procedural step are well reported; whether either passes — or a shutdown begins Oct. 1 — is unresolved.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left frames it as a fight to stop a health-cost cliff: without an ACA-subsidy extension, millions face steep 2026 premium hikes, so tying it to funding is responsible, not hostage-taking. A “clean” CR, they argue, simply papers over a crisis Republicans would rather not vote on before the midterms. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right calls the GOP bill the grown-up option — keep the lights on at current levels, then legislate ACA policy separately — and casts the Democratic demands as loading a stopgap with partisan riders and daring a shutdown. Fiscal conservatives (Reason’s instinct) are uneasy with any “clean” extension that locks in elevated spending. (Directional.)

Science · Cell Biology

Scientists find cells that ‘cheat death’ — starting to self-destruct, then reviving to rebuild tissue

+

Researchers at Israel’s Weizmann Institute of Science have identified a population of cells that begin the process of programmed cell death (apoptosis), survive it, and then help rapidly rebuild damaged tissue. Dubbed DARE cells, they multiplied quickly and replenished nearly half of the damaged tissue within 48 hours in experiments on fruit-fly larvae, and their descendants proved markedly more resistant to future injury.

The work, published in Nature Communications and covered in a Sept. 17 writeup, extends the concept of anastasis — cells recovering from the brink of death — and helps explain “compensatory proliferation,” in which surviving cells multiply to replace those lost. The double edge: the same survival trick that could improve healing may also help some cancers return after treatment. (A single study, so far demonstrated in an insect model.)

Sources: ScienceDaily, Nature Communications (PubMed), Vanguard
Science · Planetary Astronomy

A giant ten-sided ‘decagon’ has appeared around Saturn’s south pole

+

Astronomers have documented a vast ten-sided atmospheric pattern wrapped around Saturn’s south pole — a counterpart to the planet’s long-famous northern hexagon. Reported in Science Advances and tracked with the Hubble Space Telescope, the decagon appears to be an enormous, evolving wave within one of Saturn’s polar jet streams, likely a standing wave set up by its polar vortex.

The shape is genuinely new: NASA’s Cassini orbiter, which studied Saturn from 2004 to 2017, saw no sign of it, and because the south pole was tilted away from Earth between 2012 and 2023, researchers suspect the feature formed sometime in that window and is still strengthening. It was first noticed as a faint, wavy band in 2024 — including by amateur astronomers — before ground-based and Hubble data confirmed the ten-sided structure. (Interpretation of the wave dynamics is ongoing.)

Sources: NASA Science, Smithsonian, ScienceDaily
Energy · Grid & Storage

US batteries keep smashing records — and are reshaping when clean power reaches the grid

+

The US grid-battery boom keeps setting records. Industry data show the country installed about 20.2 GWh of battery storage in the second quarter of 2026 — its largest single quarter ever — putting 2026 on track for roughly 71 GWh, a 20% jump over last year, with Arizona alone adding a record 6.2 GWh. In the first quarter, solar and storage together made up about 91% of all new grid capacity, the highest share the pairing has ever recorded.

The point of all that storage is timing: it shifts abundant midday solar into the evening peak. California’s battery fleet set a discharge record of nearly 13 GW this summer — more power than New York City draws on a hot day — and in May, solar out-generated coal nationwide for the first time. The binding constraint on a cleaner grid, analysts increasingly say, is less about generation than about deploying storage and transmission fast enough. (Figures are deployment tallies and instantaneous records from grid operators and industry trackers.)
