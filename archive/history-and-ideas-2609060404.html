History & Archaeology · Old Kingdom Egypt

A 4,000-year-old tomb at Saqqara is found with its carved ‘false door’ still standing in place and the ritual offering kit laid out before it, letting archaeologists read the choreography of Egyptian funerary rites directly

+

An Egyptian mission excavating Sector A of the Bubasteum at the Saqqara necropolis has uncovered the tomb of Sekhentiu-Ptah, a senior official of the Old Kingdom (c. 2700–2200 BCE), and the find was carried across the science desks this week (Smithsonian’s Smart News, Sept. 4; Archaeology Magazine, Sept. 1; ARTnews). What makes it more than another dig report is preservation and position: the funerary chapel’s carved limestone “false door” — the symbolic threshold through which the dead were believed to pass to receive offerings from the living — was found still standing in its original location, with a complete alabaster ritual kit laid out in front of it (an offering table and disc, a purification basin, a jug). The inscriptions give not just a name but a career: Sekhentiu-Ptah was Royal Chamberlain, “Overseer of All the King’s Works,” Overseer of Royal Documents, “Keeper of the Secrets of Royal Commands,” Priest of the goddess Maat (truth and cosmic order), and Overseer of Scribes.

Why it’s interesting: in-situ finds like this are rare and disproportionately informative. Because nothing had been moved, archaeologists can read the choreography of Old Kingdom funerary ritual directly — where the offering apparatus sat relative to the door, how the living were meant to commune with the dead across that stone boundary. The tomb also became a palimpsest: alongside the Old Kingdom chapel, the mission found much later intrusive burials — painted cartonnage, three sealed sarcophagi, and more than 100 faience ushabti figurines, the little “servant” statuettes meant to labor for the deceased in the afterlife — showing the same sacred ground reused across many centuries. It is a compact lesson in how Egyptians thought about death, bureaucracy, and the permanence of a name.

Sources: Smithsonian Magazine, Archaeology Magazine, ARTnews
Sociology of Religion · Social Science

A quarter-century after 9/11, American suspicion of Muslims has hardened — even as Muslim America has grown, integrated and risen to public office — and the gap between perception and reality is the real story

+

Timed to the 25th anniversary of the attacks, the Pew Research Center published a data essay, “25 Years After 9/11, a Portrait of Muslim Americans,” on September 3, 2026. Its central, counterintuitive finding: by several measures Americans hold more negative views of Muslims now than they did in the raw aftermath of 9/11. In March 2002, six months after the attacks, 25% of U.S. adults said Islam is more likely than other religions to encourage violence; today 51% say so. In a spring 2026 survey, 43% said Muslims are less patriotic than other Americans and 42% said Muslim Americans have a negative impact on the country — and a majority of Muslim respondents themselves reported “a lot” of discrimination against Muslims and unfair media portrayals.

Why it matters: the essay sets that souring public opinion against a story of striking Muslim-American growth and integration, which makes the gap between perception and reality the real subject. The U.S. Muslim population has risen to roughly 5.5 million (from about 2.4 million in 2007); there are now four Muslim members of Congress with a fifth about to be seated, versus zero in 2001; and Zohran Mamdani was inaugurated this year as the first Muslim mayor of New York — the city where the towers fell. It is a rich case study in how a founding national trauma can calcify into durable prejudice even as the group in question becomes more numerous, more civically embedded, and more visible in public life — a sobering artifact for a week of 9/11 retrospectives.

Sources: Pew Research Center
Moral Philosophy · History of Ideas

Is there one morality or many? Susan Neiman revisits the oldest fault line in ethics — the plain fact that moral norms vary across cultures, and the stubborn intuition that some things really are better than others

+

In the new New York Review of Books (Sept. 24, 2026 issue, posted online in early September), the philosopher Susan Neiman — author of Evil in Modern Thought and Moral Clarity, and director of the Einstein Forum — reviews Steven Lukes’s The Diversity of Morals (Princeton University Press) under the title “Is Morality Universal?” Lukes, equally at home in philosophy and the social sciences, sets himself the hard task of reconciling two things that seem to pull apart: the philosophical case that there is a single morality binding on everyone, and the empirical evidence from anthropology, sociology and psychology that moral norms vary wildly across cultures. His worry, which Neiman shares, is the slide from the plain fact of moral diversity into a corrosive relativism — the idea that because norms differ, none can be judged better than another.

Why it’s interesting: the essay is a lucid tour through the deepest fault line in ethics, and it refuses the easy exits. Lukes leans on Rawls to argue that “the regulative ideal of an objective, universalizing perspective is inescapable” — that we cannot actually think morally without reaching for standards meant to hold beyond our own tribe — while still taking cultural difference seriously rather than explaining it away. Neiman highlights his use of Karl Jaspers’s “Axial Age” (roughly 800–200 BCE), when civilizations as far-flung as Persia, India, China, Greece and the Levant independently arrived at strikingly similar moral ideas — evidence, perhaps, that moral convergence is not a Western imposition but something recurrent in human history. In an era of loud cultural relativism and equally loud absolutism, it is a bracing, humane defense of the idea that some things really are better than others — without flattening the world’s genuine differences.

Sources: The New York Review of Books
