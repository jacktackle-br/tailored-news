Elections · New Today

DHS Secretary Mullin threatens state election officials with prison and loss of federal aid, a day after Trump’s primetime claim that China stole 220 million voter files

+

On Friday, July 17, Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin threatened to strip states of federal election-related aid and to pursue criminal penalties against state election officials who refuse to run their voter rolls through a DHS citizenship-verification database. Mullin said officials could face fines and “prison time,” citing statutes carrying penalties up to five years and $250,000, and sent letters to secretaries of state; DHS claimed the database flagged roughly 250,000 alleged noncitizens registered in California, New Jersey, Nevada and Pennsylvania — a figure not independently verified. The Justice Department is separately suing more than two dozen states to force release of full, unredacted voter rolls, efforts courts have repeatedly blocked. Twenty-four Democratic governors issued a joint statement vowing to “fight back against… any and all unlawful attacks.”

The moves followed Trump’s roughly 25-minute primetime White House address Thursday, July 16, in which he claimed newly declassified documents showed “the largest compromise of election data in history,” alleging China illicitly obtained 220 million U.S. voter files and sought to influence the 2020 election. He tied the claims to a demand that the Senate pass the SAVE America Act — photo ID plus documentary proof of citizenship to register — which Majority Leader John Thune says lacks the votes. Fact-checkers (FactCheck.org, CBS, Votebeat, CNN) noted voter-registration data is largely public, that a declassified 2021 intelligence assessment found China did not alter votes, and that the released documents were heavily redacted and described efforts to obtain, not manipulate, records. The Chinese Embassy denied interference.

The Left-Leaning Take

Mother Jones voting correspondent Ari Berman framed the speech as an effort to “lay groundwork” for federal interference in the November midterms while U.S. elections remain secure and extensively audited. The broader progressive read across coverage: Trump is manufacturing a foreign-threat pretext to justify voter suppression and to pre-delegitimize a midterm his party is expected to struggle in, with the DHS prison threat and the DOJ voter-roll lawsuits cast as coercion of the nonpartisan officials who actually run elections. (No standalone Guardian/Slate/Vox op-ed retrievable in-window — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Notably, the Wall Street Journal editorial board tore into Trump on Friday, calling the SAVE Act fixation a “political backfire” — arguing voter ID is popular but Trump’s approach goes far beyond it, would render most driver’s licenses insufficient to register, and that relitigating a six-year-old election already cost the GOP two Georgia Senate seats; the board urged Republicans to campaign on the future instead. The strongest sympathetic frame, reflected in the White House’s messaging, backs the underlying citizenship-verification policy while staying clear of the China-fraud conspiracy framing. (National Review / Dispatch dedicated pieces not retrievable in-window — provisional; the intra-right split is itself the story.)

Sources: CNN, CBS News, Votebeat, FactCheck.org, Democracy Docket, WSJ editorial via Raw Story
Press Freedom · New Today

Trump demands the FCC revoke ABC and NBC broadcast licenses after the networks declined to carry his election speech live; an FCC commissioner calls the threat unconstitutional

+

On July 17, Trump called for ABC (Disney) and NBC (Comcast) to lose their broadcast licenses after both declined to preempt regular programming for Thursday’s address, carrying it only on their streaming services; CBS and MS NOW cut away mid-speech, while Fox News aired it in full. Trump wrote that “fraud like this should mean a revocation of their licenses” and accused the networks of being “part of a plot.” Legal experts and the FCC’s sole Democratic commissioner said the demand is unconstitutional: networks themselves do not hold broadcast licenses — their local affiliates do — and an editorial decision to skip a political speech is First Amendment–protected, with the FCC lacking authority to punish it. Networks have declined to carry presidential addresses before (Biden 2022, Obama 2014).

The threat lands atop an ongoing pressure campaign by Trump-allied FCC Chairman Brendan Carr, including an order forcing early license renewal for ABC’s eight owned-and-operated stations, which ABC is challenging as “an extraordinary demonstration of power and coercion.” Coverage notes the networks’ caution around unverified election claims stems partly from Fox’s $787 million Dominion defamation settlement.

The Iran War · Escalation

The U.S. hits Iran for a seventh straight night, knocking out Gulf desalination plants, as Tehran threatens a “full-scale offensive” and Brent tops $88

+

U.S. Central Command completed a seventh consecutive night of strikes into early Saturday, July 18, targeting coastal areas near the Persian Gulf and Strait of Hormuz plus inland transport infrastructure. Strikes late Friday hit the port city of Bandar Abbas and the nearby islands of Qeshm and Larak; one U.S. strike damaged a desalination plant in Hormozgan province, leaving 20 villages without water, per the deputy governor. A prior wave had destroyed six bridges in Hormozgan (Iranian state media: at least eight killed, 20 injured — not independently verified) and an IRGC surveillance tower at Chabahar that CENTCOM said Iran used to target commercial shipping. Iran retaliated against U.S. Gulf allies: Kuwait said Saturday it intercepted Iranian missiles and drones and that a desalination plant was struck and caught fire — the second such attack in two days on a country that draws about 90% of its drinking water from desalination.

Mohsen Rezaei, a senior IRGC figure and adviser to Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei, warned that if U.S. strikes continue “another two or three days,” Iran will move to “full-scale offensive operations” and “no political border will be safe.” Trump is reportedly weighing a broader campaign but has not decided. Markets and shipping reflect the crisis: Brent crude rose about 4.6% Friday to close near $88.10 a barrel, its highest in a month; Hormuz transits fell to roughly eight ships Thursday, from about 100 a day pre-war, with the U.N. reporting shipping near a standstill and some 6,000 seafarers stranded and at least nine vessels attacked since July 6. July 17 marked the halfway point of the 60-day window from the collapsed June 17 U.S.–Iran memorandum of understanding, which Trump declared “over” on July 8. Outlets differ on which Gulf allies were targeted (variously Kuwait, Bahrain, Oman, Jordan and Saudi Arabia) and cite Brent at roughly $87–88.

Sources: NPR, CNN, CBS News, Al Jazeera, The Defense Post, UN News, CNBC
Europe · Ukraine

Rare wartime protests spread to 16-plus Ukrainian cities after Zelensky ousts popular defense minister Fedorov; parliament installs Naftogaz chief Koretskyi as PM

+

President Volodymyr Zelensky’s removal of Defense Minister Mykhailo Fedorov — barely six months in the job and an architect of Ukraine’s drone program — has triggered the largest domestic protests of the war. The upheaval unfolded alongside the formal completion of the reshuffle: on July 16 parliament appointed former Naftogaz chief Serhii Koretskyi as prime minister (289 votes) and approved his cabinet (264 votes), succeeding Yulia Svyrydenko. All of this plays out under continued Russian bombardment even as Ukraine claims front-line gains. Reporting diverges on Fedorov’s successor at Defense: earlier accounts pointed to Interior Minister Ihor Klymenko, while later reporting says an intelligence official was named acting minister; the succession is not yet settled in the coverage.

Middle East · Gaza

Israeli strikes kill 14 Palestinians in a day — the deadliest since the October 2025 truce — including a drone attack on a funeral procession in Nuseirat

+

Israeli forces killed 14 Palestinians and wounded at least 37 across Gaza on Friday, July 17, in what monitors describe as the most intense attacks since the ceasefire took effect October 10, 2025. The deadliest strike hit the Nuseirat refugee camp in central Gaza, where a drone struck a crowd as a funeral procession passed; a local hospital reported seven to eight killed and roughly 20 to 22 wounded. Other strikes hit a makeshift shelter for displaced families west of the camp (one killed, three injured), an apartment building in Gaza City (one killed), and Beit Lahia in the north, where a drone killed a 52-year-old woman. Israel says its operations target militants; Palestinian officials characterize them as continued ceasefire violations.

Gaza’s Health Ministry says Israeli actions have killed 1,127 Palestinians and injured 3,643 since the truce began — figures that are not independently verified and that Israel has not corroborated. The escalation deepens warnings, aired at the U.N. this week, that the deal remains unfulfilled more than nine months on. The funeral-strike toll is cited variously as seven killed / 22 wounded (AP-sourced) and eight killed / 20 wounded (Anadolu, Al Jazeera).

Space · Astronomy

Two teams independently image Beta Pictoris d — the faintest exoplanet ever photographed from Earth — a gas giant hidden in a decade of archival data

+

A third planet in the well-studied Beta Pictoris system, 63 light-years away, has been confirmed by two independent methods: ground-based infrared imaging with the European Southern Observatory’s Very Large Telescope, and molecular spectroscopy with NASA’s James Webb Space Telescope, with papers in The Astrophysical Journal Letters announced July 15–16. Beta Pictoris d is about 2.4 Jupiter masses and roughly 100 times fainter than the previously known Beta Pictoris b — among the lightest and faintest worlds ever directly imaged from the ground.

Strikingly, the planet had been lurking undetected in more than ten years of archival VLT observations before the new analysis pulled it out, a demonstration of how much signal remains buried in existing datasets. Beta Pictoris, a young system still shaped by a bright debris disk, has long been a natural laboratory for planet formation; adding a third confirmed world sharpens that picture. (The announcement sits just outside the strict 24-hour window but was still circulating July 17–18.)

Sources: Phys.org, Sci.News, University of Edinburgh
Climate & Energy · Extreme Heat

Europe’s record-shattering summer forces nuclear output cuts and fuels deadly wildfires as a fresh heat dome strains the grid

+

A fresh heat dome over Western Europe — following what researchers have called the continent’s worst heatwave on record in late June — is driving both wildfire and power-system stress in mid-July 2026. Roughly 500 firefighters battled a blaze near Fontainebleau, about 64 km south of Paris; France has now burned some 320 km² this year, roughly double the year-ago pace, while Spain fought one of its deadliest wildfires yet, with at least 13 dead across about 70 km² near Almería.

On the grid, Électricité de France warned it may curtail output at the Chooz nuclear plant because of low Meuse River cooling-water levels, with similar river-temperature risks flagged on the Rhône and Garonne; power prices are forecast to climb as cooling demand spikes. World Weather Attribution has tied the intensifying heat directly to fossil-fuel-driven warming — a live example of climate change feeding back into the reliability of a low-carbon power system that depends on rivers for cooling.
