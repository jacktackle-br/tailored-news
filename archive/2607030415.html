Jobs Report

Hiring stalls: U.S. adds just 57,000 jobs in June as the labor force shrinks

+

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported July 2 that nonfarm payrolls grew by only 57,000 in June — roughly half the consensus forecast of around 110,000–115,000. The unemployment rate ticked down to 4.2% from 4.3%, but economists across outlets attributed the drop to a shrinking labor force rather than hiring strength: participation fell 0.3 point to 61.5%, a roughly five-year low, alongside a ~720,000 decline in the labor force. BLS also revised April and May down by a combined 74,000 jobs. Health care led gains (~46,000) while leisure and hospitality shed about 61,000; average hourly earnings rose 3.5% year-over-year, trailing inflation now running near a three-year high.

The framing split cleanly: the White House and Acting Labor Secretary Sonderling called the market steady and on an "upward trajectory," while most economists and congressional Democrats called the report weak. The soft numbers ease pressure for a rate hike, but new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh — a Trump appointee — has leaned hawkish given high inflation, holding rates at 3.50%–3.75% at his first meeting and setting up a Fed-versus-White-House storyline as Trump presses publicly for cuts.

The Left-Leaning Take

The program is squeezing workers, not delivering a boom (Common Dreams, Slate's recurring economy coverage): sweeping tariffs, the Iran war's inflationary spike, and mass deportations pulling workers out of the count are the real story. On this read, the falling participation rate isn't good news — it's discouraged and deported workers vanishing from the labor force while wages lag prices.

The Right-Leaning Take

Underlying resilience, not a crisis (center-right framing): the falling headline unemployment rate and steady wage growth signal a labor market that's cooling, not cracking, and the softness strengthens the case against a Fed rate hike and for the pro-growth, deregulatory agenda. The institutional caveat on the right is that Warsh should resist Trump's pressure and keep prioritizing inflation.

Sources: CNBC, Washington Post, BLS, Axios, Detroit News
Congress

Trump's SAVE Act stalls again in the Senate as he holds a housing law hostage

+

President Trump's signature election-overhaul bill — the SAVE (Safeguard American Voter Eligibility) Act, which would require documentary proof of citizenship to register and photo ID to vote — again failed to advance in the Senate, blocked as an amendment when four Republicans (Collins, Murkowski, McConnell, Tillis) joined Democrats. The House passed its version in February, but the bill lacks the 60 votes to beat a filibuster. Trump has declared it his "No. 1 priority," said it "supersedes everything else," and pressed Majority Leader John Thune to eliminate or circumvent the filibuster — even to fire the Senate parliamentarian. Thune says Republicans have neither the votes to "nuke the filibuster" nor 10 Democrats to pass it.

The standoff has jammed the agenda: the House left early for its July 4 recess (both chambers out until ~July 13) after hardliners blocked action — including on the must-pass defense bill — over demands to attach SAVE. Trump also abruptly canceled his signing of the bipartisan 21st Century Road to Housing Act, the largest housing-affordability bill in decades, calling other legislation a "yawn" until SAVE passes while conceding passage is "probably not going to happen." Outlets stress the factual backdrop: documented noncitizen voting is exceedingly rare, while the Brennan Center estimates ~21 million eligible citizens lack ready access to the required documents.

The Left-Leaning Take

Voter suppression dressed as fraud prevention (Mother Jones): "the most restrictive voting bill" Congress has taken up would disenfranchise millions of eligible citizens — low-income voters, minorities, and married women who changed their names — to solve a problem that barely exists. Holding a bipartisan housing law hostage, they argue, shows a willingness to sabotage governance for an anti-democratic power grab ahead of the midterms.

The Right-Leaning Take

Split on tactics, wary of the means (National Review): the editorial board called SAVE "not worth the cost" and warned Republicans not to burn "our own fortifications" by nuking the filibuster, since the same protection shields the GOP in the minority. Sympathy for election-integrity goals runs alongside strong institutional resistance to blowing up Senate rules on Trump's demand; The Dispatch's Jonah Goldberg has separately defended voter ID as reasonable and popular.

Sources: Washington Post, NPR, The Hill, TIME, NBC News
Courts & Detention

Fifth Circuit says ICE can't hold migrants past 90 days without a bond hearing, deepening a split bound for the Supreme Court

+

On July 2 a divided (2–1) panel of the Fifth U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals ruled that the government cannot hold immigrants in mandatory detention beyond 90 days without a bond hearing, resting on constitutional due process rather than statute. Judge Leslie Southwick, joined by Judge James Graves Jr., invoked the principle that due process protects "everyone" within U.S. boundaries, including "a right to be heard when personal liberty is taken"; Judge Cory Wilson dissented. The case arose from three longtime Texas residents — each in the U.S. 14+ years, working, with U.S.-citizen children — detained without a hearing after routine traffic stops. It stems from a July 2025 ICE policy reclassifying interior noncitizens who entered without inspection as "applicants for admission" subject to mandatory no-bond detention.

The ruling adds a constitutional due-process floor and deepens an open circuit split: the Fifth and Eighth Circuits had sided with the government on the statutory question, while the Second, Sixth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits have rejected it. The Supreme Court granted certiorari in mid-June to resolve whether the government may hold noncitizens for prolonged periods without a bond hearing. A sourcing note: wire coverage described the July 2 decision as "a divided U.S. appeals court," while the Texas Tribune specifically identified it as the Fifth Circuit.

The Left-Leaning Take

A due-process emergency (Slate, ACLU): indefinite lockup of longtime residents with citizen children, arrested at traffic stops and denied any chance to see a judge, is exactly what the Constitution's liberty guarantee forbids. The worry is that a conservative Supreme Court could bless a sweeping detention power; the ACLU casts the appellate rulings as vindicating an established right to bond hearings against a "novel" reading of old statutes.

The Right-Leaning Take

The courts are misreading the statute (restrictionist right, e.g. the Center for Immigration Studies): Congress made "applicants for admission" subject to mandatory detention, and the 2025 guidance simply enforces that law, with earlier Fifth Circuit precedent greenlighting it. On this view, judges ordering bond hearings are bypassing binding precedent, and the Supreme Court should restore the government's detention authority — though a libertarian angle would likely favor the due-process holding.

Sources: Texas Tribune, CNBC/Reuters, NBC News, Fifth Circuit opinion, CNN

Iran

Iran opens a week of mass funerals for slain leader Khamenei, timed to defy Washington

+

The body of Ayatollah Ali Khamenei was laid in state Friday, July 3, in a vast Tehran hall as clerics, officials and foreign dignitaries paid respects, opening a week of state funeral events for the leader killed in February by U.S. and Israeli airstrikes at the start of the four-month war. Processions run July 4–9 across Tehran, Qom and Mashhad, and into Najaf and Karbala in Iraq, before burial in Mashhad. Attendees include Pakistani PM Shehbaz Sharif and representatives from China and India. His son and successor as supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei — wounded in the strike that killed his father — is unlikely to appear publicly and has not been seen since the war began.

The scale reads as a deliberately defiant signal: the July 4 lying-in-state coincides with the U.S. 250th-independence celebrations and a planned Trump address. The funeral also freezes diplomacy. Indirect U.S.–Iran talks in Doha — which Qatari mediators and Trump had described as making "positive progress" on the Strait of Hormuz and an emergency communications channel, alongside disputed claims of a partial release of frozen Iranian funds that U.S. officials denied — are paused, with the next round set only after the mourning period ends.

Sources: Reuters (via WTVB), CNN, CBS News, Al Jazeera
Sudan

Amnesty finds RSF committed crimes against humanity and ethnic cleansing in El Fasher

+

Amnesty International released a major report, "City Under Siege, Children Under Fire," concluding that Sudan's paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF) committed crimes against humanity and ethnic cleansing during the siege and October 2025 capture of El Fasher in North Darfur. Documented crimes between early 2024 and October 2025 include murder, forcible transfer, torture, rape, sexual slavery, enslavement, extermination and persecution; Amnesty says children were deliberately targeted and warns the acts may be relevant to the crime of genocide. The findings rest on interviews with 246 people, including 208 survivors, and highlight videos appearing to show an RSF commander executing captives.

The report intensifies accountability pressure: an independent UN fact-finding mission concluded in February that the assault bore the "hallmarks of genocide," and Amnesty says the burning of homes and persecution targeted the predominantly Zaghawa population, consistent with ethnic cleansing. Reuters has reported, citing RSF and army sources, that the commander in the videos was released and is again leading troops — a claim the RSF denies. The RSF broadly rejects the atrocity allegations.

Sources: Amnesty International, Al Jazeera, Middle East Eye
Venezuela

Quake toll passes 2,500 as rescuers pull a survivor from the rubble after eight days

+

More than a week after the twin June 24 earthquakes (magnitudes ~7.5 and 7.2, epicenters near Veroes, Yaracuy), the death toll has climbed to at least 2,595, with about 12,400 injured and roughly 50,000 still unaccounted for, according to acting President Delcy Rodríguez. Damage is concentrated in La Guaira and Caracas. On July 2, rescuers pulled a security guard alive from a collapsed shopping mall after roughly 120 hours trapped — a rare survival amid fading hopes — as a Mexican rescue brigade joined the effort and relief agencies warned that humanitarian needs are "skyrocketing." Reported tolls vary widely across outlets, reflecting fast-changing counts and transparency disputes; the rights group PROVEA is demanding independent verification, saying official figures "raise more doubts than they provide answers."

Politically, the disaster is unfolding under an extraordinary arrangement, per U.S. and wire reporting: Nicolás Maduro was taken into U.S. custody and replaced by his vice president, Rodríguez, and the Trump administration is now coordinating relief with her government despite having dismantled USAID — sending search-and-rescue teams and military logistics and committing $150 million to charities and the UN.

Sources: NPR, CBS News, NBC News, UN News

Semiconductors

AI-chip stocks tumble worldwide as investors question whether the buildout will pay off

+

The AI-chip trade reversed sharply on July 2 as a Wall Street selloff spread into Asian markets. SK Hynix fell more than 9% at the open and Samsung Electronics dropped over 7%, dragging down the KOSPI after U.S. chip names slid the prior session. The rout was driven by mounting skepticism over whether the enormous capital pouring into AI data centers will generate commensurate returns — compounded by reports that SK Hynix is slowing its high-bandwidth memory (HBM) capacity expansion and that Meta is building a cloud business to resell excess AI computing power, a signal read as possible softening in future chip demand.

The move follows an exceptional run: SK Hynix and Micron had each gained roughly 270% or more over the prior year as key memory suppliers to Nvidia's AI accelerators, leaving valuations exposed to any doubt about the durability of AI-infrastructure spending. The selloff is the counter-narrative to the compute arms race — a market test of whether gigawatt-scale ambition can be financed indefinitely.

Sources: CNBC, Seoul Economic Daily, Forbes
Grid & Energy

Heat wave pushes the largest U.S. grid toward a record as DOE forces data centers onto backup diesel

+

A severe pre-July 4 heat wave drove PJM Interconnection, the largest U.S. grid operator, to forecast an all-time peak of about 166,304 MW on July 2–3, edging past its 2006 record, with National Weather Service highs of 102–104°F across the Mid-Atlantic. Demand is now structurally higher than in 2006 because AI data centers in Northern Virginia and elsewhere draw large loads around the clock, regardless of weather.

To protect reliability, Energy Secretary Chris Wright issued two Section 202(c) emergency orders on June 30 authorizing PJM to curtail data centers and other large consumers with at least 50 MW of peak load, requiring them to switch to their own backup diesel generators within 15 minutes of an emergency signal. It was the third such emergency order this year — a vivid illustration of how AI-driven load growth is now colliding with extreme heat on the grid.

Sources: Utility Dive, Fox Business, E&E News
Oncology

Roche's divarasib beats approved rivals head-to-head in KRAS-mutated lung cancer

+

On July 2, Roche reported that its next-generation KRAS G12C inhibitor divarasib met both its primary and key secondary endpoints in the Phase III Krascendo 1 study — the first head-to-head trial pitting a next-gen inhibitor against approved first-generation drugs. Divarasib delivered statistically significant improvements in both progression-free survival and overall survival versus Amgen's sotorasib (Lumakras) or Bristol Myers Squibb's adagrasib (Krazati), with no new safety signals, in 338 patients with previously treated KRAS G12C-mutated non-small cell lung cancer.

The result is notable because overall-survival wins are relatively rare in this setting, positioning Roche/Genentech to challenge the incumbents. Roche said it will present the full data at an upcoming medical meeting and submit to regulators.

Sources: Roche, Endpoints News, Fierce Biotech
Astronomy

Astronomers grapple with JWST's rewritten early universe, from "little red dots" to overgrown black holes

+

A July 2 Quanta Magazine analysis synthesizes how the James Webb Space Telescope has upended models of the first billion years after the Big Bang. Chief among the puzzles are the "little red dots" — a class of compact, red objects appearing in large numbers roughly 650 million years after the Big Bang and never seen before JWST — alongside early galaxies that look too bright and supermassive black holes that grew far faster than theory predicts.

A wave of competing 2026 explanations is now being tested: that the dots are black holes in rare "super-Eddington" feeding bursts, "black hole stars"/quasi-stars cloaked in dense gas, or direct-collapse black holes seeded in the early cosmos. No single theory has won out, and the debate is reshaping how astronomers think galaxies and their central black holes first assembled.

Sources: Quanta Magazine, Phys.org, Harvard & Smithsonian CfA
