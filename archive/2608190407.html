Elections · DOJ

Justice Department plans a ‘historic’ 1,000 poll monitors for November as Senate Democrats warn of intimidation

+

The Justice Department plans to deploy roughly 1,000 monitors to polling places for November’s midterms, The Washington Post reported Monday, Aug. 17. Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon, who leads the Civil Rights Division, told Bloomberg the figure is “probably going to be a historic number for a Republican administration.” DOJ says the monitors will observe rather than intervene — watching for issues such as disability access and suspected fraud — and are trained on the same materials used by prior administrations under the division’s longstanding Voting Rights Act authority. The plan builds on a July 7 announcement of monitors in 15 jurisdictions across Arizona, Michigan, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New Hampshire and Virginia during the primaries, now expanded for the general election. Separately, the Republican National Committee announced an “election integrity” operation deploying poll watchers across at least 17 states.

Senate Democrats, including Mark Warner and Alex Padilla, pressed DOJ in a letter to ensure a “fair and legal” deployment, and Sen. Jacky Rosen (D-Nev.) called it an effort “to intimidate eligible voters.” Analysts note that many of the targeted jurisdictions are run by Democratic election officials, and that federal monitors have historically been tied to a court-order basis DOJ may lack in some states. DOJ has not yet named the fall jurisdictions. (Facts from The Washington Post, Bloomberg, The Hill and NOTUS.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads the deployment as a pretext for intimidation and a scaffold for post-election fraud claims — a partisan DOJ pairing federal monitors with an RNC observer army in Democratic-run precincts. Former DOJ voting-rights attorney David Becker is cited arguing the division’s real jurisdiction is narrow anti-discrimination work, making 1,000 monitors a disproportionate federal intrusion into state-run elections. (Directional; nearest in-window artifacts MSNBC and The New Republic.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The directional right casts this as routine, nonpartisan election-integrity enforcement — the same Civil Rights Division playbook past administrations used, now simply resourced — at a moment when public confidence in vote counts has slipped. The libertarian lane (Reason) is skeptical of an expanded federal footprint at the polls regardless of which party holds power, and The Dispatch has aired genuine intra-right unease about the administration’s posture toward the 2026 vote. (Directional; no clean in-window NR/WSJ editorial isolated.)

Polling · Trump Approval

Trump’s approval sits at 33% — the lowest of his presidency — with the Iran war and prices weighing

+

A Reuters/Ipsos poll reported this week put President Trump’s job approval at 33% — the lowest of his second term and tied for his all-time low — with disapproval at 64%. The reading is down about two points from roughly 35% two weeks earlier. The survey was conducted online Aug. 14–17 among 1,166 US adults and carries a margin of error of about three percentage points. Reuters tied the slide heavily to the war the US and Israel launched against Iran in late February, which has snarled and slowed roughly a fifth of the global oil trade, and to the broader economy.

An accompanying finding cited by Forbes reported that about 80% of respondents expect US involvement in Iran to drag on. The number lands roughly two and a half months before the November midterms and amid a run of bruising headlines on the war, prices and a drawn-down strategic petroleum reserve. (Facts from Reuters/Ipsos via US News, The Hill and Forbes.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads 33% as durable erosion rather than a blip — the country catching up to what critics warned in February, and pinning it on choices Trump owns: an unauthorized, open-ended war, tariffs and rising costs. MSNBC’s framing has the number puncturing the president’s self-congratulation and turning the war-plus-prices combination into a concrete midterm liability for the GOP. Middle East · US–Iran War

The US–Iran deadline lapses with no deal; Trump rules out talks, threatens Oman and calls Hormuz ‘New US Territory’

+

The 60-day US–Iran memorandum of understanding — signed in June to buy time for a war-ending deal after the conflict that began with joint US–Israeli strikes on Feb. 28 — lapsed Monday, Aug. 17 with no wider agreement. On Tuesday, Aug. 18 Trump said there are “no talks underway or scheduled” with Tehran, played down prospects for a deal and said he was “in no hurry” to end the roughly 24-week war. Control of the Strait of Hormuz — through which about a fifth of the world’s oil and gas flows — remains the central flashpoint, alongside frozen Iranian funds. Trump threatened to “bomb the s—— out of” Oman if it “gets in the way,” after Iran said it had reached an understanding with Muscat on future shipping routes, and posted a map labeling the strait “New US Territory,” saying “we control it” via a US naval blockade.

Outlets diverge sharply on conditions in the waterway: Trump called the strait “open and operating” and suggested it had been demined, but US Central Command declined to confirm that, while Iran says Hormuz “remains blocked” and “under Iran’s control.” Independent shipping data support a degraded picture — transits fell 19.5% to 95 last week, with only three vessels crossing on Sunday — and a vessel was struck in the strait on Aug. 18, killing a crew member. Separately, the UAE said its air defenses detected two ballistic missiles launched from Iran on Tuesday, one landing in its territorial waters, and announced it had suspended all trade and financial dealings with Tehran; Iran’s Foreign Ministry denied responsibility. The missile attribution, the claimed Iran–Oman arrangement and the competing control claims are belligerent assertions not independently verified. (Facts from CBS News, CNBC, CNN and The Washington Post.)

Technology · AI Safety

OpenAI launches ‘ChatGPT for Teens’ and will route suspected minors into it using age prediction

+

On Tuesday, Aug. 18 OpenAI announced ChatGPT for Teens, a version of the chatbot tailored to users aged 13–17, with a global rollout expected to finish within about two weeks. The teen experience applies stronger content restrictions — blocking material on suicide, self-harm and romantic or sexual conversation — and reframes homework help to support learning rather than produce finished essays. Notably, users are placed into teen mode not only if they state an age of 13–17 but also if OpenAI’s age-prediction system estimates they are under 18, drawing on account-level and behavioral signals such as how long an account has existed, usage patterns and typical times of day a user is active.

The launch drew scrutiny because OpenAI has not disclosed a launch-wide accuracy rate for the age-prediction system, nor how the model performed in testing. The move follows the company’s January 2026 signaling that it would bring age prediction to consumer plans, and arrives amid mounting regulatory pressure on AI firms over minors’ safety. Climate · Aviation

The UK and Google launch ‘Operation Blue Skies,’ a first airspace-scale trial to reroute flights around warming contrails

+

On Aug. 18 a UK-led consortium launched Operation Blue Skies, billed as the first state-backed effort to manage contrail avoidance across an entire oceanic airspace. The 30-month, £5 million program — including £2.65 million from the UK Department for Transport via the Aerospace Technology Institute — targets Shanwick Oceanic airspace over the eastern North Atlantic, a region estimated to account for roughly 5% of global contrail warming. Google will use satellite imagery, flight data and weather models to forecast where persistent, warming contrails are likely to form; air-traffic provider NATS will then direct selected commercial flights to climb or descend by a few thousand feet to steer clear of the “hotspot” zones.

Two operational trials will run across roughly 30 active trial days over two successive winters (November–February), with partners including the UK Met Office, contrails.org, Imperial College London and the University of Cambridge. Contrails — the ice clouds that form behind jet engines — are believed to contribute a significant and, until recently, under-addressed share of aviation’s total climate impact, potentially comparable to that of its CO₂ emissions. (Facts from NATS, Google, Quartz and The Next Web.)

Sources: NATS, Google, Quartz, The Next Web
Energy · EV Charging & Grid

Huawei unveils 800-amp liquid-cooled charging with on-site DC storage for sites that outrun the grid

+

On Aug. 18 Huawei unveiled an 800-ampere fully liquid-cooled charging system paired with a DC energy-storage solution, positioned to let EV fast-charging sites deliver high power even where local grid capacity is constrained. The company’s liquid-cooled DC dispensers now reach 800A (with “boost” units at 600A), and a single dispenser can supply up to 720 kW — enough, per Huawei, to add roughly 200 km of range in about five minutes. The system spans a 150–1,000V range, runs below 55 dB thanks to full liquid cooling, claims up to 96% efficiency via a power-sharing matrix and is rated for a 10-year service life.

The DC-storage pairing is the strategic point: by buffering energy in on-site batteries, operators can build ultra-fast charging without waiting on costly grid upgrades — a growing bottleneck as charger power ratings climb. The launch extends the fast-charging arms race Huawei escalated earlier in 2026 with a 1,500 kW system, shown at the Beijing Auto Show, that carries a 215 kWh DC storage module. Science · Particle Physics

Physicists find evidence the proton’s ‘baryon number’ rides on a Y-shaped gluon junction, not its quarks

+

Physicists with the STAR collaboration at Brookhaven National Laboratory report evidence, from collision data at the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider, that a proton’s baryon number — the conserved quantum property tied to the stability of matter — is carried by a Y-shaped “junction” of gluons connecting the three valence quarks, rather than by the quarks themselves. If confirmed, the result revises the standard textbook picture of proton structure and offers a mechanism for why protons are so extraordinarily stable, remaining intact over timescales far exceeding the age of the universe. The work was published in the journal Science and detailed by Brookhaven and collaborating institutions including Rice University.

This is the leading physical-sciences story in the current cycle rather than a strict last-24-hours item: the paper and institutional releases date to mid-August and remained a top item on science desks through Aug. 18. It is included as the strongest genuine natural-science development available in the window. (Facts from ScienceDaily, Phys.org, Brookhaven National Laboratory and Rice University.)
