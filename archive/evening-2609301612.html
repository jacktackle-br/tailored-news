The Budget · Shutdown Begins

It’s happening: the Senate blocked both funding bills tonight, and the government shuts down at midnight — with the White House now threatening mass firings, not just furloughs

+

The last off-ramp closed this evening. The Senate took up two rival stopgaps — the House-passed Republican “clean” bill funding the government into late November, and a Democratic measure pairing funding with an extension of the enhanced Affordable Care Act subsidies — and neither reached the 60 votes needed. With the votes failed and no deal, federal funding lapses and the government begins shutting down at 12:01 a.m. on Oct. 1, the first shutdown of Trump’s second term. Majority Leader John Thune said “Democrats may have chosen to shut down the government tonight, but we can reopen it tomorrow” if a handful cross over; Minority Leader Chuck Schumer countered that “Republicans are plunging America into a shutdown… risking America’s health care.”

What’s new since this morning is not just that the talks failed but how the administration is preparing to fight. Budget director Russell Vought’s OMB has directed agencies to go beyond routine furloughs and draw up reductions in force — permanent layoffs — in programs whose funding lapses, an unprecedented use of a shutdown to shrink the workforce. Unions have signaled legal challenges. The core dispute is unchanged: Republicans want health policy negotiated separately, Democrats say the subsidy cliff (premiums rise before Nov. 1 open enrollment) is the whole point of their leverage. (Fast-moving overnight; a quick reopening vote is possible if any Democrats defect.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left says the party that holds the White House and both chambers owns this shutdown — and owns letting health-care help lapse to protect tax cuts for the wealthy. The threat to convert furloughs into permanent firings, they argue, is lawless coercion: using workers’ livelihoods as a cudgel to break Democratic resolve rather than negotiate. Hold the line. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right says Democrats chose a shutdown to force an expensive Obamacare bailout onto an unrelated bill — pass the clean stopgap, then argue health policy on its own (the WSJ editorial line). Libertarians (Reason) note a bloated government could stand to trim. Anti-Trump conservatives at The Bulwark warn that weaponizing mass firings sets a precedent the right will regret — an intra-right dissent, not the party line. (Directional.)

The Pentagon · Civil-Military Relations

Now it’s on the record: Hegseth delivered the Quantico address — ‘highest male standards,’ no ‘fat generals,’ and a campaign-style turn from Trump before a sea of stone-faced brass

+

The speech this morning’s brief flagged as “still emerging” has now been delivered. Before roughly 800 generals, admirals and senior enlisted leaders summoned to Quantico, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth demanded an end to “division, distraction and gender delusions,” vowed to bring back “the highest male standards,” and issued directives to raise fitness requirements force-wide, tighten grooming (no “fat generals,” no “beardos”), loosen rules of engagement, scrap racial quotas and ease boot-camp restrictions. President Trump followed with a lengthy, campaign-style address that at one point floated using troop deployments to U.S. cities as “training.”

The reception, by multiple accounts, was cool. Cameras caught rows of expressionless officers; a serving Army general told reporters the event was “a massive waste of time” and “if he ever had us, he lost us.” Analysts at CSIS and elsewhere questioned the cost, security and precedent of pulling combatant commanders from posts worldwide for a message many read as a loyalty test. Supporters counter that a force distracted by social priorities was overdue for a lethality reset. (Reactions from serving officers are largely anonymous; the administration defended the gathering.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a loyalty spectacle that politicized the officer corps: hauling the entire senior brass across oceans for an anti-“woke” lecture staged with the president in the room, capping a year of purges that thin experienced leadership and chill dissent. The musing about troops in American cities, they warn, is the tell. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right largely cheers the refocus on standards and lethality over a top-heavy, distracted Pentagon — substance over stagecraft. Libertarians (Reason) flag the expense and the domestic-deployment talk. Anti-Trump conservatives at The Dispatch and The Bulwark argue the loyalty overtones are precisely what a professional military must avoid — an intra-right alarm, not the party line. (Directional.)

Moldova · Europe

A win Moscow tried hard to buy: Moldova’s pro-EU governing party holds its majority, and Sandu says Russia ‘did not achieve its objectives’

+

Results from Moldova’s parliamentary election have firmed up, and the pro-Western outcome held: President Maia Sandu’s Party of Action and Solidarity (PAS) took better than 50% of the vote, well clear of the pro-Russian Patriotic Bloc at under 25% — enough to keep its parliamentary majority and its bid to join the European Union on track. Sandu declared that “Russia has not achieved its objectives” and that Moldova “managed to resist this huge interference.”

The vote was among the most heavily targeted in Europe. Sandu’s government says Moscow poured “hundreds of millions of euros” into the race, with funds funneled to well over 100,000 voters through networks linked to fugitive oligarch Ilan Shor, alongside cyberattacks, false bomb threats at overseas polling stations and organized vote-busing. The result matters beyond Moldova: it keeps a front-line state on its EU-accession path and hands Brussels a rare clear win against the Kremlin’s influence operations, even as the razor-thin margins underscore how contested the country’s orientation remains. (Interference figures are the government’s; some totals are still being finalized.)

The Budget · Shutdown Countdown

Hours from a shutdown: the talks have collapsed, and the government is set to begin closing at midnight with the ACA subsidies still the wall

+

Federal funding lapses at the end of the fiscal year today, Sept. 30, and without a stopgap the government begins shutting down at 12:01 a.m. on Oct. 1 — now only hours away. Monday’s White House meeting between President Trump and the four congressional leaders — Senate Majority Leader John Thune, Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, House Speaker Mike Johnson and Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries — broke up with no agreement, and no breakthrough has been reported since. Trump at one point scrapped a planned sit-down, citing “unserious and ridiculous” Democratic demands; Schumer accused him of “running away” from the table and said the two sides have “very large differences.”

The divide is unchanged. Republicans, who hold both chambers narrowly, want a “clean” short-term bill funding the government at current levels into late November. Democrats are withholding their votes unless the measure also extends the enhanced Affordable Care Act premium subsidies that expire at year’s end, warning premiums will spike before open enrollment begins Nov. 1. Because a stopgap needs 60 Senate votes and Republicans hold 53, roughly eight Democrats would have to cross over, and few have signaled they will. The standoff lands five weeks before the Nov. 3 midterms, sharpening every incentive to assign blame. (Fast-moving; a last-minute stopgap could still change the picture before the deadline.)

+

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth summoned roughly 800 of the military’s most senior generals, admirals and top enlisted advisers to Marine Corps Base Quantico in Virginia for a rare, short-notice mass gathering. In a blunt address he called for an end to “division, distraction and gender delusions,” laid out his “warrior ethos,” and issued directives to raise physical standards to a “male level” across the force, toughen grooming (“no more beardos,” no more “fat generals”), loosen rules of engagement and scrap what he called racial quotas. Early reaction split sharply: a serving Army general called it “a massive waste of time” and told The Washington Post, “if he ever had us, he lost us,” while a retired four-star described it as a “very useful” session at an uncertain moment. Critics questioned the cost and security of pulling combatant commanders from posts worldwide on short notice; the White House waved off the concerns. (As of this writing, accounts of the address were still emerging; details per advance and initial reporting.)

Gaza · Middle East

Now it is Hamas’s move: Netanyahu backs Trump’s 20-point plan — a 72-hour hostage release, a ‘Board of Peace’ — but disarmament is still the catch

+

A day after President Trump and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu unveiled a 20-point framework at the White House — with Netanyahu declaring, “I support your plan” — attention swings to Hamas, which is expected to take a few days to respond. The plan calls for an immediate halt to fighting; the release within 72 hours of the 20 living Israeli hostages and the remains of the roughly two dozen believed dead, in exchange for hundreds of detained Gazans (about 2,000 prisoners cited); no governing role for Hamas; and an international “Board of Peace” to oversee an interim administration. It leaves a door open to eventual Palestinian statehood — which Netanyahu again publicly ruled out.

The old obstacle remains: sequencing. The framework envisions Hamas disarming, but the group has signalled it will not give up its weapons without a full Israeli withdrawal, while Israel says it will not withdraw until Hamas disarms. Netanyahu warned that if Hamas refuses, Israel will “finish the job.” In a notable side-move, Netanyahu phoned Qatar’s prime minister from the Oval Office and apologized for Israel’s Sept. 9 strike on Doha, a concession aimed at keeping the key mediator on board. (A framework, not a signed deal; Hamas’s answer will decide whether it holds.)

Venezuela · The Caribbean

The buildup off Venezuela hardens: at least six US strikes on alleged drug boats have killed some 27 people, as Caracas moves 25,000 troops to the coast

+

Since the start of September the U.S. military has carried out at least six strikes on vessels in or near Venezuelan waters, killing roughly 27 people, the Trump administration says, describing the targets as drug-running boats tied to the Tren de Aragua gang, which Washington has designated a foreign terrorist organization. The administration has not published evidence that the boats carried narcotics bound for the United States, and Venezuelan officials say the dead were civilians and fishermen. In a Sept. 12 incident, Caracas said the tuna boat Carmen Rosa, with nine fishermen aboard, was confronted by the destroyer USS Jason Dunham.

The confrontation is escalating on both sides. The U.S. has deployed some eight warships and about 4,500 sailors and Marines to the region for what it calls counter-narcotics operations, and President Nicolás Maduro’s government says it is sending 25,000 troops to border and coastal areas. Legal scholars and members of Congress have questioned the lethal strikes as extrajudicial killings carried out without due process or clear authorization, and Trump is reported to be weighing strikes on land targets inside Venezuela. (Casualty counts are disputed between Washington and Caracas; details are evolving.)

Astronomy · Cosmic Dawn

‘Faint quasars’ in the infant universe turn out to be something stranger: galaxies ablaze with monster stars, JWST finds

+

New observations from the James Webb Space Telescope find that two early-universe objects long taken to be faint quasars — the glow of matter falling into supermassive black holes — are instead extraordinarily luminous galaxies lit by intense bursts of star formation. Their spectra carry the signatures of extremely massive stars, some potentially more than 200 times the mass of the Sun, a population rarely glimpsed and thought to be far more common in the universe’s first few hundred million years.

The distinction matters because untangling black-hole light from raw starlight is central to reconstructing how the first galaxies — and the first black holes — actually grew during “cosmic dawn.” It adds to a run of JWST results that keep forcing astronomers to revise the early-universe picture, where objects are turning out brighter, more massive and more chemically evolved than pre-Webb models predicted. (Interpretations of these faint, extremely distant sources remain contested and typically firm up with follow-up spectroscopy.)

Sources: Phys.org, ScienceDaily
Technology · Semiconductors

The AI chip boom is about to make the industry a trillion-dollar business — and its carbon footprint is climbing just as fast

+

The semiconductor industry is on track to break the $1 trillion annual-revenue mark in 2026, roughly four years ahead of most earlier forecasts, driven overwhelmingly by AI demand — and above all by high-bandwidth memory (HBM), the stacked chips that feed AI accelerators. The scramble to supply data centers has reshaped where and how fast the industry is investing, with memory makers racing to add capacity.

The climate bill is rising in step. A Greenpeace East Asia analysis found the electricity used for AI-chip production more than tripled to about 984 GWh, a roughly fourfold jump in related carbon emissions to around 453,600 metric tons of CO₂ — concentrated in Taiwan, South Korea and Japan, where grids still lean heavily on fossil fuels. Analysts warn HBM’s carbon and supply-chain footprint is growing faster than efficiency gains can offset, tying the AI boom’s hardware layer directly to emissions. (Figures are from industry forecasts and an NGO analysis and can be revised.)

Energy · Grid & Storage

Batteries cross a milestone: global storage tops 1,000 GWh, and in the US solar-plus-storage now dominates new grid capacity

+

Global cumulative battery-storage capacity has surpassed 1,000 GWh, according to IEA data, with the United States, China and the European Union adding the most as they weave more variable wind and solar into their grids. In the U.S., solar and storage together made up roughly 91% of new generating capacity added in the first quarter of 2026 — the highest combined share on record — and the EIA expects about 24.3 GW of new storage to come online this year, past 2025’s record of roughly 15 GW.

The fleet is already doing real grid work. On one July evening the batteries within California’s CAISO footprint discharged a record 12.99 GW, covering about 36% of regional demand as solar faded and smoothing the steep evening ramp. The build-out is fast becoming a central hedge as surging AI data-center load strains grids — storage can shift midday solar into the peak hours when power is scarcest and dirtiest. (Figures are from the IEA, EIA and grid operators; annual totals are estimates.)
