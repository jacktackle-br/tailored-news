Three longer reads for the week — the kind of thing worth turning over slowly with a second cup of coffee.

History

A 60-year diary, begun in bondage, and the family that would not let its record be erased

+

Adam Francis Plummer began writing in 1841, and he did not stop for the rest of his life. Born into slavery in Prince George’s County, Maryland, in 1819, Plummer learned to read and write from a Black preacher named John Bowser at Riversdale — the Calvert family plantation where he was enslaved — a lesson that, had it been discovered, could have cost them both dearly. In a Smithsonian Magazine feature published July 17, Michelle Mehrtens tells the story of what he did with that dangerous literacy: for more than six decades he kept a diary, recording births and deaths, the goods of a household, debts owed and paid, and the constant threat of separation that hung over an enslaved family whose members were scattered across owners and counties. It is now believed to be the only known example in the United States of a multigenerational journal begun by an enslaved person.

The record did not die with him. After Plummer’s death his daughter Nellie Arnold Plummer carried the diary forward, and it became the spine of a family’s claim on its own history — against an archive that, for the enslaved, was mostly kept by other people, in ledgers of property rather than of lives. The leather-bound volume is now the centerpiece of “We Make History,” a new exhibition at the Smithsonian’s Anacostia Community Museum that opened May 30, the 185th anniversary of Plummer’s first entry, and runs through January 2028. What lingers is the quiet audacity of the project: a man who was himself counted as another’s possession insisting, day after day, on keeping the count himself.

Sources: Smithsonian Magazine, Anacostia Community Museum, Smithsonian Institution
Religion

Mindfulness is sold as calm. Its Buddhist roots warn that stillness can also be hard

+

Modern mindfulness arrives with a promise printed on the box: sit, breathe, and grow calm. In an essay published July 17 for The Conversation and syndicated by Religion News Service, Ronald S. Green — professor and chair of philosophy and religious studies at Coastal Carolina University — argues that the promise quietly omits something its Buddhist sources always knew. A growing body of research points to adverse effects for some practitioners: anxiety, emotional distress, the resurfacing of painful memories, even dissociation. That is not a malfunction of meditation so much as a feature the tradition anticipated. Buddhism has long held that turning attention inward can be difficult, even destabilizing, and built its practice accordingly.

Green’s point is about context, not contraindication. In its original setting, meditation is learned step by step from experienced teachers and inside a community — beginning with ethical living, then the training of attention, and only later the deeper insight that can unsettle before it heals. Stripped of that scaffolding and repackaged as a self-taught stress-reduction technique for the app store and the corporate wellness program, the same practice can leave people alone with exactly the material it stirs up. How mindfulness is practiced, and who guides it, turns out to matter as much as whether it is practiced at all — a reminder that a spiritual technology does not travel free of the tradition that shaped it.

Sources: The Conversation, Religion News Service
Culture

Before the village mysteries: the year Agatha Christie surfed her way around the Empire

+

The Agatha Christie of popular memory is a creature of drawing rooms and quiet English villages — which is exactly the image a JSTOR Daily essay by Nancy West, published July 14, sets out to complicate. In 1922, four years before the novel that made her famous, Christie left her two-year-old daughter with her sister and boarded a ship for a ten-month circuit of the British Empire, accompanying her husband Archie on a trade mission — led by the blustering Major Ernest Belcher — to drum up participation in the forthcoming 1924 British Empire Exhibition. The itinerary read like an adventure serial: South Africa, Australia, New Zealand, Hawaii, Canada. She wrote home to her mother nearly every week, and her letters brim with motor trips, seasickness, sunburn and glamour in roughly equal measure.

The detail that delights, and that West lingers on, is that Christie became a genuine and enthusiastic surfer — among the first Britons to ride a board standing up, at beaches in South Africa and off Waikiki. The letters sat unpublished for some ninety years before her grandson Matthew Prichard gathered them into The Grand Tour, and they reframe their author: not the cloistered miniaturist of legend but a restless, physically adventurous young woman drinking in the wider world. It is a small corrective with a larger moral about how we flatten famous lives into their most marketable shape — and how much texture waits in the correspondence we never bothered to read.

Sources: JSTOR Daily, The Agatha Christie Estate, HarperCollins, The Grand Tour
