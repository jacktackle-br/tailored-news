Iran War · Stalemate

Iran’s death toll climbs to 18 after a strike on a wedding as Trump vows to hit ‘anytime we want’ and the war grinds on with no end in sight

+

The six-month U.S.–Iran war entered another spasm of tit-for-tat this week without moving closer to resolution. After U.S. forces struck targets inside Iran on Tuesday, the death toll from the latest American strikes rose to 18 killed and more than 100 wounded, Iran’s health ministry said — among them a strike on a home hosting a wedding in Kouhestak, in Sirik County, that local officials said killed five people, including a child. Iran answered with missiles and drones it claimed targeted U.S.-linked bases across the region — Bahrain, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait and the UAE — though host governments reported the fire was intercepted or landed without damage, and the U.S. reported no casualties. Iran’s Revolutionary Guard said the strikes had only hardened its resolve and tightened its effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint for roughly a fifth of the world’s seaborne oil.

President Trump threatened further attacks, saying Washington could strike Iran “anytime we want” and that the renewed fighting would not last “too long,” even as he dismissed diplomacy. But the strategic picture is one of deadlock: a June memorandum of understanding meant to end the war has not held, and lawmakers of both parties describe a conflict that is militarily stalled. “Very clearly militarily, we are stalled,” Republican Rep. Pat Harrigan of North Carolina said. (Casualty figures per Iran’s health ministry and disputed; regional-strike claims rest on preliminary and contested reporting.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a “no more forever wars” president trapped in exactly one: six months of intermittent strikes, a wedding hit and civilians dead, allied bases absorbing Iranian fire, and a commander-in-chief who answers a stalemate with boasts about hitting “anytime we want.” With Hormuz still throttled and gas prices climbing into an election, they argue, Trump has substituted an open-ended cycle of violence for any stated war aim or exit. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The hawkish mainstream right reads continued pressure as necessary: Iran is choking the world’s key oil artery and firing on U.S. partners, and only sustained strikes force Tehran to the terms of a durable deal. The restraint and anti-Trump right breaks the other way, calling a “stalled” war with mounting civilian casualties and no achievable objective precisely the entanglement voters rejected — and warning that bravado is not a strategy. (The restraint/anti-Trump critique is intra-right disagreement, not the hawkish mainstream position; directional.)

Midterms · The Campaign

Trump vows to barnstorm 35 races in the final 30 days as a sour economy and the Iran war weigh on the GOP

+

At a Rose Garden dinner Wednesday for Republican lawmakers in competitive seats, President Trump pledged to campaign personally in roughly 35 House and Senate races over the final 30 days before the Nov. 3 midterms, promising to reprise the relentless travel schedule of his 2024 run to protect the party’s majorities. Attendees included Susie Wiles, Vice President JD Vance, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent; Trump argued Republicans have a winning message and should run on his record, and that a late blitz matters because voters “forget” appearances staged too early.

The confidence runs against the numbers. Job growth has slowed sharply, gasoline prices have climbed with the Iran war, and CBS polling has found 58% of Americans saying Trump’s policies have left them financially worse off — up from 42% in March 2025. Analysts and some Senate Republicans privately describe the economy and the war as twin drags heading into the stretch, even as the president casts himself as the party’s closer. (Per wire and Hill reporting on the event and recent polling.)

Skeptics on the right are warier — arguing that a president underwater on the economy risks reminding swing voters why they’re unhappy, and that candidates in tough seats may want distance, not a rally. Elections · Mail Voting

A USPS whistleblower warns a rushed portal for Trump’s mail-voting order could reject ballots by the thousands

+

A whistleblower disclosure has thrown fresh doubt on the machinery behind President Trump’s March executive order tightening federal control of mail voting. According to the disclosure, reported this week, the Postal Service has been building an online portal for states to submit absentee voters’ names and ballot-envelope barcodes for checking against a federally approved voter list — but has set a “zero-percent failure” rule under which a single mismatched scan would reject an entire batch, even one holding tens of thousands of ballots. The whistleblower’s lawyer warned the design could “derail” the midterms by keeping ballots from voters.

The legal ground is shifting fast. A federal judge has temporarily blocked USPS from requiring states to use the portal to mail ballots, and the Postal Service is appealing; the Supreme Court earlier handed the administration an interim procedural win on the underlying order. USPS said it is “carefully reviewing” the whistleblower’s claims, while congressional Democrats and several states demanded the plan be scrapped. (Per whistleblower disclosure and NPR/OPB reporting; USPS disputes characterizations and litigation is ongoing.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left calls it voter suppression by software: a “zero-percent failure” batch-rejection rule is, they argue, a feature rather than a bug — a way to choke off mail voting in states that rely on it, dressed up as fraud prevention for a problem that doesn’t exist. The courts, they say, are the only backstop. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The directional right frames verifying ballots against an approved voter list as basic election integrity, and casts the whistleblower furor as a partisan effort to kill oversight before it starts. Libertarian-leaning voices split the difference: favor clean rolls in principle, but warn that a brittle, centralized federal system rushed out weeks before an election is the kind of government failure that disenfranchises real people. (Directional; no in-window signed editorial verified.)

Europe · Hybrid War

Berlin blames Russia for a foiled explosive-drone plot at Leipzig airport, and the EU calls it ‘state-sponsored terrorism’

+

Germany has formally attributed an attempted attack on one of its airports to Moscow for the first time. Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt said the government concluded Russia was behind an August incident at Leipzig/Halle Airport in which a drone carrying an explosive charge was found near a Ukrainian cargo aircraft; the device failed to detonate, bouncing off the airframe and falling intact to the tarmac. Berlin said the episode fits a known pattern of Russian hybrid operations across Europe. Moscow rejected the accusation and summoned Germany’s chargé d’affaires in protest.

Europe responded in unison and with edge. EU foreign-policy chief Kaja Kallas summoned Russia’s envoy in Brussels, said the plot bore “all the hallmarks of state-sponsored terrorism,” and signaled the bloc would weigh further sanctions on Russian military assets. Germany ordered the closure of the Russian consulate in Bonn and the Russian House cultural center in Berlin and tightened entry controls for Russian nationals — a hardening that reflects mounting European alarm over sabotage, drone incursions and cyberattacks tied to the war in Ukraine. (Attribution per German and EU officials; contested by Moscow.)

Technology · AI Compute

Anthropic signs a $35 billion cloud deal for a Texas data center — and reignites the ‘circular financing’ debate around Nvidia

+

Anthropic agreed to a six-year, roughly $35 billion cloud-computing deal with Lambda, an Nvidia-backed provider, to secure capacity at a Hut 8-developed data center in Nueces County, Texas — about 350 megawatts of it. Under the arrangement, Nvidia supplies the chips and backs the tenant while Lambda installs and operates them, expanding the pool of Nvidia compute available to train and run Anthropic’s Claude models. It is the latest in a rapid string of megadeals for the company, which has separately committed tens of billions to capacity from Nscale in West Virginia and from Fluidstack.

The scale reframed the day’s tech story around a familiar worry: circular financing. Because Nvidia both invests in the cloud providers and sells them the GPUs those providers then rent back to AI labs, analysts questioned how much of the sector’s headline spending is genuinely independent demand versus money cycling within a tight cluster of firms. The binding constraints, meanwhile, are increasingly power and land — a reminder that the AI buildout is now as much an energy and real-estate story as a chip story. Planetary Science · Mars

A hidden heat anomaly beneath Mars’s southern hemisphere may explain why the planet’s two halves look so different

+

Scientists report evidence that the interior beneath Mars’s southern hemisphere is roughly 200 to 400°C hotter than the north — and may be partially molten. Writing in Nature, a team led by Alexander Berne reconstructed the planet’s gravitational field using decades of tracking data from the Mars Global Surveyor, Odyssey and Reconnaissance Orbiter missions, applying a technique called tidal tomography that gauges how Mars deforms in response to the Sun’s pull over its seasonal cycle.

The buried warm zone, centered under the southern highlands, offers a possible key to the “Martian dichotomy” — the long-standing puzzle of why the planet’s smooth northern lowlands and rugged southern highlands are so starkly different — and could bear on Mars’s odd patchwork magnetism, its seismic behavior and the history of water on its surface. It also argues, the authors say, that the Red Planet’s interior is more thermally alive than a cold, dead world would be. (Peer-reviewed result; the inferred molten fraction and its cause remain under study.)

Energy & Climate · Outlook

Major 2026 energy outlooks converge on a hard verdict: 1.5°C is out of reach, even as wind and solar take over new demand

+

This year’s flagship energy outlooks — BloombergNEF’s New Energy Outlook 2026 among them — deliver a blunt message: holding warming to 1.5°C is no longer feasible given persistently high emissions and continued investment in emissions-intensive assets. At the same time, the transition itself is accelerating on economics rather than mandates: across scenarios, growth in electricity generation is led by wind and solar, with renewables displacing coal and electric vehicles slowing the growth of oil demand over the coming decade.

The plumbing is scaling to match. In the U.S., the Edison Electric Institute raised its five-year grid-investment plan by 27%, from $1.1 trillion to $1.4 trillion, to accommodate surging demand — much of it from AI data centers — while federal forecasters expect new 2026 utility-scale capacity to be dominated by solar and batteries. The tension running through it all: clean power is winning the buildout even as the world concedes its headline temperature target. (Figures per BloombergNEF, EEI and EIA; outlooks are projections, not outcomes.)
