Military · Race & Memory

Navy weighs stripping Black WWII hero Doris Miller’s name from a new carrier to rename it for Trump, drawing NAACP and bipartisan condemnation

+

Navy leadership is weighing renaming the future aircraft carrier CVN-81 — currently designated the USS Doris Miller — potentially after President Trump, according to reporting first published by CNN on Aug. 20 and matched by NBC News, ABC and The Hill, citing multiple U.S. officials. Doris “Dorie” Miller was a Black mess attendant who manned an anti-aircraft gun during the Pearl Harbor attack, is credited with downing at least one Japanese plane, and pulled wounded sailors to safety; the carrier was named for him during Trump’s first term in 2020, making it the first U.S. carrier named for a Black American and for an enlisted sailor. Naming a carrier for a sitting president would be unprecedented — the Ford, Kennedy, Reagan and George H.W. Bush all honored those men after they left office or had died.

Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao is reported to be reviewing the guidance governing whom warships may be named for; one option under discussion, per NBC and CNN, is to move Miller’s name to a different vessel and recommend him for the Medal of Honor. The Navy has announced no decision and did not confirm the deliberations on the record. The story stayed live into Aug. 24 as the NAACP — whose president, Derrick Johnson, called it “a slap in the face to every war hero” — Miller’s family and Democratic lawmakers pushed back. (Facts from CNN, NBC News, ABC News and The Hill; sourced to anonymous officials pending any formal decision.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads this as vanity and racial erasure at once: an administration that ran against “DEI” now proposing to peel a rare honor off an enlisted Black combat hero and paste a living president’s name on it — a cult-of-personality move with no precedent in Navy history. The offer of “another ship and a medal” for Miller, they argue, is a face-saving consolation that only underscores the insult. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Mainstream and libertarian-right commentary is uneasy rather than celebratory: naming warships for sitting presidents breaks a bipartisan norm and smacks of the personality politics the right once criticized, with Reason-style objections to executive self-aggrandizement. Some caution that the reporting rests on anonymous officials and no formal decision — outrage over a trial balloon — but few defend displacing Miller specifically, given his stature. (Directional.)

Sources: CNN, NBC News, ABC News, The Hill
Foreign Policy · North Korea

Trump says he expects to meet Kim Jong Un again this year, but Pyongyang signals it won’t return to talks unless the U.S. accepts it as a nuclear power

+

Trump told reporters he expects to meet North Korean leader Kim Jong Un again in 2026 — answering “Yeah, I will be” when asked directly — reviving the personal diplomacy of his 2018–19 summits, which produced no denuclearization deal. The administration hopes to organize a meeting soon, possibly around the November APEC summit in China; a White House official said Trump is open to talks “at the appropriate time,” but no date is set and no formal planning has begun. There is a factual dispute over whether Pyongyang has engaged at all: Kim Yo Jong, Kim’s influential sister, publicly denied any leader-to-leader communication, saying there was “no communication…that we are aware of.”

Analysts across outlets stress Kim now negotiates from a stronger position — backed by Russia and China, less reliant on sanctions relief, and with a reported expanded nuclear arsenal — and would likely demand the U.S. accept North Korea as a nuclear-armed state rather than discuss giving up its weapons. Seoul is reported to fear being sidelined, including worries Trump could trade U.S. troop levels or sanctions for a deal. Coverage was concentrated Aug. 19–24 and developed into this window. (Facts from NBC News, CBS News and NPR; The Hill on the Kim Yo Jong denial.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left’s worry is legitimation without leverage: a president chasing a photo-op summit with a dictator repeats the “we fell in love” error of the first term and risks conceding North Korea’s status as a permanent nuclear power for nothing. There is added suspicion the overture is a distraction from a stalled Iran war and sagging approval, and alarm that Trump could bargain away troops in South Korea over Seoul’s objections. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Hawkish-right commentary warns against rewarding Kim’s nuclear buildup with the prestige of a summit while getting nothing verifiable — the Hanoi 2019 trap, now on worse terms since Kim holds more cards. Some allow that talking is not itself a concession and could pry Pyongyang slightly from Beijing and Moscow, but the editorial-board instinct is skepticism of any concessions — troops, sanctions — that would ratify North Korea as a nuclear state. (Directional.)

Sources: NBC News, CBS News, NPR, The Hill
Courts · Immigration

Federal judge vacates Trump’s visa freeze on immigrants from 75 countries, ruling Secretary of State Rubio exceeded his statutory authority

+

U.S. District Judge Jeannette Vargas (Southern District of New York) struck down the administration’s policy — announced in January 2026 — that suspended issuance of immigrant visas to applicants from 75 countries, which the government had justified on “public charge” grounds. In a 61-page decision issued Friday, Aug. 21, Vargas found the policy “contrary to law” and “in excess” of Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s authority, holding it violated the Immigration and Nationality Act’s bar on nationality-based discrimination in immigrant-visa issuance and usurped the individualized authority Congress vested in consular officers. The suspension covered nearly 40% of the world’s countries, with reporting citing Egypt, Afghanistan, Somalia, Haiti, Iran, Brazil and Russia.

The remedy is nationwide: the court set aside the suspension and voided visa denials based solely on the policy, ordering the State Department to resume individualized processing. The ruling kept generating Tier-1 coverage and administration reaction into Aug. 24; a government appeal had not been confirmed as of this writing. (Facts from NPR, PBS NewsHour, The Washington Post (partial — paywalled) and Al Jazeera.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left casts the ruling as a needed judicial check on a categorical, discriminatory travel-ban-by-another-name — a “public charge” pretext used to slam the door on nearly 40% of the world’s countries, disproportionately in Africa, the Caribbean, the Middle East and Latin America. The point they stress: Congress, not Rubio, sets visa criteria, and courts are — for now — still enforcing statutory limits on executive immigration overreach. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The restrictionist right frames it as yet another single district judge imposing a nationwide injunction to override the political branches’ broad, historically recognized authority over who enters the country — the “activist judge” objection sharpened by the Supreme Court’s recent skepticism of universal injunctions. A libertarian counter-current (Reason-style) diverges, welcoming the outcome as a check on blanket nationality bans and a defense of individualized, lawful immigration. (Directional.)

Sources: NPR, PBS NewsHour, The Washington Post, Al Jazeera

Middle East · Iran

The Iran sanctions land: Bessent’s ‘Operation Economic Outcast’ sweeps up Chinese and Hong Kong firms but spares major banks, as Beijing objects and Tehran vows retaliation

+

The sanctions package trailed as an “economic D-Day” arrived Monday, Aug. 24. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent’s “Operation Economic Outcast” hit nearly 60 individuals, entities and vessels tied to Iran’s nuclear and missile procurement, cyber operations and oil revenue, and expanded secondary sanctions to reach anyone operating in five Iranian sectors: aviation, digital assets, gold, shipping and technology. By Tuesday, Aug. 25, Bloomberg reported the designations had swept up dozens of firms in China and Hong Kong — but the administration stopped short of the far more consequential step of targeting major Chinese banks, even as Bessent insisted “no one is above the reach of U.S. sanctions.” Outlets differed on how hard the blow landed now: some framed country-level penalties as still on a wind-down “timeline” rather than in force, while others cast the rollout as a declared “global economic war.”

China’s response was pointed: Beijing said sanctions “do not fit the interest of any party” and would “only further intensify tensions.” Analysts flagged China, India, Turkey, Iraq and the UAE as the economies most exposed to secondary measures — and noted the campaign’s central weakness is that it largely misses its mark without China, the biggest buyer of Iranian crude. Tehran vowed retaliation, renewed threats to disrupt shipping in the Strait of Hormuz and warned Gulf neighbors that cooperating with Washington would be treated as an “act of war”; the U.S. separately cautioned mariners that complying with Iranian Hormuz “information” demands could itself trigger penalties. (Facts from CNBC, Bloomberg (partial — paywalled), Foreign Policy and Al Jazeera.)

Sources: CNBC, Bloomberg, Foreign Policy, Al Jazeera
Africa · DR Congo

Congo’s Ebola outbreak logs one of its deadliest weeks yet — 317 deaths in seven days — as the vaccine-less Bundibugyo strain outpaces every prior epidemic

+

Democratic Republic of the Congo health-ministry data cited Monday, Aug. 24 put the eastern Congo outbreak at 5,514 cases and 2,642 deaths as of Saturday, including 317 deaths in the prior seven days — among the highest weekly tolls recorded. Officials and reporting describe it as the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak in history, with many victims dying outside treatment centers; at this stage it has recorded several times more deaths than the 2014–16 West Africa epidemic did over the same span, and it has already surpassed Congo’s 2018–20 outbreak to become the country’s deadliest on record.

A central driver is that this outbreak is caused by the rare Bundibugyo species of Ebola, for which there is no approved vaccine or licensed treatment — unlike the Zaire strain covered by existing countermeasures. The scale is straining an already fragile, conflict-worn eastern Congo, and the WHO and Europe’s ECDC continue to track cross-border spillover risk toward Uganda. (Facts from ABC News/AP, The Washington Post (partial — paywalled), the WHO and the ECDC.)

Sources: ABC News (AP), The Washington Post, WHO, ECDC

Science · Space

NASA clears the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope for a targeted Aug. 30 launch aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy

+

NASA, SpaceX and mission managers completed Roman’s Flight Readiness Review on Aug. 21, formally clearing the observatory for final launch processing; by Aug. 23 the telescope had been fueled and sealed inside the Falcon Heavy payload fairing at Kennedy Space Center. Liftoff is targeted for Aug. 30 at 7:26 a.m. EDT (backup Aug. 31), sending Roman toward the Sun-Earth L2 point about 1.5 million kilometers away on a roughly month-long transit.

Named for NASA’s first chief of astronomy, the “mother of Hubble,” Roman carries a wide-field infrared instrument designed to survey vast swaths of sky to probe cosmic expansion and dark energy, map the structure of the universe and hunt exoplanets via microlensing. This is a launch-campaign milestone, not a science result — the launch itself has not yet occurred. (Facts from NASA’s Roman blog, SpaceNews and Aerospace America.)

Sources: NASA (Roman blog), SpaceNews, Aerospace America, NASA (launch countdown)
Technology · Photonics

Physicists demonstrate a ‘rainbow-on-a-chip’ microcomb that spins out many precisely spaced millimeter-wave signals at once, a possible building block for 6G

+

A team led by Loughborough University in the UK, with international collaborators, reports in Nature Communications that a grain-of-rice-sized chip can generate a stable optical microcomb — a set of evenly spaced light frequencies — that is then converted into multiple, precisely spaced millimeter-wave frequencies simultaneously. Earlier microcomb work produced a single clean millimeter-wave tone; generating many at once matters because each spaced frequency could serve as a separate high-capacity data channel.

The authors frame applications in higher-capacity 6G wireless, radar, spectroscopy and precision timing for quantum instruments. It is an experimental, proof-of-concept lab demonstration, not a deployed product — commercial 6G remains years off. (Facts from Phys.org, ScienceDaily and the arXiv preprint.)

Sources: Phys.org, ScienceDaily, arXiv preprint

Science · Biomedicine

A study of 2,500-plus people finds common drugs — not just antibiotics — leave lasting ‘fingerprints’ on the gut microbiome, sometimes for years

+

Researchers analyzing stool samples and prescription records from more than 2,500 participants in the Estonian Biobank’s microbiome cohort report in the American Society for Microbiology journal mSystems that antidepressants, beta-blockers, proton-pump inhibitors and benzodiazepines — alongside antibiotics — are associated with distinctive, durable shifts in gut bacterial composition, with some effects still detectable years after the last dose.

The authors argue this makes past medication use a hidden confounder that can bias microbiome studies, since current drug exposure alone does not capture a person’s medication history. The work is observational and association-based, so it does not establish that the drugs directly cause specific health outcomes. (Facts from ScienceDaily, Medscape and the journal mSystems.)

Sources: ScienceDaily, Medscape, mSystems
