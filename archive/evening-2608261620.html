Immigration · Courts

After a judge strikes down the 75-country visa ban, the administration pauses immigrant-visa appointments worldwide

+

The State Department has directed U.S. embassies and consulates worldwide to postpone immigrant-visa interviews while consular officers complete “in-depth” training on new “public charge” screening — guidance meant to weed out applicants officials deem likely to become dependent on public benefits. Applicants with appointments already scheduled were notified by email that their interviews are postponed; the department did not say when processing would resume. A State Department spokesperson framed it as a global training initiative to ensure applicants are “not likely to become a public charge, as defined under U.S. law.”

The worldwide pause is the administration’s answer to a courtroom loss: on Friday, Aug. 21, U.S. District Judge Jeannette Vargas struck down a policy suspending immigrant visas for applicants from 75 countries, ruling it exceeded Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s statutory authority and that nationality alone does not establish that a person cannot support themselves. Rather than appeal narrowly, the administration pivoted to a route not tied to any single passport — tougher, standardized individual scrutiny applied everywhere. The 75-country list had swept in applicants from much of Africa, the Middle East, South Asia, the Balkans, the Caribbean and parts of Latin America. (Facts from The Hill, U.S. News, The National and The Washington Post.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads the worldwide pause as pretext-shopping: told by a court that a nationality-based ban is illegal discrimination, the administration simply relabeled the same goal as “training” and applied the brakes globally, stranding legally qualified applicants and separating families indefinitely. A “public charge” rule with no end date, they argue, is a backdoor to shutting down legal immigration itself — the part of the system that plays by the rules. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The directional right frames it as ordinary sovereignty: nothing in law entitles a foreign national to a visa, and screening out those likely to draw on taxpayer benefits is exactly what the “public charge” statute exists to do. A conservative and libertarian caveat runs underneath — an open-ended, worldwide freeze on legal immigrants is a blunt instrument that punishes self-supporting applicants along with the rest, and invites the next court loss. (Directional.)

Sources: The Hill, U.S. News, The National, The Washington Post
Fed Independence · Executive Power

The deadline arrives: Cook refuses to step down as the White House weighs removing her from the Fed

+

Wednesday, Aug. 26 was the White House deadline for Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook to answer President Trump’s renewed bid to remove her over allegations she misstated her primary residence on 2021 mortgage applications — claims that predate her time at the Fed. Cook has given no sign of resigning. Her attorney, Abbe Lowell, reiterated that the allegations are “as baseless now as they were a year ago,” and that “no matter what President Trump tries to do next … there is no valid cause for removing Governor Cook” under the facts and Supreme Court precedent, vowing to challenge any removal in court.

The deadline’s passing sets up a final decision by the president that could come at any time, and with it a fresh round of litigation over whether a sitting Fed governor can be fired “for cause.” The Supreme Court on June 29 let Cook keep her seat “for now” while her earlier suit proceeds, leaving the deeper question of Federal Reserve independence unresolved; no removal had been finalized as of this writing, and Cook remains on the board. The renewed push comes as Trump publicly presses the Fed for deeper rate cuts. (Facts from NPR, Axios, SCOTUSblog and The Hill.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a manufactured “for cause” pretext to purge a governor — the first Black woman on the board — who won’t deliver the cheaper money the president wants, with stale mortgage paperwork as the flimsy cover. That the courts already paused this once, they argue, is the tell: this is about bending an institution built to be insulated from the White House. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Part of the right holds that credible mortgage-fraud allegations are disqualifying for a financial regulator regardless of who occupies the White House, and that “for cause” removal exists for exactly such conduct. But market-minded conservatives and libertarians (Reason-style) are uneasy: undermining Fed independence to force rate cuts is precisely the politicized money they warn against, and a case built on pre-appointment paperwork invites another court loss. (Directional.)

Sources: NPR, Axios, SCOTUSblog, The Hill

U.S.–Iran War · Sanctions

‘Operation Economic Outcast’: Washington escalates to an economic squeeze as oil shrugs and Trump calls the war a ‘little detour’

+

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent unveiled a sweeping new sanctions campaign against Iran — branded “Operation Economic Outcast” — hitting five sectors Washington deems critical to Tehran’s economy: digital assets, technology, gold, aviation and shipping. The Office of Foreign Assets Control designated more than 60 entities, individuals and vessels worldwide accused of helping Iran procure nuclear and missile technology, run cyber operations and move oil revenue. Bessent, who earlier promised an “economic D-Day,” cast the measures as pressure that has “never been seen” on the regime.

Markets read the package as less disruptive to physical oil flows than feared: WTI crude fell about 2.5% to roughly $85 a barrel and Brent slid a similar amount to about $92 on Aug. 24, while gold pushed to multi-month highs. On Aug. 26 Trump downplayed the conflict as a “little detour” and claimed U.S. financial strength would deliver “a very big victory,” saying he does not believe Iran’s supreme leader is dead but was “very seriously wounded.” Iran, for its part, appealed to the United Nations to investigate the humanitarian toll of the sanctions, calling the pressure campaign a violation of international law.

The economic front runs alongside the shipping standoff in the Gulf: Tehran has said the Strait of Hormuz will not fully reopen until Washington lifts its naval blockade, drops oil sanctions and unfreezes Iranian assets. For now the war has shifted from missiles to money — a slower, grinding phase whose bite on ordinary Iranians, and on global energy and gold markets, will take weeks to read. (Facts from CNN, CNBC and Al Jazeera.)

Sources: CNN, CNBC, Al Jazeera, CNN (Aug. 26)
Europe · Russia’s War on Ukraine

Russia’s ‘double-tap’ strikes intensify, now targeting the rescuers who respond to the first blast

+

Russian attacks killed at least four people and wounded roughly 95 across Ukraine over the latest 24-hour period, local authorities said on Aug. 25–26. In Kramatorsk, in eastern Donetsk region, a Russian first-person-view drone carried out a “double-tap” attack — striking an armored ambulance of the State Emergency Service as crews worked the aftermath of an earlier hit on a nine-story residential block. Rescuers said no personnel were injured in the ambulance strike, but the tactic of hitting first responders who arrive after an initial blast has become a recurring feature of the campaign.

The Kramatorsk incident fits a grim recent pattern: an Aug. 21 “double-tap” strike on a Ukrainian shopping center killed at least 14 and wounded dozens, and rights researchers have flagged the deliberate targeting of second responders as a likely war crime. Ukraine has kept up its own deep strikes, reportedly hitting a major Russian oil refinery and logistics sites inside Russia. With front lines largely static and talk of a possible “freeze” along current positions circulating on both sides, the near-term reality remains a war of attrition fought increasingly through drones and long-range strikes on rear areas. (Facts from The Kyiv Independent, Just Security and CNN.)

Fed Independence · Executive Power

Today is the White House deadline for Fed Governor Lisa Cook to answer Trump’s renewed bid to remove her over mortgage-fraud claims

+

Wednesday, Aug. 26 is the deadline the White House set for Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook to submit evidence answering President Trump’s renewed effort to remove her from the central bank’s board. In a letter dated Aug. 5 and reported Aug. 7, presidential-personnel aide Dan Scavino told Cook there is “sufficient reason to believe that you made false statements on one or more mortgage agreements” and gave her until Aug. 26 to respond before any final decision. The allegations — which predate her time at the Fed — are that in 2021 Cook listed both a Georgia condo and a Michigan house as her primary residence on mortgage applications to obtain more favorable terms. Cook denies wrongdoing; her attorney, Abbe Lowell, called the claims “as baseless now as they were a year ago,” and vowed to “challenge this latest pretext and preserve her position and the historic role of the Fed.”

The move revives a fight the Supreme Court paused on June 29, when it ruled Trump could not fire Cook “for now” while her lawsuit over an earlier removal attempt proceeds — a decision that left the deeper question of Federal Reserve independence unresolved. Firing a sitting Fed governor would be unprecedented in the institution’s history, and the renewed push comes as Trump has publicly pressed the Fed to cut interest rates more aggressively. No removal had been finalized as of this writing; Cook remains in her seat, and a fresh round of litigation is expected if the administration acts after the deadline. (Facts from NPR, CNBC, Axios, The Hill and SCOTUSblog.)

Stale, unproven mortgage paperwork, they argue, is the flimsy cover for politicizing the one institution meant to be insulated from the White House, and the courts have already had to say no once. (Directional.)

But the free-market and libertarian right (Reason-style, and market-minded conservatives) is uneasy: undermining Fed independence to bend rate policy is exactly the kind of politicized money they warn against, and a removal built on a pre-appointment paperwork dispute invites the court loss that stopped the first attempt. As of Aug. 26, some coverage signaled the two sides could still step back from an all-out trade war — and one report suggested Canada might drop some measures — but de-escalation was not confirmed. Elections · Voting Rights

DHS concedes its touted ‘250,000 noncitizen voters’ figure was inflated — a Nevada review confirms just 185

+

New reporting — led by The New York Times, with records and a call recording obtained by NPR through a public-records request — shows the Department of Homeland Security has effectively walked back its marquee claim, touted by Trump in a prime-time address, that the administration found more than 250,000 noncitizens on the voter rolls of four states (California, Nevada, New Jersey and Pennsylvania). In July, DHS said it had flagged about 15,903 noncitizens on Nevada’s rolls; on an Aug. 13 call, a DHS official told Nevada officials that figure was “the ceiling,” with each record still needing manual review. DHS then handed Nevada a list of just 185 registrants it said it was confident were noncitizens — and declined the state’s request for the underlying data or methodology.

CNN separately reported that of the roughly 250,000 touted, only about 118,003 across the four states had matched name, date of birth, address and Social Security number to a noncitizen in DHS records — a preliminary match, not a confirmation. More than a month after the claim, the four states say they are still waiting for proof; election experts note noncitizen voting is vanishingly rare, and David Becker of the Center for Election Innovation & Research said the figures “should be considered made up” absent a transparent methodology. Several Democratic-led states have sued over federal demands for their voter rolls and, per CNN, have prevailed in all 16 suits so far. (Facts from NPR, The New York Times, CNN and The Hill.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees the collapse from “250,000” to 185 as proof the bombshell was manufactured. Noncitizen voting is statistically negligible, they argue, so the campaign’s real aim is to justify aggressive roll purges, intimidate immigrant communities, and pre-seed a “rigged election” narrative before the midterms. The refusal to share any methodology is, for them, the tell. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The administration and GOP allies frame the states — not DHS — as the obstacle: Democratic-led states, they say, are stonewalling by refusing to hand over their rolls and litigating to block access, making verification impossible, and even a modest number of confirmed noncitizens warrants cleaner rolls. Mainstream conservative opinion outlets were largely quiet on the walkback itself; no dedicated National Review, Dispatch or WSJ editorial defended the specific numbers in the window. (Directional.)

Sources: NPR, CNN, The Hill, NPR (background)

Science · Astronomy

A JWST survey of 72 young stars maps how planet-forming disks lose their gas — shifting from molecular to atomic winds as systems age

+

New James Webb Space Telescope results released Aug. 25 show how gas escapes the planet-forming disks around young, Sun-like stars — and how the escape mechanism changes over a disk’s lifetime. Using JWST’s Mid-Infrared Instrument (MIRI), a team led by Naman Bajaj (University of Arizona), with SETI Institute co-author Uma Gorti, studied a sample of 72 stars and found the balance among high-speed jets, slower molecular winds and atomic winds shifts systematically as a system matures, with different wind types dominating at different evolutionary stages.

Because these winds carry away the raw material planets form from, the finding bears on how long the planet-building window stays open. This is a peer-reviewed result — the paper, “JWST/MIRI Reveals the Evolution from Molecular to Atomic Disk Winds,” appears in The Astronomical Journal — not a proof-of-concept; the team frames the next step, quantifying exactly how much gas the winds remove and from where in the disk, as still open. (Facts from the SETI Institute, Phys.org and the arXiv preprint.)

Sources: SETI Institute, Phys.org, arXiv preprint

Energy & Climate · Grid

Solar carried Europe’s grids through the summer’s heatwaves — but ‘the real challenge starts after sundown,’ Ember finds

+

Analysis from the energy think-tank Ember, with data refreshed around Aug. 24, quantifies how solar power stabilized European grids as repeated heatwaves drove up cooling demand. During the peak heat, daily electricity demand rose by up to ~28% in Italy, ~23% in Hungary, ~14% in France and ~13% in Spain versus pre-heatwave days. Solar was the only major source to outperform its norm through the extreme heat — average daily solar generation ran about 17% higher in France and Hungary — helping cover midday peaks even as some nuclear and thermal output faltered under the high temperatures.

The core caveat is temporal: cooling demand stays high into the evening after solar fades, raising exposure to expensive gas-fired generation and underscoring the case for storage. This is an observational analysis of the summer’s grid performance rather than a new technology or policy action; the underlying report was published in mid-to-late August. (Facts from Ember, pv magazine, RenewEconomy and Euronews.)

Science · Evolutionary Genomics

The largest-ever comparison of animal genomes finds chromosomes evolve along a few irreversible ‘evolutionary highways’

+

Researchers at the University of Vienna analyzed more than 5,800 chromosome-scale genomes spanning 4,454 species across 19 animal phyla — the biggest such comparison to date — and report that chromosome architecture does not reshuffle at random. Instead, genomes follow a limited set of directional, largely irreversible paths the authors call “evolutionary highways,” captured by a new framework they term evolutionary genome topology that projects the whole dataset onto a single map.

The work traces recognizable shared chromosomal blocks from a common animal ancestor more than 600 million years ago across lineages as different as humans, octopuses and corals, and the authors argue it offers a reference map useful for biodiversity conservation. It is a peer-reviewed result, published in Science Advances; the paper appeared around Aug. 20, with broad science-press coverage running Aug. 24–25. (Facts from Science Advances, Phys.org and EurekAlert.)

Sources: Science Advances, Phys.org, EurekAlert
