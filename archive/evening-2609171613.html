Midterms · The Crime Card

Trump opens his midterm push in North Carolina, running on crime for a candidate who’s 11 points down

+

President Trump held his first big midterm rally of the cycle in Gastonia, North Carolina, on Wednesday evening, stumping for Republican Senate nominee Michael Whatley, the former RNC chair who is trailing Democratic former governor Roy Cooper by roughly 11 points (Elon University/YouGov). Trump built the hour-long speech around crime, calling North Carolina “one of the most violent” states, invoking the 1988 “Willie Horton” ad, and repeatedly citing the killing of Ukrainian refugee Iryna Zarutska on a Charlotte train to argue Democrats coddle criminals.

The choice of message is itself the story: with the Fed’s rate hike, an unpopular war and a double-digit deficit in this race, Trump is betting the midterm turn on law-and-order rather than the economy that dominated the morning’s NYT/Siena numbers. Fact-checkers noted crime in the state runs below Trump’s characterization. The rally kicks off a campaign sprint just as the House left town until after November. (Poll margins and crime statistics vary by source and timeframe.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads it as a Willie Horton rerun — a racialized fear pitch that seizes on one horrific killing while overall crime trends down, deployed precisely because the president can’t sell inflation or the war. The tell, they argue, is Whatley’s 11-point hole: if fear were working, the map wouldn’t look like this. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right insists Democratic leniency — cashless bail, permissive prosecutors — is a real vulnerability, with the Charlotte transit killing as Exhibit A that Cooper must answer for. But candid conservatives (The Dispatch, National Review) concede Whatley is a weak candidate and crime won’t close an economy-driven gap; Reason warns against federalizing local law-and-order. The Bulwark (anti-Trump right, a dissent) calls it demagoguery over a tragedy. (Directional.)

Sources: Washington Post (partial — paywalled), Roll Call, NBC News, Spectrum News (fact-check)
Congress · AI & Power Bills

House passes the Ratepayer Protection Act 417–3 — then adjourns until after the midterms

+

The House on Wednesday night passed the Ratepayer Protection Act by a lopsided 417–3, its first major AI-related bill, aimed at keeping the electricity costs of the data-center boom off household bills. The measure sets a framework states may adopt to require AI data centers drawing 100 megawatts or more to pay for the new power plants, transmission and infrastructure they need, rather than socializing those costs onto ratepayers. The only “no” votes came from three progressives — Reps. Rashida Tlaib, Summer Lee and Delia Ramirez — who argued it doesn’t go far enough; Tlaib called instead for a national moratorium on data centers.

The politics undercut the tally. Speaker Mike Johnson promptly canceled Thursday’s votes and sent the House home until after November, and in the Senate, Democrat Martin Heinrich blocked a bid by sponsor Sen. John Husted (R-Ohio) to fast-track it by unanimous consent — all but ensuring the bill stalls before Election Day. A rare bipartisan swing at a real pocketbook grievance thus doubles as a pre-recess messaging vote. (Bill remains far from becoming law.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left welcomes a populist win — make Big Tech, not families, pay for the AI power surge — but splits over whether an opt-in framework has teeth. Progressives who voted no (Tlaib) want a moratorium, not a permission slip; and with Johnson adjourning the House and a Senate Democrat’s own procedural block leaving it stranded, critics call it good politics with little enforceable law. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Consumer-minded conservatives (Daily Signal framing it “Big Tech, not consumers”) like forcing data centers to internalize their own costs while preserving state choice. Market libertarians (Reason) are warier: mandated cost-allocation edges toward energy price controls that distort the grid and could chill exactly the AI buildout Washington says it wants to win. (Directional.)

Sources: NBC News, CNBC, Tech Times, Daily Signal

Iran War · International Law

A UN mission finds ‘reasonable grounds’ the U.S. committed war crimes in Iran — a school strike that killed some 120 children

+

A UN Human Rights Council fact-finding mission, chaired by Bangladeshi jurist Sara Hossain, said Thursday it has “reasonable grounds to believe” the United States committed war crimes in Iran. It centered on the Feb. 28 strike on the Shajareh Tayyebeh primary school in Minab — carried out, the mission said, with Tomahawk missiles — which killed more than 150 people, roughly 120 of them children. Across two strikes cited, the mission counted at least 178 civilians killed, concluding the U.S. failed its obligation to verify the school was a military objective.

The report is not a court verdict, and Washington disputes such findings, but it lands as a significant legal marker mid-war — and a politically awkward one, arriving the same day the president spoke of winding the conflict down. Notably, the same mission also accused Iran of crimes against humanity in its 2025–26 crackdown on protesters — internet shutdowns, unlawful killings, torture and enforced disappearances — underscoring that its remit cut in both directions. (A fact-finding mission establishes grounds for belief, not criminal liability.)

Sources: UN News, NPR, NBC News, Al Jazeera

+

The episode sharpens the central European question of the moment: how the alliance answers gray-zone attacks engineered to stay just below the Article 5 threshold. The Fed · Independence Fight

Warsh hikes, Trump erupts: the first rate rise since 2023 reopens the war over the Fed’s independence

+

A day after the Federal Reserve voted unanimously, 12–0, to raise its benchmark rate a quarter-point to 3.75%–4.00% — the first hike since 2023 — the story on Sept. 16–17 became the political fallout. Chair Kevin Warsh, President Trump’s own pick, justified the move by saying inflation had been running too high for too long, and the Fed’s new projections show 16 of 18 officials expecting another increase later this year. Analysts across CNN, CNBC and Bloomberg read the hike as an unambiguous message from Warsh: hands off the central bank.

Trump answered almost immediately, posting a demand to “LOWER THE INTEREST RATES…AND FAST!” and later saying he “wouldn’t have chosen Warsh” for the job if he had wanted rate hikes — his sharpest, if still indirect, shot yet at the chief he installed in January after souring on Jerome Powell. A White House spokesman called the decision “rather unfortunate.” The confrontation revives the central question hanging over this administration: whether an independent Fed can hold the line while the president publicly demands cheap money. The proximate driver is the Iran-war oil shock keeping headline inflation elevated. (Dot-plot counts and market levels move; figures are as reported around the decision.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left is split-screen: uneasy that Warsh is tightening into a supply shock the Fed can’t drill away — squeezing mortgages and jobs to fight a war-driven oil spike — but quietly relieved that an institution is refusing to bend to a president openly bullying it for cheap credit. The through-line: defend the Fed’s independence, and end the war that’s actually driving prices. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

A sharp intra-right split. Institutionalist conservatives (WSJ editorial) and libertarians (Reason) cheer Warsh for defending Fed credibility against a 1970s-style inflation drift. The Trump-aligned, easy-money right is furious that the president’s own pick hiked into a slowdown before the midterms and reads it as betrayal. The Bulwark’s anti-Trump conservatives (a dissent, not the mainstream view) frame Warsh’s defiance as exactly the guardrail the moment demanded. (Directional; opinion desks not individually reachable this cycle.)

Midterms · Polling

Trump sinks to 38% as Democrats open an 8-point midterm lead in the NYT/Siena poll

+

A new New York Times/Siena College poll, conducted Sept. 8–13 among 1,503 likely voters (margin of error ±2.9 points), lands President Trump’s approval at 38%, with 52% strongly disapproving. On the generic congressional ballot, 51% of likely voters back the Democrat versus 43% the Republican — an eight-to-nine-point edge — and Democrats lead by roughly 23 points among independents (57%–34%).

The more striking finding is the breadth: likely voters disapproved of Trump’s handling of every major issue tested, from the economy and the cost of living to the foreign wars, immigration and health care. Democrats are now more trusted on pocketbook issues that had recently favored Republicans — a notable reversal that both parties will read as a verdict on inflation, the oil shock and an unpopular war. With the midterms still more than a year out, the numbers are a snapshot, not a result. (Cross-tabs and topline vary slightly across write-ups of the same survey.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees vindication: war, inflation and executive overreach catching up with a president who governed as if he had a mandate. The emphasis is that the backlash is broad-based — independents, the economy, the wars — while cautioning that polls aren’t votes and that gerrymanders and turnout still stand between this lead and a House majority. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right waves off an out-year poll (“a year is a lifetime”) and questions the sample, but the more candid conservative voices (The Dispatch, National Review) treat it as a real warning: the economy — oil, rates, cost of living — is the anchor, and complacency plus weak candidates could hand Democrats the House. The anti-Trump right (The Bulwark — a dissent) reads it as the country recoiling from the whole project. (Directional.)

Iran · UN Security Council

A year after ‘snapback,’ the Security Council splits again over who still holds Iran to account

+

The UN Security Council returned this week to the wreckage of the Iran nuclear file. After the E3 — France, Germany and the United Kingdom — triggered the JCPOA’s “snapback” in August 2025 and previously lifted UN sanctions were reimposed on Sept. 27, 2025, the Council remains deadlocked over whether those measures — and the machinery that enforces them — are still valid. In a September session, members surmounted a procedural vote (11–2, two abstentions) to keep the 1737 sanctions committee on the agenda, defeating a Russia-China bid to drop the item entirely.

Russia, China and Iran argue the snapback mechanism has expired and that the Council “cannot proceed to reestablish” the committee; the E3 counter that only specific paragraphs of Resolution 2231 lapsed. The practical result is an oversight vacuum: with the Council split over even whether sanctions are in force, there is no agreed mechanism to monitor Tehran’s program — precisely as the 2026 Iran war grinds on and IAEA access remains contested. The dispute is now as much about the Council’s own authority as about Iran. (Vote tallies and procedural framing as reported by the UN and Security Council Report.)

Iran & Russia · Two War Zones

Moscow feeds Tehran targeting data and hypersonic know-how as two wars converge

+

Intelligence assessments surfacing this month describe a rapidly deepening Russia–Iran military partnership now spanning both the Ukraine and Iran wars. Russia is reported to be sending Iran upgraded munitions, satellite imagery and targeting data usable against American forces in the Middle East, and — per a Financial Times investigation — running a covert program (codename C430L) since 2023 to help Tehran develop ramjet-powered supersonic and hypersonic cruise missiles, work that continued into 2026. The two governments formalized ties with a 20-year strategic treaty in 2025.

The relationship is reciprocal and, analysts warn, self-reinforcing: Iran armed Russia with Shahed drones for Ukraine; Russia is now repaying with precision and propulsion expertise pointed at U.S. warships. The Institute for the Study of War cautions that Russian-supplied targeting intelligence could make future Iranian drone and missile attacks “substantially more sophisticated” than past campaigns — a quiet but consequential fusion of two conflicts into a single, shared arsenal. (Based on intelligence assessments and FT reporting; both governments dispute or decline to confirm specifics.)

Energy · Batteries & Storage

The battery industry pivots: energy-storage shipments nearly doubled as EV demand wobbles

+

The global battery business is entering a more competitive — and more diversified — phase in 2026. As EV demand turns uneven and cell prices keep falling, manufacturers are leaning hard into stationary energy storage as a second demand engine. Global energy-storage cell shipments reached roughly 612 GWh in 2025, nearly double the year before, and the lines between EV and grid-storage manufacturing are increasingly converging on the same factories.

The scale-up is capital-heavy: more than $130 billion in U.S. gigafactory investment has been announced (LG Energy Solution, Samsung SDI, SK On, Panasonic), while CATL remains the world’s largest maker at about 260 GWh and a 37% share, ahead of BYD’s FinDreams (~111 GWh, 16%). Europe is emerging as a major EV growth center even as U.S. and Chinese producers hedge toward storage. The takeaway: batteries increasingly power the grid, not just the car. (Figures are industry-tracker estimates and forecasts, not spot readings.)

Climate · The Arctic

The Arctic hit its 2026 sea-ice minimum on Sept 10 — after a summer that shadowed the record-melt year of 2012

+

The National Snow and Ice Data Center announced on Sept. 17 that Arctic sea ice reached its 2026 annual minimum on Sept. 10, at 4.60 million square kilometers — another among the lowest extents in the satellite record. For much of the summer the 2026 melt tracked uncomfortably close to 2012, the single most catastrophic year on record, before a persistent low-pressure system parked over the high Arctic slowed the season’s momentum and left 2012’s record intact, arguably by luck.

The science is arriving under institutional strain. NSIDC’s widely used Sea Ice Today narrative nearly went dark after 2025 funding cuts before support was restored just in time for the minimum, and NOAA terminated backing for the annual Arctic Report Card in August, with the American Geophysical Union, CU Boulder and the University of Alaska Fairbanks now stepping in to keep it alive. The monitoring, in other words, is becoming as fragile as the ice it tracks. (Extent figures reflect NSIDC methodology; other agencies report slightly different numbers.)

Science · Physics

Physicists watch Einstein’s gravity reach into the quantum world — with an atom in two places at once

+

An international team has directly observed a long-predicted quantum effect of gravity, testing one of Einstein’s foundational ideas in a new regime. Using an atom interferometer, researchers split the quantum wavefunction of ultracold rubidium atoms so that one part fell freely under gravity while the other was held stationary, then recombined them to measure the tiny quantum phase difference that emerged. The measured phase matched the predictions of Einstein’s equivalence principle — showing the principle holds even for matter in a quantum superposition.

The work — led by Ben-Gurion University, the University of Ulm and the University of Oxford, with a team that included Nobel laureate Sir Roger Penrose and published in Science Advances — does not unify gravity with quantum mechanics or prove gravity is itself quantum. But it demonstrates a technique precise enough to probe where the two great theories meet, and the authors say it could be extended to heavier objects such as nanodiamonds. (Single-study result; the finding tests, rather than overturns, existing theory.)
