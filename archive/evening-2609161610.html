The Fed · Decision Day

Warsh hikes and defies Trump: the Fed raises rates for the first time since 2023 — unanimously

+

The Federal Reserve did what this morning’s brief said was only a coin-flip likelihood: at 2 p.m. ET the FOMC voted unanimously, 12–0, to raise its benchmark rate a quarter-point to a 3.75%–4.00% target range — the first hike since 2023. Chair Kevin Warsh, whom President Trump handpicked expecting cuts, said inflation had been “too high for too long” and that the Fed “must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective clearly and at sufficient speed.” He made no reference to politics and reaffirmed the Fed’s independence.

Fresh projections show 16 of 18 officials expect another hike later this year, with no increases penciled in for the following years and modest cuts sketched for 2028–29. The response was immediate: Trump posted “LOWER THE INTEREST RATES FOR THE UNITED STATES OF AMERICA, AND FAST!” within the hour, and a White House spokesman called the move “rather unfortunate” and not “backed by a particularly compelling economic case.” The hike lands squarely on the war-driven oil shock keeping headline inflation above target. (Dot-plot counts and market levels move; figures are as reported at the decision.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left is split-screen: uneasy that Warsh is tightening into a supply shock the Fed can’t drill away — squeezing mortgages and jobs to fight a war-driven oil spike — but quietly relieved that an institution is refusing to bend to a president openly demanding cheap money. The through-line: end the war that’s driving prices, and don’t let the White House bully the central bank. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

A sharp intra-right split. Institutionalist conservatives (WSJ editorial) and libertarians (Reason) cheer Warsh for defending Fed credibility and independence against a 1970s-style inflation drift. The Trump-aligned, easy-money right is furious — the president’s own pick just hiked into a slowdown — and reads it as a betrayal that will cost growth before the midterms. The Bulwark’s anti-Trump conservatives (a dissent, not the mainstream view) frame Warsh’s defiance as exactly the guardrail the moment required. (Directional.)

Sources: CNBC, Fox Business, CNN Business, Bloomberg (partial — paywalled)
War Powers · Capitol Hill

House votes a third time to end the Iran war as Massie moves to impeach Hegseth

+

The domestic fight over the Iran war escalated on Capitol Hill. The House voted 220–204 to invoke the War Powers Resolution and halt US military action against Iran without congressional authorization — the third such vote since the spring. A few more Republicans crossed over than before: seven joined all Democrats, including Reps. Zach Nunn and Mariannette Miller-Meeks of Iowa, Nancy Mace of South Carolina, Brian Fitzpatrick of Pennsylvania, Tom Barrett, Warren Davidson and Thomas Massie. As with the earlier measures, it is not expected to reach the president’s desk, where Trump would veto it.

Separately, Rep. Massie (R-Ky.) read eight articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth on the House floor, accusing him of illegally starting and continuing the war, defying Congress, causing needless civilian casualties, retaliating against a member of Congress over protected speech, and directing unauthorized operations in Venezuela and Yemen. Because the resolution is privileged, Massie can force a floor vote and put Republicans on the record; the effort is a long shot in a GOP-controlled chamber. Massie said he acted now because “Speaker Johnson has basically canceled the rest of Congress until the elections.” (Roll-call totals and article counts as reported; procedural timing was still moving.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a lawless, open-ended war being waged with no congressional authorization and mounting civilian toll — and takes grim satisfaction that even a handful of Republicans and a conservative like Massie are now forcing accountability. The emphasis: the votes may be symbolic, but they build the record that Trump and Hegseth defied the Constitution. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream and MAGA-aligned right largely dismisses the war-powers votes as grandstanding that undermines the commander-in-chief mid-conflict, and treats impeaching Hegseth as a doomed stunt. But this is a genuine intra-right fracture: the constitutionalist and libertarian right (Reason, and Massie himself) argue Congress alone can declare war and that executive war-making is exactly what conservatives should check; the anti-Trump right (The Bulwark — a dissent, not the mainstream view) backs the accountability push outright. (Directional.)

Iran War · Hormuz & Oil

Oil eases off its highs even as Hormuz stays effectively shut — the tanker war grinds into a seventh month

+

The oil picture that has driven this cycle’s inflation eased slightly on Wednesday: Brent crude fell to about $105 a barrel, down from roughly $108 on Tuesday, as some acute supply fears cooled. But the underlying blockade endures. Traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remains at a reported ~2% of pre-war levels — effectively closed — with roughly a single tanker a day transiting, and the US military has escorted only limited volumes (about 18 million barrels of oil and products) out of the Gulf. Iran continues to demand that commercial vessels follow Tehran-set routes and fees; the US answers attempted attacks on shipping with strikes on IRGC-linked targets and tankers.

The war, launched Feb. 28 by the United States and Israel, has now dragged past six months with no diplomatic off-ramp, even as the House votes repeatedly to end it (see Politics). The modest price dip does not change the structural squeeze: with the Saudi east–west pipeline still down and Hormuz throttled, the market has no slack, which is precisely why the Federal Reserve felt compelled to hike into a slowing economy today. The analytical bottom line is a war that has settled into an attritional maritime stalemate — painful for the global economy, decisive for neither side. (Price levels move intraday; throughput and export figures rest on tracking-firm and agency estimates and are contested.)

Russia & NATO · The Frontier

NATO jets down a drone over Lithuania as the war’s edge presses harder against the alliance

+

While this morning’s brief tracked the stalled US peace push, the more urgent development is on NATO’s frontier. NATO fighter jets shot down a drone in Lithuanian airspace overnight, destroying it near the village of Pratčūnai, about 100 km west of Vilnius; preliminary data indicated the drone had crossed in from Belarus. Poland separately announced it had launched “preventive” aviation operations to secure its airspace against Russian jet-powered drones striking Ukraine, and a Russian frigate was reported to have fired flares at a Danish helicopter.

The incidents come a day after NATO’s leadership warned that strikes close to alliance territory would only deepen its support for Ukraine, and amid a widening pattern — drone incursions, sabotage, arson and surveillance — that Germany, Denmark, the UK and Poland have attributed to Russia in recent weeks. Foreign Minister Lavrov paired the pressure with warnings about conflict with Europe, even as Putin insists Moscow poses “no threat.” The analytical takeaway: the risk now is less a breakdown at the negotiating table than an accident or miscalculation on the alliance’s eastern edge dragging NATO directly into the war. (Attribution of individual drones is preliminary; Moscow disputes involvement.)

AI Policy · Washington

Trump calls AI fears a ‘hoax’ as Congress trains its fire on data-center bills, not the frontier

+

President Trump on Monday, Sept. 14, dismissed warnings about advanced artificial intelligence as “a hoax,” said the technology does not need federal guardrails, and alleged a “sick conspiracy” against AI and data centers — landing days after a weekend in which AI leaders including Anthropic’s Dario Amodei and OpenAI’s Sam Altman urged governments and companies to slow, or “pace,” the frontier. The president has framed rapid buildout as an economic win, telling Nvidia’s Jensen Huang that “data centers are great, and they make people wealthy.”

On Capitol Hill, the action is narrower than the doomsday debate. Lawmakers this week focused on the costs data centers push onto neighbors: the House took up the bipartisan Ratepayer Protection Act, aimed at keeping utilities from passing data-center power costs to nearby households. Two safety-focused bipartisan efforts are also circulating — a Frontier Act that would require major AI developers to publish safety plans, report serious incidents and submit to independent evaluation, and a separate “kill switch” bill letting the government order a dangerous model slowed or shut down — but leaders in both chambers have signaled that sweeping AI regulation is unlikely in the near term. (Legislative status is provisional; bill texts and floor timing were shifting through Sept. 15.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads “hoax” as a president siding with data-center money over the public — waving away safety, grid strain and ratepayer costs to protect a favored industry, even as the people building the technology beg for guardrails. The emphasis: pass the ratepayer and Frontier bills, and don’t let “beat China” become an excuse for zero accountability. (Directional; opinion desks not individually reachable this cycle.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The MAGA-aligned right cheers a deregulatory, pro-growth stance and dismisses “doomer” alarmism as a bid for Washington control of a strategic race. But there is a live intra-right split: national-conservative and populist voices want the ratepayer protections (voters, not neighbors, shouldn’t eat the bill), Reason’s libertarians distrust a government “kill switch” as much as heavy mandates, and the anti-Trump right (The Bulwark — a dissent, not the mainstream view) calls “hoax” a reckless brush-off of real risk. (Directional.)

The Fed · Rate Decision Day

Warsh’s Fed is set to hike into a war-driven oil shock — the first rate rise since 2023

+

The Federal Reserve announces its rate decision at 2 p.m. ET today, Sept. 16, and markets lean toward a quarter-point hike to a 3.75%–4.00% target range — what would be the first increase since 2023. The pivot follows Chair Kevin Warsh’s hawkish Jackson Hole address on Aug. 28; the Fed held at 3.50%–3.75% in a divided 9–3 vote in July. Futures pricing has hovered around a coin-flip-to-likely move (reported near 57%), making it one of the more contested decisions in recent memory.

The driver is the energy shock from the Iran war: crude has pushed past $100 a barrel and diesel has run toward $6 a gallon, keeping headline inflation elevated (about 3.4% year-over-year in August, with core near 2.4%) even as growth cools. The 10-year Treasury yield has climbed to roughly 4.8%, its highest since late 2023, and equities have slid. The dilemma is textbook and brutal: a supply-side price spike the Fed cannot drill away, versus the risk that letting inflation expectations drift un-anchors the dollar’s credibility. (Rate odds and market levels are as of the Sept. 15 close and move intraday; the decision itself was pending at publication.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left warns against hiking into a shock the Fed didn’t cause: raising rates to fight an oil spike squeezes workers, mortgages and jobs for a war of choice, when the real fix is ending the conflict that’s driving prices. Blame, in this frame, sits with the war — not with demand that needs crushing. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Institutionalist hawks (WSJ editorial) back Warsh: credibility is everything, and a Fed that blinks at 3-handle inflation invites a 1970s spiral. But the Trump-aligned, easy-money right wants cuts and cheap credit and will hammer Warsh if a hike deepens a slowdown — a real fault line between the president’s pressure for low rates and the central bank’s inflation hawks. (Directional.)

Russia’s War · Diplomacy Stalls

The US peace push limps on as Russia keeps pounding Kyiv: ‘the war will continue’

+

President Trump’s envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner spent the first part of September shuttling between Moscow and Kyiv — a three-hour Kremlin meeting with Putin, then their first wartime visit to the Ukrainian capital — to revive trilateral talks frozen for roughly eight months. No breakthrough emerged: Witkoff said he was “confident there’ll be a good resolution,” and President Zelensky reported “several new ideas” from “good discussions,” but Ukrainian and Russian assessments landed closer to “the war will continue.” A rare trip by CIA chief John Ratcliffe reportedly failed to move Putin and may have hardened his resolve to seek a battlefield outcome.

The diplomacy is unfolding under fire. A short mutual pause on strikes against each other’s capitals — arranged to protect the US negotiators — gave way to renewed Russian barrages, and on Sept. 15 a fresh attack damaged four districts of Kyiv, with roughly eight people injured, fires at a gas station and a non-residential building, and falling debris across the city; earlier barrages this month killed several, including rail workers. Putin, meanwhile, publicly insisted Russia poses “no threat” to Europe even as drones and missiles crept up to NATO’s frontier. (Casualty figures are preliminary and rest partly on Ukrainian officials; Moscow’s account differs.)

Iran War · Hormuz & Oil

A severed Saudi pipeline and a US blockade keep the Hormuz oil war grinding — and prices above $100

+

More than six months into the 2026 Iran war — which began Feb. 28 and drew in a US aerial campaign from mid-March to reopen the Strait of Hormuz — the maritime standoff shows no sign of resolving. Iran continues to demand that commercial vessels follow Tehran-set routes and fees through the strait; the US answers attempted attacks on shipping with fresh strikes on IRGC targets, and its naval blockade had by early September halted new Iranian oil exports from the Persian Gulf. The Sept. 11 shutdown of Saudi Arabia’s east–west crude pipeline — the workaround that lets Gulf oil bypass Hormuz — tightened an already strangled market.

The economic fallout is now global and domestic at once. The IEA has characterized the episode as the largest supply disruption in the history of the oil market; crude sits above $100 a barrel, US pump and diesel prices have jumped, and the shock is the central reason the Federal Reserve is weighing a rate hike today. With Iran ruling out talks while strikes continue and Washington holding the blockade, the war’s off-ramp looks as distant as ever. (Disruption and export figures rest on agency and tracking-firm estimates and remain provisional; combatant claims are contested.)

Energy · Grid & Storage

Batteries become the grid’s fastest-growing power tech — 108 GW added worldwide in a single year

+

Grid-scale batteries are now the fastest-growing power technology on the planet, according to the IEA’s Electricity 2026 analysis: about 108 GW of new battery storage was deployed globally in 2025, roughly 40% more than the year before. Utility-scale storage has become a major source of short-term flexibility — absorbing midday solar surpluses and discharging into the evening peak — with the steepest growth in high-renewables systems including California, Texas, Germany, South Australia and the United Kingdom.

The storage surge tracks a broader tilt in what gets built. US forecasts point to a record year for new capacity dominated overwhelmingly by solar, wind and batteries, and the IEA expects global energy investment near $3.4 trillion in 2026, including roughly $665 billion for renewable power. Storage is increasingly the piece that lets variable generation actually run the grid rather than merely feed it. (Figures are IEA/EIA estimates and forecasts, not spot readings.)

Science · Physics & Math

The ‘Einstein’ tile that never repeats turns out to twist light into pinwheels

+

The aperiodic monotile — the single “einstein” shape discovered in 2023 that tiles a plane without ever repeating — has revealed an unexpected physical trick. Researchers report that structures patterned on the monotile twist light into chiral patterns: when laser light was shone through them, the diffraction formed distinctive pinwheel-like shapes that directly exposed the handedness of the underlying aperiodic order.

The result, covered Sept. 14–15, matters because chirality — a built-in left- or right-handedness — is usually engineered deliberately; here it emerges from pure geometry. The authors suggest monotile-inspired structures could eventually feed technologies that manipulate light, control polarization and build advanced optical devices, turning a piece of recreational mathematics into a substrate for photonics. (Single-study result; awaiting independent replication.)

Sources: ScienceDaily
Biomedicine · Brain Aging

Alzheimer’s may hide in the genome’s folds — and the brain reorganizes sharply between 50 and 75

+

Two new studies point to the aging brain’s genome architecture as an under-appreciated player in neurodegeneration. In one, scientists found that the three-dimensional folding of DNA is disrupted in several brain-cell types affected by Alzheimer’s disease, altering which genes get switched on and off — a “new layer” of the disease that sits above the familiar plaques and tangles.

A companion line of work describes sweeping changes in how the brain controls and organizes its genome beginning in midlife, with a notable shift between roughly ages 50 and 75. Together they reframe part of dementia risk as a problem of genome regulation in aging cells, potentially opening targets that don’t depend solely on clearing protein deposits. (Early-stage findings, published Sept. 12–14; mechanisms and therapeutic relevance remain to be established.)

Sources: ScienceDaily (genome), ScienceDaily (midlife brain)
