Executive Power

A term that reshaped the presidency: Court hands Trump control of the "independent" agencies

+

As the dust settles on a Supreme Court term that ended June 30, the through-line is a marked expansion of presidential control over the executive branch. In a 6–3 decision on June 29, the Court let Trump remove FTC commissioner Rebecca Slaughter, effectively overturning the 1935 precedent Humphrey's Executor and turning heads of long-independent, bipartisan agencies into officials who serve at the president's pleasure. The Court carved out one exception, ruling 5–4 that Federal Reserve governor Lisa Cook may keep her seat while her case proceeds — a signal that the justices see central-bank independence as distinct.

The wins were not total: this week the Court also rejected Trump's bid to end birthright citizenship and earlier ruled against his tariff authority. But analysts across outlets read the term as one that weakened Congress and enlarged the presidency. In dissent, Justice Sotomayor wrote that the result is "a President who emerges with far greater power than ever before … a power, however, that neither the People, nor Congress, nor the Constitution bestowed upon him."

The Left-Leaning Take

A slow-motion gutting of the administrative state (MSNBC, Vox): agencies built to be shielded from partisan capture — the FTC, FCC, NLRB — are now extensions of the White House, and consumer, labor and antitrust enforcement will bend to whoever holds the Oval Office. Congress, they argue, must legislate new protections or watch its own design dismantled.

The Right-Leaning Take

A long-overdue correction (National Review, WSJ editorial): under a unitary-executive reading of Article II, the president must be able to control those who wield executive power, and Humphrey's Executor was an anomaly. The Fed carve-out shows the Court is drawing principled lines, not simply rubber-stamping Trump.

Sources: NPR, Washington Post, Axios, CNN
Immigration

ICE arrests hit 10,000 in five days as a 2,000-a-day quota takes hold

+

Immigration and Customs Enforcement detained more than 10,000 people over five days, a surge driven by an internal directive that 2,000 arrests a day is the new standard — up from about 1,000 earlier this year and roughly 300 a day before Trump took office. Officials say the White House pushed for higher numbers; ICE was told to put as many officers as possible on arrest operations, with many working seven days a week and up to 80% assigned to the field.

The campaign is deliberately quieter than 2025's high-profile city sweeps: arrests are happening at check-ins, courthouses, traffic stops and on the street rather than through pre-announced operations. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin pledged the lower-key approach after a monthlong Minnesota operation in which federal officers killed two U.S. citizens.

The Left-Leaning Take

Arrest quotas invert due process (Mother Jones, Vox): when officers are graded on volume, the incentive is to grab whoever is reachable — people with no criminal record, at check-ins they attended in good faith — not to prioritize public-safety threats. The "quieter" tactics are a feature, designed to blunt the backlash that visible raids provoked.

The Right-Leaning Take

A mandate being enforced (National Review, WSJ editorial): voters returned Trump to restore immigration control, and moving from symbolic sweeps to steady, unglamorous enforcement is how a real deterrent is built. The libertarian dissent (Reason): quotas plus courthouse arrests risk sweeping up the lawful along with the unlawful, and warrantless street stops invite abuse.

Sources: Philadelphia Inquirer, Islander News, TRAC
Ethics & the Presidency

Trump's 2025 income topped $2.2 billion, with $1.4 billion from crypto

+

Newly released financial disclosures — nearly 1,000 pages — show President Trump reported roughly $2.2 billion in total income in 2025, his first year back in office and more than triple the prior year. About $1.4 billion came from cryptocurrency and meme-coin ventures, including some $635 million tied to a coin business and more than $500 million from the World Liberty Financial crypto firm; another line item was $10.71 million in licensing fees connected to an Amazon MGM documentary about the First Lady.

The disclosures land as the administration has loosened federal restrictions on the crypto industry, and ethics specialists warn of the conflict inherent in a sitting president earning fortunes from a sector his government regulates. The White House has said the president's assets are managed by his children and that he is focused on the country, not his businesses.

The Left-Leaning Take

Monetizing the office in plain sight (Mother Jones, Slate): a president deregulating crypto while banking more than a billion from crypto is the textbook definition of a conflict of interest, and "the kids run it" is no firewall when policy moves the very assets the family holds.

The Right-Leaning Take

Mixed. Disclosure hawks on the right (Reason, some at The Dispatch) are uneasy about a president profiting from a lightly regulated, politically favored industry. Others note the holdings are disclosed and legal, that Trump entered office wealthy, and that "he's rich" is not itself a scandal absent evidence of a specific quid pro quo.

Sources: Washington Post, PBS NewsHour, Union-Bulletin (wire)

Ukraine

One of the war's biggest barrages hits Kyiv; at least 22 killed

+

Russia struck Ukraine overnight into July 2 with 74 missiles and 496 drones — among the largest aerial assaults of the war. Ukrainian officials said at least 22 people were killed and dozens injured, with more than 130 sites damaged across Kyiv. A missile tore a gaping hole through a nine-storey residential block, destroying 64 apartments; other hits included an ambulance station, a scientific institute, a hotel and a publishing warehouse where roughly 800,000 books were destroyed. At least two children were among the wounded.

The barrage follows weeks of tit-for-tat escalation, with Ukraine striking Russian oil and energy infrastructure and Moscow answering by saturating Ukrainian cities. The scale of the assault — and the destruction of civilian and cultural sites — underscores how far the front has drifted from the diplomatic overtures floated earlier this year.

Sources: Al Jazeera, NPR
Iran

U.S.–Iran talks stall as escalating violence strains a fragile ceasefire

+

Efforts to convert the interim pause in the 2026 U.S.–Iran war into a durable settlement have stalled. Iranian negotiators blamed the slow going on a lack of trust, what they call contradictory positions from Washington, and continued Israeli strikes in the region; U.S. officials continue to describe the indirect talks in cautiously positive terms. Vice President JD Vance asserted that American strikes had set Iran's nuclear program back "decades," saying Tehran is "further away from developing a nuclear bomb than they have ever been" in 20 or 30 years.

The unresolved flashpoints are familiar: a mechanism for releasing frozen Iranian assets, terms for restoring shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, and the future of nuclear inspections. With regional violence still simmering, both sides face the risk that a shaky truce unravels before it can be formalized.

Sources: FDD Overnight Brief, CNN
Israel

Knesset advances "Torah study" Basic Law shielding Haredi draft evaders

+

Israel's Knesset voted 63–53 on July 1 to advance, through its first reading, a contentious Basic Law declaring Torah study a foundational value of the state — a measure whose practical aim is to shield ultra-Orthodox (Haredi) men who avoid military service from sanctions and prosecution. Sponsored by United Torah Judaism MKs Moshe Gafni and Yaakov Asher and backed by Shas, it must clear two more readings to pass.

Because Israel has no written constitution, Basic Laws carry the highest legal status and can override future High Court rulings — meaning this one, if enacted, could immunize Haredi draft exemptions from judicial challenge. The attorney general's office and the Knesset's own legal advisers have panned the bill, and it deepens a fault line that has repeatedly threatened Netanyahu's coalition ahead of elections due by October 27.

Sources: Times of Israel, Ynet

Neuroscience

A surprise in the cerebellum upends a long-held model of how we move

+

Researchers report that two key cell types in the cerebellum — the brain's movement-coordination center — do not behave as textbook models predict, even though one directly drives the other. The finding challenges a long-standing assumption about how signals flow through the region and suggests the circuitry translating intention into smooth motion is more flexible, and less strictly hierarchical, than taught.

If it holds up, the work could reshape how scientists think about movement disorders, from tremor and ataxia to the motor symptoms of Parkinson's, by pointing to cell-type interactions that current models overlook. It is an early-stage result, but a reminder that even well-mapped brain regions still hold basic surprises.

Sources: ScienceDaily, Nature News

Energy & Climate

Storage enters the 100-gigawatt era as solar and wind pass gas

+

Two milestones are reshaping the power system. Annual battery-storage additions crossed the 100-gigawatt threshold: excluding pumped hydro, the world added about 112 GW (307 GWh) in 2025, up nearly half on the prior year, and BloombergNEF tallies still-steeper growth ahead — much of it now pulled forward by AI data-center demand. Separately, in April 2026, monthly global electricity generation from solar and wind surpassed gas-fired generation for the first time.

The pairing matters: cheap solar and wind increasingly set the marginal supply, and fast-growing storage is what lets that variable generation firm up and displace fossil peakers. Fresh project news underscores the momentum — from gigawatt-hour-scale batteries signing round-the-clock power deals to the U.K.'s first long-duration storage support scheme shortlisting 7.6 GW of capacity.

Sources: BloombergNEF, Energy-Storage.news
Biomedicine

Study: nearly half of U.S. kidney-failure patients referred for transplant never start evaluation

+

A large national study finds that among Americans with kidney failure who are referred for a transplant, nearly half never even begin the formal evaluation — and only about 19% make it onto the waitlist. The drop-off exposes how much of the transplant bottleneck lies not in the supply of organs but in the referral-to-evaluation pipeline, where administrative, logistical and access barriers stall patients before they can be considered.

Because transplantation offers better survival and lower long-term cost than years of dialysis, the findings point to a fixable failure: streamlining evaluation and closing gaps that fall hardest on under-resourced patients could widen access without a single additional donor organ.

Sources: ScienceDaily, Science News
