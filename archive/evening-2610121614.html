Justice Department · Congress

Federal agents search Rep. Ilhan Omar’s D.C.-area home three weeks before the midterms; she calls it an attempt to “target political enemies”

+

Early on Monday, Homeland Security Investigations agents searched the Washington-area home of the Minnesota Democrat. The Justice Department said HSI carried out a “judicially authorized search warrant secured by the Department of Justice” as part of an “ongoing federal criminal investigation.” Its statement did not name Omar or say what crime is suspected. People familiar with the case told NPR, ABC and others that it concerns immigration paperwork filed by her late father when she came to the U.S. from Somalia as a child. NPR’s sources described a multi-year probe that includes whistleblower complaints. Fox News first reported the search. Omar has not been charged.

Omar said the search was an attempt to “weaponize federal law enforcement” and that she “will not be silenced.” House Democratic leaders called it “deeply disturbing.” Asked about it, Trump said the search had “nothing to do with me” and was up to the Justice Department. The timing, 22 days before the Nov. 3 election, raises the question of DOJ’s election-year practice. Justice Manual §9-85.500 bars timing investigative steps to influence an election. Long-standing practice also discourages overt steps involving candidates close to a vote, though there is no binding 60-day rule. A person familiar with the matter told CNBC that DOJ does not believe the policy was violated because the search was not meant to become public. It is not yet known whether the Public Integrity Section was consulted, as DOJ rules require for a search involving a member of Congress. Vice President Vance said in March that Omar “definitely committed immigration fraud.”

The Left-Leaning Take

Mother Jones’ same-day report presents the search as the end point of a long administration campaign against Omar. It points to Vance’s and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin’s public statements that she is under investigation, and argues that DOJ has given no evidence or reason for acting this close to the election. Democratic voices quoted in coverage call it “another hard norm out the window.” (No Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion piece found yet.)

The Right-Leaning Take

No in-window National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason piece turned up; the search happened this morning. The administration’s position, from DOJ’s statement and earlier remarks by Vance and Mullin, is that a judge found probable cause and an existing fraud inquiry is simply running its course. From the anti-Trump right, The Bulwark’s Monday “MAGA Mondays” episode (Sam Stein, Will Sommer) took up the search; it is a podcast, not a written argument. (Partial.)

Trump donor Eric Deters says that before her confirmation hearing Guilfoyle asked him to pay a $100,000 American Express bill. Her lawyers deny the allegations, say Deters owed her money for speeches and consulting, and call reports about her travel misleading.

Russia · Ukraine

Kremlin rejects Zelensky’s offer of a 60-day truce to hold elections, offering a one-day pause instead, as Russia hits six energy sites

+

On Monday, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov rejected Zelensky’s offer, made on ABC’s This Week, to hold an election if Russia agreed to a full 60-day ceasefire. Peskov said Moscow does not want a ceasefire but a broader settlement. He argued Kyiv wanted the pause to regroup and “heal their wounds” rather than to vote. Peskov said Putin would be willing not to strike on election day itself. He declined to comment on the “energy ceasefire” Trump announced Sunday. Ukrainian law bars elections while martial law is in force.

Zelensky said Russia hit six energy facilities in a single day, wounding 33 people. He said it is “increasingly evident that Russia is unwilling to accept an energy ceasefire.” Ukrenergo reported new power cuts in the Kharkiv, Donetsk and Zaporizhzhia regions. Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov was quoted as saying Ukraine did not deserve the “respite” a truce would bring.

Analysis: Russia is rejecting a truce outright while leaving Trump’s energy-only deal publicly unanswered. That leaves Washington pressing Kyiv to stop refinery strikes without any confirmed Russian commitment in return. The one-day election-day offer looks designed to keep Trump’s push for new Ukrainian elections alive without giving up anything militarily.

Iran · United Kingdom

UK Prime Minister Burnham says the RAF Fairford plot was “almost certainly directed” by Iran’s Revolutionary Guard and sanctions two Iranians

+

On Monday, Prime Minister Andy Burnham told Parliament the suspected plot against RAF Fairford was “highly likely” to have been coordinated in Iran and “almost certainly directed” by the IRGC. U.S. forces use the base. London sanctioned two people. One is Ruhollah Bazghandi, described by the Foreign Office as the IRGC’s head of counterespionage operations, who faces an asset freeze and travel ban. The other is Shahram Ali Reza Tamarzadeh Jakki, who is linked to the already-sanctioned Zindashti criminal network. Iran’s ambassador was summoned; Tehran denies involvement and asks for evidence. Seven men have been arrested in the case. All have been released on bail and none charged, and police found gasoline but no explosives in the suspects’ vehicles. Some outlets report that a joint statement from 29 countries also blamed Iran.

Also on Monday, Iran’s foreign ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei said Tehran is still reviewing Washington’s reply to its Strait of Hormuz proposal and will respond through mediators. The day before, security official Mohsen Rezaei had called the U.S. response “inadequate.” Baghaei said security will not return to the strait while the U.S. blockade continues.

Analysis: Britain directly blaming the IRGC for a plot on its own soil, and sanctioning a named Guard official, adds a European front to pressure on Tehran. Iran’s mixed signals on Hormuz suggest the indirect talks have stalled, not collapsed.

Sources: NBC News (AP), Oct 12, CBS News, The National, Oct 12, Haaretz (Reuters), Oct 12

Courts · Midterms

A federal judge orders the administration to cancel its taxpayer-funded promotional TV ads and stop spending the rest of the $20 million budget

+

On Sunday evening, U.S. District Judge Jennifer Rochon in Manhattan issued a 14-day temporary restraining order against the Department of Homeland Security’s ad campaign, as reported by the AP and others. The order cancels remaining broadcasts and bars any further spending from the campaign’s $20 million budget. It also bars the government from entering, renewing or expanding contracts to produce or distribute the ads. Government filings show about $19 million had already been spent. The plaintiffs are Common Cause, SEIU 32BJ and its PAC, and an Alabama state House candidate, represented by Democracy Forward. Rochon found they were “likely to succeed on the merits.”

The administration had said the ads would end Sunday night anyway. In a Friday filing, Justice Department lawyers argued that emergency relief was unnecessary and that the plaintiffs lacked standing. The judge rejected both arguments, so the administration cannot extend or restart the campaign while the case proceeds. The finding is preliminary, and the merits are still to be argued. A separate Democratic National Committee suit is pending in Washington. Reports say the White House will not reimburse the money already spent.

The plaintiffs’ argument, quoted in coverage, is the one the left will press: an agency is spending public money on what amounts to campaign advertising weeks before the midterms. Yemen · Saudi Arabia

Houthis hit Riyadh’s airport again a day after a strike that killed 12, including an American; Riyadh vows a “decisive” response and Trump weighs joining

+

On Sunday, a projectile struck Terminal 4 of King Khalid International Airport. Saudi aviation authorities said it killed a Pakistani resident and wounded 11. The day before, a missile strike on the terminal 3–4 complex killed 12 and wounded 309, Saudi authorities said, and Secretary of State Rubio confirmed a U.S. citizen among the dead. Reports say an Indian national died of injuries on Sunday, which would take that toll to 13. A Thursday strike had already killed three. The Houthis claimed the attacks as retaliation for Saudi strikes on Sanaa and Hodeidah airports, and warned airlines that Saudi airports are “potential targets.” Hundreds of flights have been cancelled, and Riyadh schools moved online for a week.

The Saudi-led coalition called Saturday’s strike a “full-fledged war crime” and vowed to respond “decisively.” Asked whether the U.S. would join strikes in Yemen, Trump said, “We may. We’re going to look at it.” The U.S. currently provides intelligence, targeting help and about 100 advisers, but no airstrikes. UN envoy Hans Grundberg called the attack “a grave escalation.” As of Monday, the Houthis said airstrikes had hit Sanaa and blamed Saudi Arabia, which has not confirmed.

Analysis: Repeated mass-casualty strikes on a capital’s main airport make a direct U.S. role in the Yemen war more likely. Combined with the closed Strait of Hormuz, the Gulf now has two live fronts. The Saudi–Pakistan–Turkey defense pact activated on Oct. 5 adds another way the war could widen.

Pakistan

Pakistan declares emergency rule in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and suspends the Imran Khan party’s provincial government ahead of a march on Islamabad

+

On Sunday, President Asif Ali Zardari declared a state of emergency in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and suspended its government, led by Khan’s PTI party. He acted on the advice of Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif’s cabinet under Articles 232 and 234 of the constitution. The PM’s office cited the “provincial government’s deliberate disengagement from the fight against terrorism.” Governor Faisal Karim Kundi takes executive power for an initial six months with an advisory council. Parliament must take up the emergency in a joint sitting within 30 days.

The move came as PTI began a march on Islamabad demanding the jailed former prime minister’s release. Hundreds of activists and three of Khan’s sisters were detained beforehand. A PTI lawmaker said Chief Minister Sohail Afridi was arrested; that is unconfirmed. PTI’s spokesperson called the emergency “more of a coup disguised as emergency.” Protests were reported, and mobile data was slowed or cut in parts of the province.

Analysis: Removing the main opposition party’s elected government in a militancy-hit border province is the state’s sharpest move yet against Khan’s movement. It comes as Pakistan is also committing forces to Saudi Arabia’s defense.

Sources: Washington Post, Oct 11, Al Jazeera, Oct 11, Al Jazeera explainer, Oct 12
Korean Peninsula

North Korea fires multiple ballistic missiles, one flying more than 900 km, in its fourth launch in about a month

+

On Monday morning local time, North Korea fired several ballistic missiles from the Pyongyang area toward the sea east of the peninsula, South Korea’s Joint Chiefs of Staff said. Japan’s Defense Ministry counted at least two. One reached about 70 km in altitude and flew more than 910 km. Both landed outside Japan’s exclusive economic zone, and no damage was reported. A Japanese deputy defense minister suggested one may have been a hypersonic weapon. Seoul’s presidential office called the launch a violation of UN Security Council resolutions.

It follows an Oct. 3 launch from Wonsan described as a hypersonic intermediate-range missile, and the South’s own first hypersonic missile test last week, overseen by President Lee Jae Myung. Tensions are also high over a Sept. 21 landmine blast in the DMZ that wounded three South Korean soldiers. A joint Seoul–UN probe blamed the North; Pyongyang denies it.

Analysis: The two Koreas are now matching each other’s hypersonic tests while U.S. attention and assets are pulled toward the Gulf. (No Tier-1 copy found yet; reporting relies on AFP, Jiji, the Japan Times and Al Jazeera.)

AI · Deals

Amazon is in talks to buy AI video startup Decart for about $7 billion, the Wall Street Journal reports

+

The Wall Street Journal reported on Sunday that Amazon is negotiating to acquire Decart for roughly $7 billion. Decart was founded in Israel, is based in San Francisco, and builds real-time AI video-editing and interactive “world simulation” models. No deal has been signed. One could come soon if talks hold and no rival bidder appears. Reuters, relaying the report, said Amazon and Decart did not respond to requests for comment and that it could not independently verify the report.

Decart has drawn interest from several big buyers. Anthropic did due diligence on a deal of about $6 billion before walking away in September. Nvidia, an investor, earlier held exclusive talks that collapsed. Nvidia joined Decart’s $300 million round in May, which valued the company near $4 billion. Amazon has given Decart early access to its in-house Trainium AI chips.

EVs · China

China’s September EV sales hit a 2026 high on a surge in exports, even as domestic car sales slump

+

Data released Monday by the China Association of Automobile Manufacturers (CAAM) show new-energy-vehicle sales of 1.823 million in September. That is up 13.6% year on year and the best month of 2026. NEV exports reached 571,000, up about 160%. According to the AP, total passenger-car exports rose 64% to about 919,000, driven by plug-in hybrids and battery EVs, while domestic passenger-car sales fell about 27%. Sales of conventional gasoline passenger cars dropped 47%.

Retail figures from the China Passenger Car Association show the same split. Domestic NEV retail sales fell 16.7% to 1.084 million, a ninth straight monthly decline, even as NEVs hit a record 67% share of the shrinking market. Analysts quoted by the AP expect Chinese automakers to keep shifting production overseas to get around tariffs.

Energy Policy · Permitting

The Senate’s bipartisan permitting bill splits environmental groups from the clean-energy industry ahead of a November floor push

+

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (S. 5653) is a 417-page bill from Sens. Capito, Lee, Heinrich and Whitehouse. It would speed permitting for solar farms, power lines and oil and gas pipelines, make it harder for a future administration to revoke permits, and narrow environmental (NEPA) reviews and litigation. Sen. Heinrich says it would also make large data centers pay for the grid upgrades they need. Floor consideration is expected in early November through a manager’s amendment, with the House to follow.

Fossil-fuel and renewable industries are aligned in support, Fortune reported Sunday. Clean-energy backers argue its technology-neutral transmission and interconnection provisions could unlock tens of gigawatts of stalled clean power. Some green groups call it a giveaway to fossil fuels that weakens state and tribal authority. (The bill text dates from Sept. 30; what is new is how the coalitions are lining up.)

Sources: Fortune, Oct 11, Rigzone, Oct 1
