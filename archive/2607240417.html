Trade · Now In Effect

Trump’s forced-labor tariffs on 60 economies take effect at midnight — hitting 99.4% of U.S. imports as Wall Street sinks and Brent tops $100

+

New U.S. tariffs of 10% to 12.5% took effect at 12:01 a.m. ET Friday, July 24, after the U.S. Trade Representative published its final Section 301 action on Thursday, replacing Trump’s expiring temporary 10% worldwide tariff. The measure covers the top 60 trading partners — about 99.4% of all goods imported into the U.S. — and USTR Ambassador Jamieson Greer framed it as a response to those economies’ failure to enforce bans on goods made with forced labor, calling the U.S. “the only country in the world to adopt, and effectively enforce, a ban on imports made with forced labor.” The structure sets 12.5% on roughly 45 economies (including China and Vietnam) and 10% on about 15 to 17 partners with some existing prohibitions (including Canada, Mexico, the UK and India); oil and gas, fertilizer, and USMCA duty-free goods are exempt. It follows a March 12 investigation launch, a June 2 proposed action, more than 1,600 public comments and July 7–9 hearings.

The action is a deliberate legal end-run: after the Supreme Court struck down Trump’s IEEPA “emergency” global tariffs as illegal in February, the White House pivoted to Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 as sturdier statutory footing. Markets had an ugly Thursday, though the sell-off was driven mainly by oil and tech earnings rather than the tariffs directly: the Dow fell 506.93 points (0.97%) to 51,711.65, the S&P 500 dropped 1.21% to 7,408.30 and the Nasdaq slid 2.15% to 25,137.69, dragged by Alphabet (down about 6.5% on higher AI capital-spending guidance) and Tesla (down about 14% on falling profit), while Brent crude settled above $100 a barrel for the first time since late May on the Iran war and the 10-year Treasury yield hit its highest since January 2025.

The Left-Leaning Take

The synthesized left frame is a consumer tax wearing a human-rights costume: the forced-labor findings are read as a pretext to keep a blanket tariff wall standing after the courts knocked the original one down, with the real cost landing on U.S. shoppers and importers. The scramble to relocate the same policy into a sturdier statute is cast as an admission the emergency legal theory was never sound. (No dedicated in-window Mother Jones / Vox / Slate / Guardian opinion column isolated to this action surfaced — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Unusually, the free-trade right is hostile. The Dispatch (“Here Comes Another Round of Sham Tariffs”) calls the forced-labor rationale “nothing more than legal cover” and a “pretext,” argues the White House “scrambled for a replacement” after SCOTUS voided the IEEPA tariffs, and notes a lower court has already knocked the new levies down (the case is on appeal); Reason calls the Section 301 gambit legally “dubious.” The pro-Trump case rests on leverage and durability — a tariff wall built to survive the courts — but here the mainstream-right and libertarian objections converge rather than split. War Powers · Constitutional Showdown

Congress splits on the Iran war — the House rebukes Trump a second time, 214–208, but the Senate refuses to bind him, 47–49

+

On Thursday, July 23, the House voted 214–208 to adopt a war-powers resolution — sponsored by Rep. Pramila Jayapal (D-Wash.) — directing President Trump to “remove the use of United States Armed Forces from hostilities against” Iran, the second such rebuke the chamber has passed since the war restarted. Four Republicans crossed to join every Democrat: Thomas Massie (Ky.), Brian Fitzpatrick (Pa.), Warren Davidson (Ohio) and Tom Barrett (Mich.) — the same four defectors as before. The measure is a non-binding concurrent resolution that does not reach Trump’s desk, and Democrats forced the vote as lawmakers left Washington for a five-week August recess.

Hours later, the Senate voted 47–49 to reject advancing a competing binding joint resolution from Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.) that would have legally required Trump to halt hostilities. The party-line breakdown was the story: Susan Collins (R-Maine) was the lone Republican to vote to advance it, while John Fetterman (D-Pa.) was the lone Democrat against — netting the failure. Lawmakers cited mounting costs: 18 U.S. troops have died since fighting began in February, four of them since July 17, with roughly 500 injuries; Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth told a July 21 hearing the war has cost $37.5 billion. Trump has argued the War Powers Resolution’s constraints are themselves unconstitutional and would veto any binding curb, and Rep. Gregory Meeks (D-N.Y.) filed a resolution to force Speaker Mike Johnson to initiate litigation to enforce the vote.

The Left-Leaning Take

Congress is belatedly reasserting a constitutional power it never surrendered over a war lawmakers never authorized, now in its sixth month and killing Americans. The synthesized left frame singles out Fetterman and the 49 Senate Republicans for “sinking” the one measure with legal teeth, treating the split votes as proof the war can command neither a durable majority nor public consent. (In-window progressive coverage came from Common Dreams, activist news rather than the named opinion desks; no dedicated Mother Jones / Vox / Slate / Guardian column surfaced — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right largely held the line — 208 House Republicans and all but one Senate Republican voted to preserve the president’s hand, on the argument that you do not tie a commander-in-chief mid-war and that a symbolic vote only signals division to Tehran. The friction is a restrainer-libertarian strand: Massie on the floor and Reason argue Congress must reclaim the war power from an open-ended, unauthorized conflict. Intra-right dissent (anti-Trump): The Bulwark casts the war as improvisation dressed as strategy — label that a minority anti-Trump line, not the mainstream-right position. The Home Front · Escalating

Trump says he’s “close” to a “massive attack” on Iran “bigger than ever before” — even as his own war polls collapse

+

In an Axios interview published Thursday, July 23, Trump said: “I am considering a massive attack. Bigger than ever before. I am close to making a decision. We are all set for it.” He argued the Iranians “want to negotiate” but “haven’t received enough pain yet,” and threatened to destroy an Iranian bridge or power plant — “including in Tehran” — each time Iran attacks a ship in the Strait of Hormuz. Two U.S. officials told Axios no fresh military orders had yet been issued, and U.S. Central Command said it had completed a 13th consecutive night of strikes; Iranian state media reported at least four people killed.

The escalation lands against a war that has grown historically unpopular. A Fox News survey put Trump’s handling of Iran at 34% approve to 66% disapprove, and a University of Maryland poll found 52% of Americans want the war ended now — including 76% of Democrats, 65% of independents and 23% of Republicans. U.N. Secretary-General António Guterres warned the situation “is getting out of control… It’s time to step back,” calling diplomacy “the only way forward.” The political bind is acute: the GOP’s last major act before the recess was funding a conflict whose support is eroding even within Trump’s own base.

The synthesized left frame is a self-made quagmire: a war Trump promised would be quick is now in its sixth month, killing service members, spiking gas prices and threatening to widen toward Tehran and Tel Aviv with no defined endpoint. The nightly “bridge or power plant” retaliation is read as escalation for punishment’s sake, untethered from any achievable objective, and the lopsided independent opposition is cited as proof the country never signed up for a third Gulf war. Middle East · Regional War

Iran rejects a U.S. ceasefire as the war hits a 13th night — the Houthis blockade Saudi shipping and Brent crosses $100

+

U.S. Central Command completed its 13th consecutive night of airstrikes on Iran overnight into Friday, July 24, saying it hit “Iranian military command centers, drone storage facilities, communication networks, coastal surveillance sites, and maritime capabilities”; Iranian state media reported at least four killed. Diplomacy stalled hard: Iran rejected a U.S. ceasefire proposal carried to Tehran by Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi, who met President Masoud Pezeshkian and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, saying it would not accept a “temporary deal” that leaves unresolved control over the Strait of Hormuz — its central demand. Trump signaled he is not ready to negotiate: “They would like to do something, but I say they’re not ready yet. They need more of the same.” Iran warned that if Washington strikes Tehran and vital infrastructure, it will widen the war — including “striking Tel Aviv and asking the Houthis to close the Bab el-Mandeb Strait.”

The war has opened a second maritime front. Yemen’s Houthis declared a naval blockade of Saudi Arabia and claimed drone-and-missile strikes on two Saudi oil tankers, the Encelia and Layla, in the Red Sea; the Saudi Press Agency confirmed the Encelia was hit and caught fire at the bow, with all crew safe, and at least seven vessels have rerouted to avoid Bab el-Mandeb. On markets, Brent crude crossed $100 a barrel for the first time since May 26, gaining about 7% to close near $100.69 — with the IEA calling the near-total halt of Hormuz traffic the “largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market.” The significance is that the conflict is no longer contained to Hormuz: the Houthi blockade opens a second chokepoint, drags Saudi Arabia in as a direct target, and Iran’s Tel Aviv threat risks re-internationalizing the war toward Israel, while the diplomatic sticking point — sovereignty over Hormuz — is structural rather than tactical.

Sources: Reuters via U.S. News, NBC News, CNBC, Times of Israel, Lawfare
Asia-Pacific · South China Sea

China fires water cannon at Philippine boats for a third time this week — with a CNN crew aboard — as ASEAN ministers meet in Manila

+

On Friday, July 24, China Coast Guard vessels fired water cannon at Philippine ships near Scarborough Shoal (Bajo de Masinloc), the third confrontation this week in the contested waters. At least three Philippine vessels were hit during a morning resupply run by the Philippine Bureau of Fisheries and Aquatic Resources, with a CNN crew aboard one of the boats; larger Chinese vessels circled continuously, blasting water while warning over a megaphone: “Leave immediately… Otherwise, we will take firm action in accordance with the law.” The week’s first clash came Monday at Second Thomas Shoal, where the Philippines keeps a garrison aboard the grounded WWII-era BRP Sierra Madre; Philippine video showed roughly eight Chinese personnel ramming a smaller craft and swinging wooden batons and oars, injuring two Philippine service members.

The confrontations are unfolding as ASEAN foreign ministers — and Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi — gather in Manila; Rubio and Wang discussed Monday’s clash on Wednesday, with Washington condemning China’s “dangerous and aggressive” actions and Wang warning the Philippines of “bitter consequences.” The significance is the tempo and the theater: three physical confrontations at two flashpoints in one week, staged during a diplomatic summit, signal that Beijing will not moderate gray-zone coercion for the sake of optics — while the embedded CNN crew hands Manila an information-war win, and each injury to Filipino personnel edges closer to the threshold where Manila could invoke its Mutual Defense Treaty with the U.S.

Diplomacy · Nonproliferation

A day after signing a 30-year nuclear pact with Riyadh, Trump makes it “totally subject to” Saudi Arabia joining the Abraham Accords

+

On Wednesday, July 22, the U.S. and Saudi Arabia signed a civilian nuclear cooperation agreement — inked by Energy Secretary Chris Wright and Saudi Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman — reportedly running about 30 years and involving U.S. companies in developing the kingdom’s nuclear program. The signed announcement made no mention of Israel. Then on Thursday, July 23, less than 24 hours later, Trump attached a new condition, writing that the deal “will be approved, but is totally subject to Saudi Arabia joining the very respected and successful Abraham Accords.” He also said the agreement would bar uranium enrichment, addressing proliferation fears that the plants could be used to enrich weapons-grade material or reprocess spent fuel.

As of Thursday, Saudi Arabia had not responded to the new demand. Riyadh has long held that it will not normalize with Israel absent a credible pathway to a Palestinian state — a position hardened by the war in Gaza. The move retroactively binds Trump’s signature foreign-policy prize to a technical energy pact his own administration had just concluded without it, colliding two hard constraints: his Abraham Accords ambition versus Riyadh’s statehood insistence, which Israel’s government will not grant. It also lands amid the Iran war, where Saudi Arabia is now a direct Houthi target — giving Riyadh more reason to want U.S. security guarantees but less domestic room to embrace Israel. The likely result is protracted limbo, and the pact still faces a mandatory Congressional review.

Sources: NPR, NBC News, Axios, Washington Post (partial — paywalled), JTA

Technology · AI Silicon

AI-chip startup Etched doubles to a $10.3 billion valuation, betting a transformer-only chip can beat Nvidia’s GPUs

+

On July 23, AI-chip startup Etched — founded in 2022 by three Harvard dropouts — closed a $300 million Series C at a $10.3 billion valuation, roughly doubling the ~$5 billion mark it carried in December, when it raised $500 million. The round was led by Sequoia (reportedly the highest valuation ever for a Sequoia-led Series C), with Andreessen Horowitz, SK Hynix, Jane Street and Diffusion Capital participating. Etched is betting against general-purpose GPUs with Sohu, a transformer-specific ASIC built on TSMC’s 4-nanometer process that the company claims delivers up to a 20× inference speedup over Nvidia’s H100 on transformer models.

What is genuinely new is that Etched says it has now successfully manufactured the chips, has first full systems in testing with customers, and has already booked about $1 billion in orders — moving it from a promising design to shipping product. Proceeds go to scaling production and customer deployments, making it a notable data point in the wave of inference-specialized silicon challenging Nvidia’s dominance.

Energy · Power Demand

The IEA says global electricity demand is accelerating — AI, EVs and air-conditioning push growth toward 3.8% by 2027

+

The International Energy Agency released its Electricity Mid-Year Update 2026 on July 23, forecasting global power-demand growth of 3.6% in 2026 and 3.8% in 2027, up from 3% in 2025, with consumption reaching about 30,700 terawatt-hours in 2027 (versus 28,600 in 2025). The agency frames this as the “dawn of the Age of Electricity,” driven by industrial growth, rising ownership of appliances, air-conditioning and heat pumps, EV charging and data-centre expansion. Regionally, China accelerates to 5.5% in 2026 on manufacturing and EV charging, India rebounds to roughly 7% after a weather-dampened 2025, and both the U.S. and EU hold near 2%.

The report also flags a real-world shock tied to the Gulf war: disruptions to LNG flows through the Strait of Hormuz have pushed Asian and European gas prices to their highest since the 2022–23 energy crisis, prompting emergency demand-curbing measures in some regions. The quantified acceleration — and the reminder that electrification is colliding with a fresh fossil-fuel supply squeeze — is the newsworthy shift.

Sources: IEA, IEA report, Reuters via Business Recorder
Climate · Attribution

Heat, not lack of rain, is now parching Europe: scientists find climate change made bone-dry soils up to 11 times likelier

+

A World Weather Attribution study published July 23 finds that summer 2026 is shaping up as one of Europe’s most significant heat-and-drought events in recent years — and that record heat, not a rainfall deficit, is now the dominant driver, evaporating soil moisture faster than rain can replenish it. The headline attribution figures: extremely dry soils are roughly 5× more likely in western Europe and about 11× more likely in eastern Europe today than in a world without human-caused climate change. Between April and June, swaths of the continent from Portugal to southern Finland saw below-average rainfall; combined with successive heatwaves, that drove unusually low river levels and severe wildfires — 116,000 hectares burned in Spain and 42,000 in France.

The analysis, conducted by scientists across roughly a dozen countries using established peer-reviewed methods, reframes the problem: as the climate warms, drought in Europe is increasingly a story of heat pulling moisture out of the ground rather than of skies failing to deliver rain — a shift with direct implications for how water and land are managed on the continent.

Sources: World Weather Attribution, Euronews
Biomedicine · Oncology

Chemotherapy leaves a readable DNA “signature” in childhood tumors — a SickKids study points to catching resistance earlier

+

A study led by Toronto’s Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids), published in Nature on July 23, finds that nearly half of childhood tumors treated with common chemotherapies carry detectable DNA changes linked to the treatment within 18 months. By combining whole-genome sequencing with detailed medical records and computational methods developed at SickKids, the team identified distinct genomic “signatures” left by different chemotherapy drugs, with some patterns appearing as early as 91 days after treatment began. Platinum-based chemotherapy — given to nearly half of all treated patients — was among the most striking: within 18 months, 48% of tumors showed a detectable platinum-related signature on surviving cancer cells.

The researchers examined more than 600 tumors from 544 patients across Canada, Australia and the United States, and say the signatures could help clinicians spot treatment resistance earlier — enabling more precise use of chemotherapy and potentially reducing its toxic effects on young patients. It is a step toward reading, in the tumor’s own genome, when a therapy is starting to fail.

Sources: Nature, GenomeWeb, Mirage News
