Three longer reads for the week — the kind of thing worth turning over slowly with a second cup of coffee.

History

How a vast archive can hide the Ming dynasty in plain sight

+

The art historian Craig Clunas — professor emeritus of the history of art at Oxford and a Fellow of the British Academy — used a new Aeon essay, published July 7, to make a deceptively simple argument about one of the best-documented empires in world history. The Ming (1368–1644) left an enormous written record, yet Clunas contends that this very abundance can mislead us: the surviving sources, including the gossipy unofficial “wild histories” (yeshi) he prizes, often tell us more about the anxieties and preoccupations of the men who compiled them than about the reality they claim to describe. His case study is the imperial family itself. The dynasty’s founder, Zhu Yuanzhang, sat atop not just a state but a sprawling polygynous lineage that multiplied across generations into tens of thousands of stipended princes and relatives — an institution so large it became a fiscal and political problem, and one historians have tended to flatten into a footnote.

The essay draws on Clunas’s book Sons of Heaven: Family and Dynasty in Ming China (Reaktion Books, 2026), billed as the first accessible, freshly researched English-language account of the Ming imperial family, following emperors, empresses, consorts and hangers-on through both official annals and unofficial sources. The deeper payoff is historiographical: Clunas is interested in how “knowing a lot” and “knowing well” diverge, and in the way even a mountainous archive is shaped by who was allowed to write, what they wished to conceal, and which lives — women, minor kin, the enslaved and servile — were recorded only obliquely. It is a meditation on the limits of documentary knowledge disguised as a portrait of a dynasty.

Sources: Aeon, Reaktion Books, University of Chicago Press
Ideas

What the fair of 1876 reveals about how America invents itself

+

As the United States marks its semiquincentennial, Brenda Wineapple uses the July 23, 2026 issue of The New York Review of Books — in an essay titled “Blowing Out America’s Birthday Candles,” published online in early July — to look back a century and a half, to the Centennial Exhibition of 1876 in Philadelphia. Her occasion is Fergus M. Bordewich’s new book Centennial: The Great Fair of 1876 and the Invention of America’s Future (Knopf). Bordewich argues that the exhibition — with its Corliss steam engine, its dazzling machinery hall, its projection of industrial confidence — sold the country a future largely authored by its industrial magnates: an “expansive vision of a glittering consumerist future” staged precisely during a moment of financial depression, labor unrest and post-Reconstruction political rot. The fair, in other words, was less a mirror of the nation than an argument about what the nation should want to become.

Wineapple’s review-essay turns this into a reflection on the uses and evasions of national anniversaries. She foregrounds the “uncertain triumphalism” beneath the celebration: William Lloyd Garrison, still alive in 1876, saw plainly that the Declaration’s promise of inalienable rights remained unfulfilled, noting that the South was “still insolent and brutal to the race it once held in abject bondage.” Native Americans, freedpeople and women were largely written out of the 1876 pageant even as it claimed to speak for all Americans. Read against the 2026 backdrop, the essay becomes a quietly pointed meditation on how a country narrates its own progress — what gets displayed in the machinery hall and what gets left outside the gates.

Sources: The New York Review of Books, NYRB (July 23, 2026 issue), Fergus Bordewich, Centennial
Natural Sciences

New evidence that quantum proofs can say what classical ones cannot

+

Quanta Magazine reported on July 6 on a result that sharpens one of theoretical computer science’s most philosophically loaded questions: what does it mean to prove something, and does quantumness make a proof more powerful? The complexity class QMA contains problems whose “yes” answers can be certified by a quantum witness — a fragile quantum state — that an efficient quantum verifier checks; its cousin QCMA restricts the witness to ordinary classical bits. Whether these are genuinely different classes has been open for over two decades. John Bostanci (Columbia), Jonas Haferkamp (Saarland), Chinmay Nirkhe (University of Washington) and Mark Zhandry (NTT Research & Stanford) constructed a classical oracle under which QMA is strictly larger than QCMA — strong evidence that some truths can be efficiently vouched for by a quantum proof but not by any classical one. The work earned a best-paper award at the 2026 Symposium on the Theory of Computing (STOC).

What makes the story more than a technical milestone is the collaborative twist Quanta narrates. After Bostanci presented the oracle separation, MIT master’s student Andrew Huang recognized that a piece of the machinery could be repurposed; he and his adviser Vinod Vaikuntanathan joined Bostanci to prove a second, independent oracle separation between the two classes by different means. Two roads to the same conclusion strengthen the case that the gap is real rather than an artifact of one clever construction. Because these are oracle (relativized) results, they do not settle the question absolutely — but they map the boundary of where quantum information carries evidentiary weight that classical information cannot, a genuinely conceptual question about the nature of verification wearing the clothes of complexity theory.

Sources: Quanta Magazine, Columbia University CS
