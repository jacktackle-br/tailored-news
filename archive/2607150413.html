Immigration · ICE Use of Force

ICE orders a nationwide pause on most vehicle stops after two fatal shootings of bystanders in Maine and Texas

+

U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement directed officers on Monday–Tuesday, July 14, to halt most non-urgent vehicle stops, effective immediately, following two fatal shootings by federal agents in under a week. The most recent came Monday, July 13, in Biddeford, Maine, where agents shot and killed a 26-year-old man during an immigration operation; the earlier shooting killed 52-year-old Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, a Mexican national who had lived in the U.S. for more than 35 years, during a July 7 traffic stop in Houston. DHS confirmed neither man was the target of the operation that killed him. The directive — which applies to Enforcement and Removal Operations, with carve-outs for criminal warrants and joint operations — came from Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin after conversations with Maine Sens. Susan Collins (R) and Angus King (I); Collins said she urged Mullin to “cease all non-urgent vehicle stops.” ICE also said it would move to expand body-camera use, aiming to equip arrest teams within 60 days — a pledge it also made after a January Minneapolis shooting but did not fulfill.

Neither agent in the Maine or Texas shootings was wearing a body camera, per DHS. Border czar Tom Homan characterized the move as a “short pause” rather than a policy change, predicting agents would be “back up and running” within “a couple of weeks.” Outlets stressed the context: vehicle stops had become a core tactic driving a surge in detentions, including roughly 10,000 arrests over a five-day span in late June. The Maine victim’s identity was reported inconsistently across outlets — his name spelled several ways and his nationality reported by some as Colombian — so treat those specifics as provisional.

Congress · Defense Authorization

Senate Democrats block the $1.15 trillion defense bill in a rare procedural defeat, protesting Trump’s unauthorized Iran war

+

The Senate voted 50–46 on Tuesday, July 14, largely along party lines, against a motion to proceed on the $1.15 trillion National Defense Authorization Act — a rare stall for one of the chamber’s few “must-pass” bills. Minority Leader Chuck Schumer led the opposition, arguing the NDAA “cannot become a permission slip” and accusing the president of “waging an unauthorized war, defying bipartisan majorities in Congress, [and] refusing to level with the American people about the cost, the mission, or the end game.” Democrats cited three objections: escalation of the Iran war without congressional authorization; provisions deepening U.S. military and intelligence integration with Israel; and the bill’s record size amid a White House push to raise Pentagon spending sharply (reported toplines vary — the bill on the floor was $1.15T, with the administration seeking as high as $1.5T versus roughly $900B a year earlier).

The blocked legislation would have provided about $1.1 trillion to the Defense Department, $41 billion to the Energy Department for the nuclear arsenal, and $11 billion to other defense activities, plus a 3.6% troop pay raise and family housing, health, childcare and education investments. A group of Democrats — including Chris Van Hollen, Bernie Sanders, Elizabeth Warren, Ed Markey, Jeff Merkley and Peter Welch — circulated a letter urging colleagues not to advance a bill that would “compel [Trump] to deepen the US relationship with Netanyahu’s extremist government.” With the war now in its fifth month and no clear endgame, the vote leaves the annual bill’s path uncertain.

Standing progressive framing, reflected in Guardian and allied coverage: this is Democrats finally using real legislative leverage to check an unauthorized, undeclared war, and refusing to be conscripted into funding a deeper alliance with the Netanyahu government — a rare and overdue exercise of Congress’s war-powers role rather than obstruction.

The Iran War · Escalation

The U.S. reimposes its naval blockade and launches a seven-hour strike wave; India summons Tehran’s envoy after an Indian sailor is killed in the Strait of Hormuz

+

The war intensified sharply overnight into Wednesday, July 15. U.S. Central Command said American forces reinstated a naval blockade of vessels transiting to and from Iranian ports at 4 p.m. ET Tuesday, then launched a fresh “seven-hour wave” of strikes beginning around 6 a.m. ET Wednesday, hitting “dozens of military targets near the Strait of Hormuz and Iranian coastal areas” to degrade Iran’s ability to threaten commercial shipping; CENTCOM said more than 20 Navy warships and hundreds of aircraft are operating across the region. Iran’s army said a strike on a barracks in Bampour, in southeastern Sistan and Baluchestan province, killed at least seven personnel of the 388th Mechanized Infantry Brigade and vowed “a decisive response”; Iran’s Health Ministry said more than 260 people were wounded overnight. In a Fox News interview aired Tuesday, President Trump said strikes would continue “until I say it’s enough” and threatened “next week comes the power plants … next week comes the bridges” absent a return to talks; he backed off a proposed 20% Hormuz transit toll, saying Gulf-state investment in the U.S. would replace it.

The rupture is diplomatic as well as military: Iranian Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said the blockade’s resumption has “dismantled the Islamabad memorandum” underpinning the now-collapsing peace talks, and Tehran threatened to halt all Middle East energy exports. The freshest 24-hour spillover reached South Asia: India summoned senior Iranian diplomats after an Indian crew member was killed and several others injured when two tankers — MT Al Bahiyah and MT Mombasa, carrying roughly 30 Indian seafarers among a crew of 46 — were struck by Iranian missiles while transiting the strait; New Delhi lodged a “strong protest” and demanded an immediate halt to attacks on commercial shipping. Note factual variance: outlets differ on the tankers’ flags and on casualty counts, the Bampour death toll is single-sourced to Iran’s army, and Iranian casualty figures come from Iranian officials — none independently confirmed by Western wires.

Arabian Peninsula · Yemen & Saudi Arabia

Houthis strike Saudi Arabia’s Abha airport and threaten a “siege,” shattering four years of calm after Sanaa airport was hit to block an Iranian flight

+

A new front reopened on the Arabian Peninsula. On Monday, July 13, Yemen’s internationally recognized government said its forces struck the runway of Sanaa International Airport specifically to prevent an Iranian aircraft from landing “in violation of Yemeni sovereignty.” Yemen’s Houthi movement blamed the strikes on Saudi Arabia and retaliated by launching ballistic missiles and drones at Abha International Airport in southern Saudi Arabia; Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree announced the strike as a response to “criminal Saudi aggression.” No casualties were reported, but multiple Tier-1 desks (Washington Post, AP) characterized it as the sharpest escalation since the Saudi-led coalition campaign years ago, effectively ending roughly four years of relative calm. The immediate trigger traces to July 3, when the first publicly announced Iranian flight in over a decade landed in Sanaa.

By Tuesday, July 14, a leading Houthi official escalated the rhetoric, threatening to impose a “siege” on Saudi Arabia — arguing that Saudi willingness to strike Sanaa airport “gives Yemen the right to strike their airports and to impose on them a siege.” The episode is entangled with the broader U.S.–Iran war but represents a distinct Saudi–Houthi theater and a direct challenge to the détente Riyadh has maintained since the 2022 truce. Note an unresolved attribution dispute: the Houthis attribute the Sanaa strikes to Saudi Arabia, while Yemen’s recognized government claimed responsibility itself, framing the raid as an anti-Iran interdiction — a discrepancy outlets have not reconciled.

Asia-Pacific · South China Sea

China summons Japan’s envoy and rejects a 14-nation statement reaffirming the 2016 South China Sea ruling on its tenth anniversary

+

Marking ten years since the 2016 Permanent Court of Arbitration award that rejected Beijing’s expansive “historic rights” claims, 14 countries issued a joint statement (dated around July 12) reaffirming the ruling as “final, legally binding, and definitive between China and the Philippines.” The signatories — the Philippines, Australia, Canada, Estonia, Germany, Italy, Japan, Latvia, Lithuania, New Zealand, Romania, Slovenia, the United Kingdom and the United States — framed their commitment around “a free and open Indo-Pacific that is peaceful, stable, and rules-based.”

The diplomatic fallout landed within the 24-hour window: on July 14 the Chinese Embassy in Japan lodged “solemn representations,” with embassy minister Shi Yong delivering a demarche to Masaaki Kanai, director-general of the Asian and Oceanian Affairs Bureau of Japan’s Foreign Ministry, over the Japanese foreign minister’s remarks; China separately summoned Japan’s envoy in Beijing. Beijing reiterated that the arbitration is “illegal, null and void, and has no binding force,” saying it “neither accepts nor recognizes it,” while Chinese state media framed the statement as part of a “remilitarization agenda.” The exchange adds to already strained Tokyo–Beijing ties and underscores how the Philippines-anchored maritime dispute keeps drawing in European and Indo-Pacific partners.

Tech · Semiconductors

ASML beats expectations and raises its 2026 forecast a second time as AI-chip demand drives record chipmaking-tool orders

+

ASML, the Dutch monopoly supplier of extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines, reported second-quarter 2026 results on Wednesday, July 15, posting revenue of about €9.33 billion (versus roughly €8.80 billion expected) and net income of about €2.92 billion, at a 54% gross margin. The company raised its full-year 2026 revenue guidance to €43–45 billion — about 16% higher at the midpoint than its prior €36–40 billion range — and lifted gross-margin guidance to 54–56%, while guiding third-quarter revenue to €11–12 billion.

Management said first-half orders were “extremely strong” as customers accelerated capacity expansion for AI chips, citing solid logic and DRAM investment and order visibility into 2027–2028. ASML plans to add roughly 30% to both its 2026 low-NA EUV and DUV immersion capacity, and noted that Intel has begun using High-NA EUV in production. Shares jumped sharply at the open (reports ranged from about 4% to over 7%). The results are a fresh marker that the AI build-out is still pulling capital through the entire chip supply chain, not just the accelerator designers.

Energy · Batteries

A Max Planck team pins down how soft lithium dendrites crack solid-state batteries’ ceramic electrolyte — a key barrier to EVs

+

Researchers at Germany’s Max Planck Institute for Sustainable Materials say they have resolved a decade-long debate over why solid-state batteries short-circuit — a chief barrier to commercializing the high-energy-density technology for EVs and electronics. Using cryo-electron microscopy under vacuum, the team showed that soft lithium-metal filaments (dendrites) enclosed inside an existing micro-crack build up pressure that fractures the rigid ceramic (garnet) electrolyte, “like water pressing into cracks of a rock,” causing brittle fracture and eventual short-circuiting.

The findings, published in Nature, establish the mechanical fracture mechanism and point toward mitigation strategies such as toughening the solid electrolyte to delay crack formation. The underlying paper dates to April 2026; the results drew wider attention via a public press release around July 10, so this is slightly outside the strict 24-hour window but was in circulation this week — a genuine development on one of electrification’s hardest engineering problems.

Sources: ScienceDaily, Max-Planck-Gesellschaft, electrive, chemeurope
Biomed · Aging

In a first randomized trial, semaglutide slowed markers of biological aging in adults with HIV

+

A study published in Nature Communications and highlighted this week (ScienceDaily release July 13) reports the first randomized-controlled-trial evidence that the GLP-1 drug semaglutide — the active ingredient in Ozempic and Wegovy — may slow biological aging. Led by researchers at UC San Diego with partner institutions, the team reanalyzed a trial of 108 adults with HIV-associated lipohypertrophy; roughly half received weekly semaglutide injections and half placebo over a 32-week treatment period.

Using “epigenetic clocks,” the researchers found participants on semaglutide showed about a 9% reduction in the pace of biological aging on the DunedinPACE clock, along with improvements in markers linked to mortality risk and to the blood, brain, heart, liver, kidneys and metabolic health. The authors cautioned that this is a signal the drug may slow some aging-related processes, not evidence that it reverses aging — and that the cohort, adults with HIV, is a specific population.

Space · Astrobiology

One of the deepest technosignature searches yet finds no artificial radio signals from the “Hycean” candidate world K2-18b

+

Astronomers conducted one of the broadest and most sensitive searches to date for artificial radio signals from the K2-18 system, home to the intensely studied sub-Neptune K2-18b — a possible “Hycean” (ocean-covered) world. Using the Very Large Array in New Mexico and the MeerKAT array in South Africa, the team observed the system for 33 days — one full orbital year for K2-18b — across frequencies from 544 MHz to 9.8 GHz, a far broader range than prior searches and sensitive enough to detect transmitters up to about 1,000 times weaker.

After sifting more than 20 million candidate signals from the VLA alone, the researchers found no transmissions consistent with a technological origin, placing upper limits on any transmitter roughly comparable to the power of the former Arecibo radar. The work was published in The Astronomical Journal and publicized around July 12, 2026 (just outside the strict 24-hour window). A null result, but a meaningful tightening of the limits on any broadcasting civilization around one of the most scrutinized nearby exoplanets.

Sources: Astronomy.com, ScienceDaily, The Astronomical Journal, arXiv
