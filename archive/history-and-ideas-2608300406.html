History & Archaeology · Neolithic Anatolia

An 11,000-year-old statue of a human riding a leopard, pulled from the dirt at Karahantepe, quietly inverts how we thought the first monument-builders imagined the line between people and predators

+

During the 2026 excavation season at Karahantepe — the sister site to Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Türkiye — archaeologists uncovered a 70-centimeter composite sculpture of a human figure seated on the back of a leopard, found exactly where it had been set down in the Pre-Pottery Neolithic, resting on a bench inside a circular structure whose original flagstone floor was still intact. Turkey’s Minister of Culture and Tourism, Mehmet Nuri Ersoy, announced the find on August 25, calling its style “unlike anything seen before.” What makes it genuinely provocative is the reversal it represents: earlier Karahantepe statues show the opposite arrangement — a human carrying a leopard on their back, the predator as burden or trophy. Here the beast is mounted, the human atop the apex hunter, in what excavators call the first known “rider” composition anywhere in the Pre-Pottery Neolithic record.

Why it matters: this is the era, around 9000 BCE, before pottery and before farming was fully settled, when humans were first raising monumental communal architecture. The iconography of these sites is our best window into their cosmology, and a shift from “human bears the beast” to “human sits astride the beast” hints at an evolving conception of dominance, status, or the human–animal relationship at the very dawn of settled ritual life — evidence, the excavators argue, of surprisingly developed forms of social organization and symbolic thought among some of the world’s earliest quasi-urban communities.

Sources: ARTnews, Daily Sabah
Religion · Sociology of Belief & the Machine-Made Sacred

A new audit finds most religious and spiritual books now sold on Amazon are probably machine-written — raising the unsettling question of what it means for sacred texts to be authored by something with no beliefs at all

+

An analysis by the AI-detection firm Originality.ai, reported the week of August 24–26 (including an August 26 write-up in The Christian Century and coverage in Publishers Weekly), flagged 1,272 of 2,034 books — 63% — across fourteen religious and belief categories on Amazon as “likely AI-written,” drawing on titles sampled between January and June 2026. The rates were highest in exactly the corners you might not expect: witchcraft (78%), Hinduism (76%) and Taoism (74%). Researchers hypothesized that the concentration in Eastern and esoteric traditions partly reflects non-native English speakers using AI to break into the Anglophone market. Most striking for anyone who cares about religious knowledge: the auditors flagged 53% of fact-checkable claims in the witchcraft books as potentially false, and found a quarter of the sampled Hindu titles carried AI-generated summaries, author bios and text samples alike — a fully synthetic apparatus of spiritual authority.

Why it’s interesting: religious publishing has always been a vehicle of formation — you read a devotional or a primer to be shaped by a tradition and, implicitly, by a human teacher standing in that tradition. The study surfaces a genuinely new problem in the sociology of religion: a flood of plausible, cheaply produced and frequently inaccurate “spiritual” text authored by systems that hold no faith, belong to no lineage, and answer to no community of practice. It is the ancient question of who has the authority to speak for a tradition, arriving in an algorithmic form — a story about trust, authenticity and the quiet commodification of belief.

Sources: Publishers Weekly, Originality.ai
Philosophy of Physics · The Nature of Reality

A meditation on Hilbert space asks whether the true ‘theater of reality’ is not the space and time we live in, but a vast abstract arena of pure possibility from which spacetime itself might merely emerge

+

In an essayistic piece for Quanta Magazine (August 26, 2026), the science writer Charlie Wood walks readers out of the familiar world and into Hilbert space — the abstract mathematical arena where quantum mechanics actually plays out. Every quantum object carries a menu of possible futures before it is measured; Hilbert space is the structure that holds all those possibilities at once, and the more futures an object has, the higher-dimensional its Hilbert space (a two-outcome “qubit” lives in a mere two dimensions; richer systems sprawl into staggering dimensionality). The genuinely provocative turn is metaphysical. A camp of physicists — most prominently Sean Carroll, whose position is nicknamed “Hilbert Space Fundamentalism” — argues that if quantum mechanics is truly the bottom layer of nature, then Hilbert space isn’t a convenient bookkeeping device but the fundamental theater of reality itself.

On this Everettian, Many-Worlds reading, the entire universe is a single quantum state vector evolving smoothly via the Schrödinger equation, and the things we take as bedrock — space, particles, fields, even the three dimensions we move through — are emergent, secondary patterns crystallizing out of that abstract dynamics. Why it’s interesting for an ideas digest: this is one of the deepest live disputes about what is actually real, sitting right where physics becomes philosophy. It flips the intuitive picture — that math describes a physical world — and asks whether the mathematical structure is the world, with our tangible reality a kind of afterimage. It is the sort of argument that would have been at home in a medieval debate about universals, now waged with the machinery of quantum theory.

Sources: Quanta Magazine
