Justice Department · AG Confirmation

Blanche’s math tightens as the floor vote nears: McConnell’s absence shrinks the GOP cushion while Murkowski and Cassidy go quiet

+

The whip count on acting Attorney General Todd Blanche hardened Wednesday without resolving. Sen. Susan Collins (R-Maine) reaffirmed her “no,” while the two pivotal undecideds — Lisa Murkowski (R-Alaska) and Bill Cassidy (R-La.) — declined to say how they will vote; Cassidy told reporters he “remains undecided,” reiterating skepticism he voiced last week. The wrinkle that sharpened since this morning is arithmetic: with GOP leader Mitch McConnell (R-Ky.) still absent — sidelined since a June 14 fall and a bout of pneumonia, and telling colleagues he “won’t be able to return to the Senate floor to vote quite yet” — the Republican margin is effectively one seat thinner. Reuters characterized the confirmation as a “work in progress” after Collins’s defection, and CNN said the nomination is “hanging by a thread.”

Blanche is Trump’s personal lawyer installed atop a department he cannot keep independent; a WaPo editorial and a Boston Globe op-ed both called him “unfit,” and the last-minute fund rescission reads as vote-buying, not reform. In this view Murkowski and Cassidy are the last off-ramp. (Synthesized from Washington Post / Boston Globe opinion and MSNBC.)

Immigration · Temporary Protected Status

TPS for up to 350,000 Haitians officially ends after a judge lifts the last stay, applying the Supreme Court’s June ruling

+

Temporary Protected Status for an estimated 330,000–350,000 Haitians officially lapsed Wednesday after U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes lifted her stay and applied the Supreme Court’s June ruling, which held 6–3 (Justice Samuel Alito writing) that DHS’s TPS determinations are largely shielded from judicial review. The termination strips work authorization and deportation protection from a population that has lived and worked legally in the U.S. for years; the Washington Post reports roughly 200,000 hold jobs concentrated in health care, food service, warehousing and long-term caregiving. Advocacy groups say deportations to Haiti — a country in acute gang-driven collapse — could begin as soon as Sept. 2, and congressional Democrats renewed a push for a legislative extension.

The Left-Leaning Take

This is cruelty as policy: uprooting hundreds of thousands of legally present workers and sending them toward a country the State Department itself deems too dangerous to visit, gutting hospitals and nursing homes that depend on them. Advocates warn the human costs could be “devastating and possibly deadly.” (Synthesized from immigrant-rights groups and Vox / Guardian-adjacent opinion.)

The Right-Leaning Take

“Temporary” was always meant to be temporary, the mainstream-right argument runs, and the Supreme Court simply restored the executive’s statutory discretion after years of courts second-guessing DHS. A dissenting conservative strand — George Will’s WaPo column argued the Court erred — shows the right is not monolithic on the humanitarian merits. (Synthesized from enforcement-first commentary and George Will’s dissent; George Will is not on the columnist whitelist and is cited only as directional context.)

Europe · Russia’s War on Ukraine

After the deadly Kyiv barrage, Ukraine orders families out of Kramatorsk and answers with drone strikes deep inside Russia — as its interceptor stocks run dry

+

The picture darkened through Wednesday after overnight Russian strikes killed at least 17 in Kyiv and its region — a night in which Ukraine downed 98 of some 115 drones but not one of roughly two dozen ballistic missiles, a starker-than-ever sign of depleted Patriot interceptor stocks. Authorities ordered up to 525 children and their families to evacuate the eastern stronghold of Kramatorsk as Russia intensified its push, and Bloomberg reported Kyiv is running critically low on the only weapon in its arsenal able to stop ballistic missiles.

Ukraine is answering asymmetrically and offshore. Ukrainian drones struck a warehouse of Russia’s largest online retailer, Wildberries, in the Tula region south of Moscow — reportedly the 14th such warehouse set ablaze in under three weeks — part of a campaign to hit the logistics feeding Russia’s war economy. The analysis worth flagging tonight: the interceptor shortfall is now the war’s central variable. Zelensky is arguing that anti-ballistic interceptors “could have saved the lives of those who died today,” turning a battlefield-supply question into a direct plea to Washington and European capitals, even as Moscow presses on land and squeezes Black Sea shipping.

Middle East · Strait of Hormuz

Iran and Oman agree the coordinates of a Hormuz shipping route — a deal taking shape, but not the one Trump wants, even as he insists an agreement is “literally” a day away

+

The diplomacy moved a real step Wednesday: Iran and Oman agreed on the geographic coordinates of a proposed safe shipping corridor through the Strait of Hormuz, with Iranian spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei calling the talks “professional” and “progressing” and both sides saying a joint statement was being finalized. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said progress had been made “but not finality yet.” President Trump went further, telling reporters the strait could reopen “literally by tomorrow” and predicting oil prices would fall “to the floor” — after warning earlier this week that Iran faced a “last chance” before “decapitation.” Iran continued to deny any direct negotiation with the United States, insisting it is dealing only with Oman.

The analysis to carry from this morning’s cargo-ship strike: the emerging arrangement is not the outcome Washington is seeking. As CNN’s analysis put it, an agreement is taking shape “but not one Trump wants” — Tehran is treating supervision of the waterway as a red line “as important as the right to nuclear enrichment,” so a route it helps police would leave Iran with more leverage over Hormuz, not less. A Gulf official put the odds of an Iran–Oman deal by Friday at roughly 50-50. With some 15 million barrels of crude a day still not moving through the strait, the gap between Trump’s imminent-deal messaging and Iran’s narrower, control-preserving track is the story’s live fault line.

Sources: CNN (analysis), Bloomberg, CBS News, CNBC, Washington Post
Middle East · Gaza

Trump’s “Board of Peace” declares a phase-two deal to disarm Hamas — the group signals acceptance of the roadmap while Israel balks and strikes continue

+

Nine months after the U.S.-brokered ceasefire took hold, President Trump announced that his “Board of Peace” had reached what he called a “historic” agreement for the “complete disarmament” of Hamas and other factions and the rebuilding of Gaza. The published roadmap ties Israel’s completion of its Sharm el-Sheikh commitments to Palestinian factions ending military operations, and calls for an implementation timeline within 14 days, after which a national committee would gradually assume civil and security administration of the Strip. Hamas has signaled acceptance of the roadmap in principle — while insisting Israel first honor its prior ceasefire obligations.

The obstacle is Israel. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s government has voiced “serious security concerns” and has not committed to the plan, with strong resistance from Israeli politicians; Al Jazeera reported Israel escalating strikes even as the announcement landed, and rights monitors say attacks have continued to kill Palestinians throughout a truce many describe as an “illusion.” The analysis: this is the same sequencing trap that has stalled the process for months — each side demanding the other move first — now dressed in a formal phase-two framework and a presidential deadline. Whether Trump can convert a plan Hamas nominally accepts and Israel resists into actual disarmament is the open question.

Sources: Newsweek, Al Jazeera, US News / Reuters, Al-Quds

2026 Midterms · Michigan Senate Primary

Progressive Abdul El-Sayed wins Michigan’s Democratic Senate primary, upsetting Schumer-backed Rep. Haley Stevens and handing AIPAC one of its costliest 2026 defeats

+

Abdul El-Sayed — a former Detroit and Wayne County health director backed by Sen. Bernie Sanders and Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez — won Michigan’s Democratic Senate primary held Tuesday (Aug. 4), defeating centrist Rep. Haley Stevens. NBC News projected the race the morning of Aug. 5 after a night in which El-Sayed held a lead of roughly 19,000 votes that Wayne County’s slow-counting ballots never closed. Rival state Sen. Mallory McMorrow endorsed him immediately and called for unity.

El-Sayed, who would be the first Muslim U.S. senator, ran on Medicare for All, campaign-finance reform and ending U.S. military aid to Israel — and Gaza became the race’s defining fault line. He now advances to face Republican nominee and former Rep. Mike Rogers in the Nov. 3 general election in a genuine swing state; pre-primary polling cited by The Hill had Rogers leading El-Sayed by about 10 points (49–39) while running roughly even with Stevens.

The result is a reckoning: grassroots Democratic opinion has turned against unconditional support for Israel, and AIPAC’s record spending could not buy the seat. El-Sayed’s allies frame it as economic populism on health care and wages plus a generational, Mamdani-style insurgency — “not a leftward lurch,” but voters, not consultants, choosing the party’s direction. Justice Department · Attorney General Confirmation

Blanche’s AG nomination reaches the Senate floor on a knife’s edge — Collins is a firm “no,” Curtis and Husted flip to “yes,” and Murkowski and Cassidy hold the deciding votes

+

The Senate Judiciary Committee advanced acting Attorney General Todd Blanche — President Trump’s former personal defense lawyer — on Aug. 4 by a party-line 12–10 vote, sending the nomination to the floor. The turnaround followed Blanche’s late-Sunday (Aug. 3) order formally rescinding the roughly $1.8 billion “Anti-Weaponization Fund,” created in May under a settlement of Trump’s $10 billion IRS lawsuit over leaked tax returns and criticized because payouts could have reached Jan. 6 defendants. Outgoing Sens. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.) and John Cornyn (R-Texas) had conditioned support on killing the fund and released their holds after a written agreement. On Aug. 4, Sen. Susan Collins (R-Maine) announced she will vote no, while Sens. John Curtis (R-Utah) and Jon Husted (R-Ohio) said they will vote yes. That leaves Lisa Murkowski (R-Alaska), “still deliberating,” and Bill Cassidy (R-La.), who has raised concerns about Blanche’s independence, as the pivotal undecideds. With 53 Republicans and unified Democratic opposition, Blanche can lose no more than two more GOP votes beyond Collins (VP JD Vance would break a tie); Majority Leader John Thune is pushing a cloture-then-confirmation sequence, with a final vote expected Friday or Saturday.

The legal backdrop is heavy. On July 13, U.S. District Judge Kathleen Williams issued a 56-page ruling voiding the Trump–IRS settlement as “collusive” — finding Trump and his own Justice Department were never truly adverse — sanctioning Trump’s attorneys and suggesting Blanche himself should face discipline. Trump has appealed.

The Left-Leaning Take

Blanche is unfit and will not keep political interference out of the department; the fund’s rescission is a cynical, last-minute maneuver to buy confirmation votes rather than genuine reform. Judge Williams’s “collusive” finding, in this read, is disqualifying on its own. (Synthesized from MSNBC op-eds and a Washington Post editorial.)

Europe · Russia’s War on Ukraine

A massive overnight Russian barrage kills at least 17 in Kyiv as Ukraine’s defenses down none of the ballistic missiles

+

Russian missiles and drones struck Kyiv and the surrounding region overnight into Wednesday (Aug. 5), killing at least 17 people and wounding 44, President Volodymyr Zelensky said. Russia fired roughly 24 ballistic missiles plus about four Zircon/Oniks-type missiles and some 115 drones; Ukraine intercepted 98 drones but shot down none of the ballistic missiles — a stark sign of depleted Patriot interceptor stocks. Zelensky said the strikes mainly hit civilian sites “with no connection to the war,” naming a brewery, building-materials warehouses and a railway station, and argued that anti-ballistic interceptors “could have saved the lives of those who died today.” Russia’s Defense Ministry disputed that, saying it struck transport, logistics and distribution centers running “war-related operations” (Reuters could not independently verify either claim).

The Kyiv strike compounds a widening Russian pressure campaign that has moved offshore. Over Aug. 4, Moscow said it hit three vessels at Mykolaiv, a ship off Odesa and a container terminal at Chornomorsk; Ukraine’s acting foreign minister warned Russia is trying to turn the Black Sea into a “second Strait of Hormuz.” No vessel has entered Odesa-area ports for nearly two weeks, threatening the export of more than 30 million tonnes of grain and oilseeds, and Turkey urged both sides to protect civilian navigation. The pattern — striking ports, ships and insurers’ appetite for risk — squeezes Ukraine’s economic lifeline even as the land war intensifies.

Middle East · Israel–Lebanon Diplomacy

A seventh round of US-brokered Israel–Lebanon talks ends in Rome inching toward “pilot zone” withdrawals — but with no Hezbollah verification mechanism and Israeli demolitions unresolved

+

Israel and Lebanon concluded three days of direct, U.S.-mediated negotiations in Rome, their seventh round since a framework deal was reached in Washington in June. That framework pairs Hezbollah’s disarmament with a phased Israeli withdrawal from southern Lebanon and deployment of the Lebanese Armed Forces (LAF), beginning in test areas called “pilot zones.” The first stage covers three villages — Zawtar al-Gharbiyeh, Srifa and Froun — where the LAF must verify Hezbollah’s disarmament; Beirut pushed to add locations including Bint Jbeil and Khiam, and demanded Israel halt actions, notably controlled demolitions, that it says obstruct implementation.

Outlets disagree on how much was achieved, a material split worth flagging. Reuters and Al-Monitor framed the round as moving “closer to launching” the pilot zones, with a withdrawal mechanism for the first two areas and talks to continue; Israel Hayom, by contrast, reported “no progress.” Both sides’ accounts agree no verification mechanism for Hezbollah’s disarmament was agreed and that Israel gave no final answer on halting demolitions — leaving the core sequencing dispute unresolved. The backdrop remains hostile: Hezbollah chief Naim Qassem on Tuesday called the direct talks a source of “nothing but shame, humiliation and successive compromises.”

Europe · Migration & Border Politics

The Ceuta border catastrophe drives the EU toward emergency talks as Spain’s death toll climbs and Italy suspends Schengen free travel

+

The aftermath of late July’s mass border rush into Spain’s North African enclave of Ceuta — when an estimated 60,000 to 80,000 mostly young men breached the frontier with Morocco — dominated European diplomacy this week. The episode is reviving the broader debate over Morocco’s role as gatekeeper and the durability of the Schengen system under migratory pressure.

Technology · Quantum Computing

NTT takes a stake in startup OptQC in a bid to build a million-qubit optical quantum computer by 2030

+

On Aug. 3, Japanese telecom giant NTT and University of Tokyo-backed startup OptQC signed a capital-and-business alliance to jointly develop a fault-tolerant, “one-million-qubit-class” photonic (optical) quantum computer, expanding a research partnership first struck in November 2025. NTT is making a strategic equity investment in OptQC (the amount was not disclosed), and the two will collaborate on hardware architecture, supply-chain build-out and use cases across finance, materials, drug discovery, energy optimization and AI. The roadmap is aggressive: co-creation with early users beginning FY2026, supply-chain work in FY2027, proof-of-concept projects on a 10,000-qubit-class system in FY2028, and a commercial million-qubit machine by FY2030. The companies also claim to have developed “the world’s highest-quality quantum light source,” a key component for photonic machines.

An important caveat: the qubit targets and 2030 timeline are self-set corporate goals, not demonstrated results, and the “highest-quality” light-source claim is the companies’ own. Optical/photonic approaches remain less proven at scale than the superconducting and trapped-ion systems pursued by most rivals.

+

At a virtual meeting on Aug. 3, the eight-nation OPEC+ core — Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria and Oman — agreed to raise combined oil output by roughly 188,000 barrels per day in September, the latest in a run of monthly hikes unwinding the voluntary cuts announced in April 2023. Science · Paleontology

A millimeter-long fossil from China is identified as the oldest known cephalopod, pushing the group’s origin back some 25 million years

+

A study in Nature describes 32 tiny shell fossils — some just about a millimeter long, smaller than a grain of rice — from roughly 520-million-year-old early Cambrian rock in southern Shaanxi province, named as a new genus and species, Eoceras shaanxiense. The shells preserve a distinct siphuncle, the internal tube cephalopods use to regulate gas and fluid for buoyancy control, which the authors call the oldest siphuncle on record. That makes these the earliest confirmed cephalopods — the lineage that became squid, octopus and nautilus — and pushes the group’s fossil record back more than 25 million years, placing its origin much closer to the Cambrian explosion. The work was led by Pan Bing of the Nanjing Institute of Geology and Palaeontology (Chinese Academy of Sciences), with UK and U.S. collaborators.

A note on timing and certainty: the paper published July 29 and recirculated heavily on science desks into early August; some outlets frame the specimens as only “possibly” the earliest cephalopod, since the classification of such microfossils can be contested.

Sources: Nature, Sci.News, Phys.org, Chinese Academy of Sciences
