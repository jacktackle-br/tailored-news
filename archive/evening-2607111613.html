Congress · Housing

The biggest housing bill in decades becomes law at midnight — without Trump’s signature, after he held it hostage to a voter-ID demand

+

The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act — described as the most comprehensive federal housing legislation in decades — became law shortly after midnight on Saturday, July 11, without President Trump’s signature. Under Article I, Section 7 of the Constitution, a bill the president neither signs nor vetoes within ten days (Sundays excepted) becomes law automatically. Trump had refused to sign it, calling it “a big yawn” and “of minor importance,” canceling a planned White House signing ceremony, and saying he would only sign once the Senate passed the SAVE America Act — a stalled voter-ID and proof-of-citizenship measure that lacks the 60 votes to clear the chamber. Congress had passed the housing bill by lopsided bipartisan margins last month: 85–5 in the Senate and 358–32 in the House.

The law bundles 56 provisions aimed at boosting supply and lowering costs: competitive “build more” grants for localities that ease zoning, streamlined NEPA environmental reviews and expanded by-right permitting, updated federal model zoning codes (reduced parking minimums, larger allowed building sizes), cheaper manufactured-home construction, and a bar on large institutional investors — those controlling at least 350 single-family homes — buying new single-family houses. The package was a Warren–Scott compromise: a corporate-landlord crackdown traded for supply-side deregulation. Confirmed: the bill is now law, the vote margins, and Trump’s stated SAVE-Act rationale. Not in dispute but worth flagging: even a Trump outside adviser, economist Stephen Moore, questioned the tactic (“I’m not sure how holding this bill hostage is going to move this other bill”).

On Trump’s refusal itself, the record shows discomfort rather than defense even among allies (Stephen Moore’s doubt about the hostage tactic); no qualifying in-window editorial from National Review, The Dispatch or the WSJ editorial board defending the maneuver was found, and none is invented.

US & Iran · Strait of Hormuz

Iran’s Revolutionary Guard declares the Strait of Hormuz “closed until further notice” after firing on a ship — and the U.S. flatly rejects the claim

+

The Iran crisis sharpened sharply since this morning. Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps navy announced — early Sunday, July 12, local time — that it had closed the Strait of Hormuz “until further notice and until regional interference by the U.S. ceases,” after firing a warning shot at a vessel it said was transiting an “unapproved” route. Tehran argues a clause in last month’s memorandum of understanding gives it authority to manage traffic through the waterway. Washington rejects that outright: U.S. Central Command said Iran “does not control the Strait of Hormuz,” that the strait is governed by international law, and that since early May U.S. forces have helped facilitate the passage of more than 800 commercial vessels and some 380 million barrels of crude. U.S. officials read the MoU clause as an obligation on Iran not to impede transit — not a veto over who passes.

Diplomacy ran in parallel to the escalation. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi met his Omani counterpart, Sayyid Badr Albusaidi, in Muscat on Saturday, and Oman drafted a tentative proposal to manage the strait via two corridors — a northern lane through Iranian territorial waters requiring Tehran’s prior approval (no tolls) and a separate route — an attempt to square Iran’s demand for a management role with the U.S. insistence on open passage. Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman and Trump discussed the standoff by phone. The U.S. precondition for resuming nuclear talks is unchanged: Iran must publicly declare all channels of Hormuz open and pledge no further attacks on shipping. Confirmed: the IRGC closure declaration, the warning shot, CENTCOM’s rebuttal, the Muscat meeting and the Omani draft. Unresolved: whether any transit is physically being blocked, the legal reading of the MoU, and whether a weekend negotiating round produces anything.

Sources: RFE/RL, CNN, Times of Israel, Jerusalem Post, Al Jazeera, Anadolu Agency
Russia & Ukraine · Air War

Another overnight barrage hits Kyiv — ballistic missiles get through as Ukraine strikes back at Russian vessels

+

Russia launched a fresh large-scale overnight assault on Ukraine into Saturday, July 11, firing roughly 12 missiles — including about six ballistic missiles — and some 121 drones. Ukrainian air defenses downed most of the drones and several missiles, but the ballistic rounds reached their targets, igniting fires and explosions across Kyiv’s Solomianskyi, Darnytskyi and Dniprovskyi districts. Reports put the toll at two people killed and roughly a dozen to nearly twenty wounded nationwide, with about 11 hurt in the capital, among them an 11-year-old boy; several required emergency hospitalization. Casualty figures varied across outlets through the day. Ukraine, for its part, said it had struck Russian vessels.

The strike extends a punishing month for the capital: coverage notes more than 60 people killed in Kyiv and its metropolitan area since the start of July, underscoring how Russia has intensified long-range bombardment of urban centers even as front-line movement stays incremental. The pattern — mass drone salvos meant to saturate and exhaust air defenses, paired with hard-to-intercept ballistic missiles that slip through — is now the defining feature of the summer air war, and a steady driver of civilian casualties and infrastructure damage far from the front.

Europe · Nuclear Deterrence

France and Poland hold their first formal talks on a European “nuclear umbrella,” testing whether Paris’s deterrent can cover the continent

+

France and Poland held their first official consultations on July 10 in Paris — the inaugural meeting of a bilateral Nuclear Steering Group — on extending the French nuclear deterrent to European partners. Warsaw described the session as a “strategic dialogue on long-term deterrence” centered on the concept of “advanced” or forward deterrence. The talks flow from President Emmanuel Macron’s initiative, floated in early 2025, to open a conversation with European leaders about whether France’s arsenal — the EU’s only nuclear force after Brexit — could help shield the continent. By mid-2026, nine European states, including the UK, Germany, the Netherlands and the Nordics, had agreed to structured bilateral discussions in the framework.

The move is a marker of how far European security thinking has shifted amid doubts about the durability of the U.S. umbrella and the grinding war on the EU’s eastern flank. For front-line Poland, a French deterrent conversation is both a hedge and a signal to Moscow; for Paris, it is a bid to convert its independent force into continental leverage. The hard questions — command and control, forward basing, and whether any ally would truly rely on French warheads in a crisis — remain unresolved, and analysts caution this is an opening of dialogue, not a treaty.

Sources: GlobalSecurity (Élysée), GlobalSecurity (Polish MFA), Bruegel, CSS / ETH Zurich

Immigration · ICE Use of Force

Houston man killed by ICE was not the target, witnesses and the medical examiner contradict the agency’s “self-defense” account

+

Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, a 52-year-old Mexican national and homebuilder who had lived in the U.S. for roughly 35 years with no criminal record, was shot and killed by an ICE officer around 6:50 a.m. on Tuesday, July 7, in Houston’s East End as he drove a construction crew to a job site. DHS says officers were surveilling a white van tied to two other people — neither of them Salgado Araujo, who a DHS official said “resembled the target” — that he rammed an ICE vehicle and tried to run over an agent, and that the officer fired “in self-defense.” The developments that pushed the story into the last 24 hours cut against that account: the Harris County Medical Examiner ruled the death a homicide; three men riding in Salgado Araujo’s van told their attorney the van never rammed agents and no agent’s life was in danger; Rep. Sylvia Garcia (D-TX) said the acting ICE director confirmed Salgado Araujo was not the person officers were seeking; and DHS has refused to release the shooting officer’s name.

Also newly reported in-window: the agents involved were not wearing body cameras, despite a DHS pledge earlier in 2026 — after the Minneapolis-area killings of Renee Good and Alex Pretti — to “rapidly acquire and deploy” them. DHS’s Office of Inspector General is leading the probe, while the Harris County DA says federal officials sidelined Texas investigators. Four Houston-area House Democrats demanded an independent investigation and release of “complete and unedited” footage, hundreds marched in Magnolia Park, and Mexico’s government is pressing for a criminal investigation. The killing is the first fatal immigration-agent shooting since Good and Pretti, and it lands amid a funding-fueled surge that outlets report has pushed ICE arrests toward roughly 2,000 a day. Confirmed: the homicide ruling, that he was not the target, the absence of body cameras, and the OIG probe. Reported and disputed: the ramming/self-defense narrative (DHS versus the van’s passengers). Unverified: the officer’s identity.

Environment · Endangered Species Act

Interior finalizes a rule so that destroying a species’ habitat no longer counts as “harm” under the Endangered Species Act

+

On Friday, July 10, the Interior Department, with the Commerce Department, finalized a rule rescinding the regulatory definition of “harm” under the 1973 Endangered Species Act. Since 1981 the term had included habitat modification or degradation that kills or injures wildlife by impairing essential behaviors such as breeding, feeding, or sheltering; the new rule leaves “harm” undefined, so destroying a listed species’ nest or habitat would no longer, by itself, be prohibited. OIRA completed review of the final rule on July 7, and Interior announced the finalization Friday afternoon. The administration’s legal rationale rests on the Supreme Court’s 2024 Loper Bright v. Raimondo decision — that agencies must follow a statute’s single best meaning rather than a preferred interpretation — and Interior’s release framed the prior definition as an “unlawful regulatory intrusion” on private property rights. Critics counter that the Supreme Court upheld the habitat-inclusive definition in the 1995 Babbitt v. Sweet Home case.

Practically, outlets report the change could open critical wildlife habitat to oil and gas drilling, mining, logging, and development so long as animals are not directly killed or injured. Hundreds of thousands of public comments opposed the change when it was proposed in spring 2025. Environmental groups — the Center for Biological Diversity, Earthjustice, and Defenders of Wildlife — call it a “death knell for America’s wildlife,” arguing that habitat loss is the leading driver of extinction. Confirmed: the rule was finalized July 10, the definition is rescinded, and the rationale rests on Loper Bright. The scope of real-world impact is a contested projection, not settled fact.

The Left-Leaning Take

No qualifying in-window commentary was found from the left opinion desks (Mother Jones, Vox, Slate, Guardian opinion), and none is invented here. The oppositional voices in the record are advocacy groups — the Center for Biological Diversity and Earthjustice, echoed by Common Dreams — which sit outside the commentary tier; their framing is that habitat loss drives extinction, so removing habitat from the definition of “harm” guts the Act’s core protection. Flagged as advocacy framing, not a synthesized editorial take.

The Right-Leaning Take

No qualifying in-window editorial was found from the mainstream-right whitelist (National Review, The Dispatch, WSJ editorial, Reason), and none is invented. The supportive framing in the record is the administration’s own — Interior Secretary Doug Burgum casting the old rule as a “weaponized” burden on families and businesses, and the department’s Loper Bright-grounded property-rights argument — carried as straight news rather than as a Tier-3 conservative opinion take.

DR Congo · Public Health

Congo’s Ebola death toll passes 600 and spreads to a fourth province as the WHO keeps national risk “very high”

+

As of data through July 8 (reported July 9–10), the Democratic Republic of Congo had recorded 1,792 confirmed cases and 625 deaths in an outbreak of the Bundibugyo species of Ebola, described as the worst start to any Ebola outbreak on record. The latest update logged 33 new cases and 25 new deaths, drawn from Ituri — the epicenter, at roughly 1,631 cases and 535 deaths — and North Kivu (about 158 cases and 89 deaths). Critically, the disease has now jumped beyond Ituri: cases tied to travel from the Nia Nia health zone have appeared in Wamba (Haut-Uélé) and in Kisangani (Tshopo), a city of more than a million, marking spread into a fourth province. The WHO says it “cannot say” the outbreak is stabilizing and rates the national risk “very high.”

The regional and international footprint continues to widen: Uganda has reported 20 confirmed cases including two deaths, and imported cases have reached the United States and France. A clinical trial that began July 2 is evaluating the monoclonal antibody MBP134 and the antiviral remdesivir. The confirmed figures come from DRC authorities via the WHO and UN, which caution that the “true scale has not yet been fully established.”

Science · Paleontology

Thailand names its first long-necked “mamenchisaurid” dinosaur, Uragasaurus, from a single vertebra

+

A study in Scientific Reports, published July 9–10, describes Uragasaurus kalasinensis, the first formally named mamenchisaurid sauropod from Thailand. The animal is known from a single well-preserved anterior dorsal vertebra (specimen PRC 460) recovered at the Phu Noi fossil site in Kalasin province, in the Lower Phu Kradung Formation, which the authors date to the Late Jurassic. CT scanning revealed a distinctive camellate — honeycomb-like — internal air-sac structure and a unique Y-shaped lamina configuration that together mark it as a new taxon.

The herbivore is estimated at roughly 18 to 20 meters long, and the specimen is housed at Mahasarakham University’s Palaeontological Research and Education Centre. Mamenchisaurids — the extravagantly long-necked sauropods best known from China — help chart how these giants dispersed across Jurassic Asia, and a Thai member extends that story into Southeast Asia.

Sources: Scientific Reports, Sci.News, Phys.org, Khaosod English
