Religion · Christian Nationalism & Belief

A former Archbishop of Canterbury argues Europe’s new nationalists want Christianity as ballast, not belief — a sacred alibi for anti-Muslim politics

+

In an interview for New Humanist that ricocheted across the British and international press around Aug. 20–21, Rowan Williams — theologian, poet and former head of the Anglican Communion — offered a pointed diagnosis of how faith is being conscripted by the populist right. His claim is not that figures like Nigel Farage or Tommy Robinson have undergone some conservative religious awakening, but almost the opposite: that Christianity is being deployed as cultural “ballast,” a way to lend anti-Muslim sentiment the borrowed dignity of a “we’re a Christian society” identity while remaining indifferent to the actual content of the faith. Williams suspects Farage does not “lie awake at night thinking about his soul,” and says of Robinson that if he is genuinely attempting Christian discipleship, he would “really want to ask him some awkward questions.”

Underneath the headlines is a genuinely interesting argument about the sociology of belief: that Christian nationalism is animated less by conviction than by “the very simple search for someone to blame,” and that such movements reach for “a bit of theology injected into them” precisely because tribal identity craves a sacred alibi. What makes this more than a political spat is that it comes from a man who spent a decade as the institutional face of English Christianity, now warning that the tradition is most endangered not by secularism but by those who would hollow it out and wear it as a flag. It is a live reprise of an old question — whether a religion can survive being useful — from an unusually credentialed source.

Sources: New Humanist, The Irish News, Middle East Monitor
History & Anthropology · Madagascar & Restitution

France returns a king’s skull taken 128 years ago — but for the Sakalava, giving back the bone may reopen a struggle over who is allowed to be sacred

+

As France prepared to hand back three colonial-era skulls — one believed to be that of King Toera of the Menabe, killed and beheaded by French forces around 1897 — the anthropologist Maurizio Esposito La Rossa used the occasion for a bracing essay in The Conversation (republished Aug. 18) about what repatriation actually does and does not accomplish. The Western frame treats returning human remains as the tidy correction of a colonial wrong. But among the Sakalava, royal relics are not symbols; they are instruments of power. Fragments of a dead sovereign’s body carry hasina, the generative force of royal ancestors, and it is that force — not election or descent alone — that legitimizes the living king. Only the recognized “gold” princes may possess relics; a “bathing of the relics” ceremony completes a dead ruler’s transformation into an ancestor and consecrates his successor.

So a skull is not a museum object being restored to a nation; it is a potent political and spiritual charge re-entering a system where rival branches already contest the Menabe throne. Esposito La Rossa’s point, drawn from his new book Sovereigns of Gold, Spirits of Silver, is that restitution can as easily detonate as heal: it forces the question of who controls the relic, and therefore who gets to rule. It is a rich reappraisal of the whole repatriation debate — a reminder that “giving it back” assumes the receiving culture shares our sense of what the thing is, when in fact the bone may be more alive, and more dangerous, on arrival than any European vitrine imagined.

Sources: The Conversation, allAfrica
Natural Science · Acoustic Ecology & Soil

Beneath our feet, an audible world — the chewing of earthworms, the creep of growing roots — and why learning to eavesdrop on it may tell us whether the living soil is dying

+

In an Aeon essay published Aug. 17, the ecologist Ella Browning (Leverhulme Centre for Nature Recovery, Oxford) makes the case that the ground is not the silent, inert stuff we imagine but a soundscape we are only now learning to record — using passive acoustic sensors of the kind long trained on bats and crickets, now pointed downward. “Worm munch,” as she calls it, is the audible chewing and burrowing of earthworms, alongside the faint sounds of roots pushing through soil; captured on a buried microphone, it becomes a signature of biological activity.

The interesting turn is conceptual, not merely charming: acoustic monitoring could let us gauge the health of a whole subterranean ecosystem non-invasively, the way birdsong indexes the vitality of a forest. In an era when soil degradation is one of the quieter planetary emergencies — farmland biodiversity collapsing largely out of sight — the prospect of simply listening to whether the underground is alive reframes conservation as an act of attention. Browning’s essay, which includes a fifteen-minute recording of Oxfordshire soil, sits in a lineage of “acoustic ecology” that keeps expanding the domain of the audible, and it carries a gentle philosophical undertow: the health of the living world may hinge on our willingness to notice forms of life whose language we had assumed was silence.

Sources: Aeon
