The Iran War · Decision Point

Trump huddles with advisers on a “massive attack” but issues no order — as Iran strikes back at U.S. Gulf bases and a leaked Oval Office tirade shows a president in “revenge mode”

+

The morning brief left Trump “close to” a decision on a “massive attack”; by Friday evening he had convened it but not made it. Trump met behind closed doors at the White House with senior cabinet members and advisers on Friday, July 24, to weigh significantly expanding military action against Iran, but two U.S. officials said no new strike orders had been issued and the president set no deadline. U.S. forces pressed on regardless: after completing a 13th consecutive night of strikes overnight, the military kept up the campaign Friday as the war neared the five-month mark, with Iran’s Tasnim agency reporting four killed and five wounded near Ahvaz and blasts reported in Khorramabad, Jask, Bandar Abbas and on Qeshm Island.

What is new since morning is the two-way escalation and a rare window into Trump’s own mood. Iran retaliated across the Gulf: its army and Revolutionary Guard claimed Arash kamikaze-drone strikes on the Isa Air Base in Bahrain (hitting fuel tanks, warehouses and barracks) and on U.S. facilities in Kuwait and Jordan, and said they targeted an Amazon data center in Bahrain; six drones were intercepted over Erbil, briefly closing its airport, and Bahrain sounded sirens. The Pentagon did not confirm any U.S. facilities were hit. Meanwhile the Wall Street Journal reported that in a recent White House meeting Trump launched into an expletive-laden tirade, calling Iran’s leaders “scumbags” and “lunatics”; a senior official said he is in “revenge mode,” sees few options besides continued strikes, and some aides fear the war — higher prices, sinking approval, more than a dozen U.S. troop deaths — is “consuming his presidency.” Reporting also indicated Trump has shown private interest in deploying a limited number of ground troops for targeted objectives, short of a full invasion. Oil, which topped $100 Thursday, reversed hard: Brent fell about 4% Friday to settle near $97 a barrel — its biggest one-day drop since late June — on conflicting reports about a possible return to talks.

The Left-Leaning Take

Slate argues the escalation “makes little sense” on its own terms and reads the leaked tirade as confirmation the war has become a matter of personal grievance rather than strategy — pointing to Trump’s musings about seizing the Strait of Hormuz and levying a 20% transit toll as evidence of aims untethered from any exit. The New Republic calls the reported interest in ground troops a “horrible new reason to worry,” and the Guardian’s editorial line casts a president “breaking things” with no one able to say how it ends. The through-line: a fifth-month war of choice widening for want of an off-ramp.

The Right-Leaning Take

Strikingly, much of the mainstream right is not cheering escalation but questioning the strategy. National Review (“Trump Doesn’t Have the Stomach for a Long War with Iran”) and the WSJ editorial board argue the campaign has “failed to intimidate Tehran” and that the Iranians believe they can outlast a president who no longer wants the fight — a drift the Journal has called a “strategic retreat.” The hawkish case, echoed in administration framing, is that the regime respects only force, so half-measures invite more attacks. Reason’s libertarian line rejects the open-ended, unauthorized war outright. Here the right’s “no coherent endgame” critique converges with the left rather than splitting from it.

Sources: NPR, CNBC, TIME, Bloomberg, Just Security, Slate, National Review, WSJ editorial via The Hill
Energy Policy · At the White House

Trump declares a “nuclear renaissance” as four U.S. startups hit reactor criticality ahead of his July 4 deadline

+

In an Oval Office ceremony Friday, July 24, flanked by industry leaders, Trump announced that four American nuclear-energy startups — Antares, Deployable Energy, Aalo Atomics and Valar Atomics — had successfully brought advanced reactors to “criticality” under an Energy Department pilot launched last year, exceeding his goal of three cutting-edge reactors starting up safely by July 4 (Aalo’s reached criticality in the early hours of Independence Day). “Today we’re celebrating an historic milestone for civilian nuclear energy and the renaissance of nuclear power in America,” Trump said. The milestone lands 14 months after he signed four executive orders directing the government to quadruple U.S. nuclear capacity by 2050, compressing Nuclear Regulatory Commission approval timelines and setting the nation’s 250th anniversary as a target for advanced projects to achieve criticality. The announcement doubled as an implicit answer to the week’s energy shock: with Brent whipsawing around $100 on the Gulf war, the administration is casting reactors as domestic insurance against oil-price volatility and Chinese competition.

Middle East · A Widening War

The Iran war spills outward — Britain pulls its diplomats, airlines flee the Gulf, and a China-backed Pakistan channel quietly reopens

+

Beyond Washington’s decision-making, the day’s new development was the war visibly internationalizing. After Iran’s claimed drone strikes on U.S. bases in Bahrain, Kuwait and Jordan — and with Erbil’s airport briefly shut — the United Kingdom withdrew its remaining embassy staff from Iran and advised against all travel, after Iran branded Britain an “accomplice” to the U.S. campaign. Qatar Airways suspended passenger flights to Bahrain, Kuwait and Erbil through July 31, other carriers canceled Gulf routes, and shipowners increasingly transit the Red Sea with transponders dark to dodge the Houthis’ declared blockade of Saudi Arabia. Iran, meanwhile, rejected the U.S. ceasefire proposal carried by Iraq’s prime minister, holding that it will not accept a “temporary” deal that leaves control of the Strait of Hormuz unresolved.

The counter-current is diplomatic. Reuters reported that Pakistan — in a push initiated by China — is exploring a path back to U.S.–Iran talks, with Iran’s interior minister, Eskandar Momeni, visiting Islamabad twice in ten days and meeting army chief Asim Munir; Beijing said it “supports the mediation efforts.” But a Pakistani official said an end to attacks on Saudi Arabia and the other Gulf states is now a precondition for any negotiations, and all sides cautioned the obstacles remain high. The significance is twofold: the conflict is metastasizing along economic and diplomatic fault lines — aviation, shipping, allied capitals — even as a Beijing-Islamabad track emerges to compete with the U.S. pressure campaign for control of how, and on whose terms, the war might end.

Europe · Russia’s War on Ukraine

A Russian missile hits a Ukrainian arms-industry event near Kyiv, killing 10 — and triggers a criminal probe into who gathered them there

+

A Russian missile strike on Friday, July 24, hit a defense-industry event in the Kyiv region, killing at least 10 people and wounding more than 100, President Volodymyr Zelensky said. In an unusual step, Ukraine’s prosecutor general opened a criminal inquiry into who authorized holding the gathering — reportedly an arms exhibition drawing representatives of the country’s weapons industry — and whether the risks were properly assessed under martial law, a rare public reckoning over Ukraine’s own wartime decision-making. Officials tallied at least 21 dead across both countries as Moscow and Kyiv traded fire.

Ukraine’s response ran deep into Russian territory and the sea. Overnight, the SBU struck oil refineries, pumping stations and a radar site inside Russia and occupied Crimea, and Kyiv said its forces sank the FSB vessel Izumrud near Novorossiysk and damaged tankers south of Kerch — part of a sustained campaign against Moscow’s energy revenue and its Black Sea “shadow fleet.” Ground fighting persisted around Hulyaipole, Kostiantynivka and Kherson, where forced civilian evacuations hinted at a possible Russian southern push. The two fronts capture the war’s current shape: Russia inflicting mass-casualty strikes on Ukrainian soil while Ukraine increasingly takes the fight to Russian oil and maritime infrastructure — and, in the Kyiv probe, a government willing to investigate itself even mid-war.

Trade · Now In Effect

Trump’s forced-labor tariffs on 60 economies take effect at midnight — hitting 99.4% of U.S. imports as Wall Street sinks and Brent tops $100

+

New U.S. tariffs of 10% to 12.5% took effect at 12:01 a.m. ET Friday, July 24, after the U.S. Trade Representative published its final Section 301 action on Thursday, replacing Trump’s expiring temporary 10% worldwide tariff. The measure covers the top 60 trading partners — about 99.4% of all goods imported into the U.S. — and USTR Ambassador Jamieson Greer framed it as a response to those economies’ failure to enforce bans on goods made with forced labor, calling the U.S. “the only country in the world to adopt, and effectively enforce, a ban on imports made with forced labor.” The structure sets 12.5% on roughly 45 economies (including China and Vietnam) and 10% on about 15 to 17 partners with some existing prohibitions (including Canada, Mexico, the UK and India); oil and gas, fertilizer, and USMCA duty-free goods are exempt. It follows a March 12 investigation launch, a June 2 proposed action, more than 1,600 public comments and July 7–9 hearings.

The action is a deliberate legal end-run: after the Supreme Court struck down Trump’s IEEPA “emergency” global tariffs as illegal in February, the White House pivoted to Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 as sturdier statutory footing. Markets had an ugly Thursday, though the sell-off was driven mainly by oil and tech earnings rather than the tariffs directly: the Dow fell 506.93 points (0.97%) to 51,711.65, the S&P 500 dropped 1.21% to 7,408.30 and the Nasdaq slid 2.15% to 25,137.69, dragged by Alphabet (down about 6.5% on higher AI capital-spending guidance) and Tesla (down about 14% on falling profit), while Brent crude settled above $100 a barrel for the first time since late May on the Iran war and the 10-year Treasury yield hit its highest since January 2025.

The Left-Leaning Take

The synthesized left frame is a consumer tax wearing a human-rights costume: the forced-labor findings are read as a pretext to keep a blanket tariff wall standing after the courts knocked the original one down, with the real cost landing on U.S. shoppers and importers. The scramble to relocate the same policy into a sturdier statute is cast as an admission the emergency legal theory was never sound. (No dedicated in-window Mother Jones / Vox / Slate / Guardian opinion column isolated to this action surfaced — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Unusually, the free-trade right is hostile. The Dispatch (“Here Comes Another Round of Sham Tariffs”) calls the forced-labor rationale “nothing more than legal cover” and a “pretext,” argues the White House “scrambled for a replacement” after SCOTUS voided the IEEPA tariffs, and notes a lower court has already knocked the new levies down (the case is on appeal); Reason calls the Section 301 gambit legally “dubious.” The pro-Trump case rests on leverage and durability — a tariff wall built to survive the courts — but here the mainstream-right and libertarian objections converge rather than split. (No in-window WSJ editorial or National Review column on this action surfaced — provisional.)

War Powers · Constitutional Showdown

Congress splits on the Iran war — the House rebukes Trump a second time, 214–208, but the Senate refuses to bind him, 47–49

+

On Thursday, July 23, the House voted 214–208 to adopt a war-powers resolution — sponsored by Rep. Pramila Jayapal (D-Wash.) — directing President Trump to “remove the use of United States Armed Forces from hostilities against” Iran, the second such rebuke the chamber has passed since the war restarted. Four Republicans crossed to join every Democrat: Thomas Massie (Ky.), Brian Fitzpatrick (Pa.), Warren Davidson (Ohio) and Tom Barrett (Mich.) — the same four defectors as before. The measure is a non-binding concurrent resolution that does not reach Trump’s desk, and Democrats forced the vote as lawmakers left Washington for a five-week August recess.

Hours later, the Senate voted 47–49 to reject advancing a competing binding joint resolution from Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.) that would have legally required Trump to halt hostilities. The party-line breakdown was the story: Susan Collins (R-Maine) was the lone Republican to vote to advance it, while John Fetterman (D-Pa.) was the lone Democrat against — netting the failure. Lawmakers cited mounting costs: 18 U.S. troops have died since fighting began in February, four of them since July 17, with roughly 500 injuries; Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth told a July 21 hearing the war has cost $37.5 billion. Trump has argued the War Powers Resolution’s constraints are themselves unconstitutional and would veto any binding curb, and Rep. Gregory Meeks (D-N.Y.) filed a resolution to force Speaker Mike Johnson to initiate litigation to enforce the vote.

The Left-Leaning Take

Congress is belatedly reasserting a constitutional power it never surrendered over a war lawmakers never authorized, now in its sixth month and killing Americans. The synthesized left frame singles out Fetterman and the 49 Senate Republicans for “sinking” the one measure with legal teeth, treating the split votes as proof the war can command neither a durable majority nor public consent. (In-window progressive coverage came from Common Dreams, activist news rather than the named opinion desks; no dedicated Mother Jones / Vox / Slate / Guardian column surfaced — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right largely held the line — 208 House Republicans and all but one Senate Republican voted to preserve the president’s hand, on the argument that you do not tie a commander-in-chief mid-war and that a symbolic vote only signals division to Tehran. The friction is a restrainer-libertarian strand: Massie on the floor and Reason argue Congress must reclaim the war power from an open-ended, unauthorized conflict. Intra-right dissent (anti-Trump): The Bulwark casts the war as improvisation dressed as strategy — label that a minority anti-Trump line, not the mainstream-right position. (No in-window NR / Dispatch / WSJ editorial endorsing the vote surfaced — provisional.)

Diplomacy · Nonproliferation

A day after signing a 30-year nuclear pact with Riyadh, Trump makes it “totally subject to” Saudi Arabia joining the Abraham Accords

+

On Wednesday, July 22, the U.S. and Saudi Arabia signed a civilian nuclear cooperation agreement — inked by Energy Secretary Chris Wright and Saudi Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman — reportedly running about 30 years and involving U.S. companies in developing the kingdom’s nuclear program. The signed announcement made no mention of Israel. Then on Thursday, July 23, less than 24 hours later, Trump attached a new condition, writing that the deal “will be approved, but is totally subject to Saudi Arabia joining the very respected and successful Abraham Accords.” He also said the agreement would bar uranium enrichment, addressing proliferation fears that the plants could be used to enrich weapons-grade material or reprocess spent fuel.

As of Thursday, Saudi Arabia had not responded to the new demand. Riyadh has long held that it will not normalize with Israel absent a credible pathway to a Palestinian state — a position hardened by the war in Gaza. The move retroactively binds Trump’s signature foreign-policy prize to a technical energy pact his own administration had just concluded without it, colliding two hard constraints: his Abraham Accords ambition versus Riyadh’s statehood insistence, which Israel’s government will not grant. It also lands amid the Iran war, where Saudi Arabia is now a direct Houthi target — giving Riyadh more reason to want U.S. security guarantees but less domestic room to embrace Israel. The likely result is protracted limbo, and the pact still faces a mandatory Congressional review.

Technology · AI Silicon

AI-chip startup Etched doubles to a $10.3 billion valuation, betting a transformer-only chip can beat Nvidia’s GPUs

+

On July 23, AI-chip startup Etched — founded in 2022 by three Harvard dropouts — closed a $300 million Series C at a $10.3 billion valuation, roughly doubling the ~$5 billion mark it carried in December, when it raised $500 million. Proceeds go to scaling production and customer deployments, making it a notable data point in the wave of inference-specialized silicon challenging Nvidia’s dominance.

Energy · Power Demand

The IEA says global electricity demand is accelerating — AI, EVs and air-conditioning push growth toward 3.8% by 2027

+

The International Energy Agency released its Electricity Mid-Year Update 2026 on July 23, forecasting global power-demand growth of 3.6% in 2026 and 3.8% in 2027, up from 3% in 2025, with consumption reaching about 30,700 terawatt-hours in 2027 (versus 28,600 in 2025). The report also flags a real-world shock tied to the Gulf war: disruptions to LNG flows through the Strait of Hormuz have pushed Asian and European gas prices to their highest since the 2022–23 energy crisis, prompting emergency demand-curbing measures in some regions. The quantified acceleration — and the reminder that electrification is colliding with a fresh fossil-fuel supply squeeze — is the newsworthy shift.

Climate · Attribution

Heat, not lack of rain, is now parching Europe: scientists find climate change made bone-dry soils up to 11 times likelier

+

A World Weather Attribution study published July 23 finds that summer 2026 is shaping up as one of Europe’s most significant heat-and-drought events in recent years — and that record heat, not a rainfall deficit, is now the dominant driver, evaporating soil moisture faster than rain can replenish it. The headline attribution figures: extremely dry soils are roughly 5× more likely in western Europe and about 11× more likely in eastern Europe today than in a world without human-caused climate change. Between April and June, swaths of the continent from Portugal to southern Finland saw below-average rainfall; combined with successive heatwaves, that drove unusually low river levels and severe wildfires — 116,000 hectares burned in Spain and 42,000 in France.

The analysis, conducted by scientists across roughly a dozen countries using established peer-reviewed methods, reframes the problem: as the climate warms, drought in Europe is increasingly a story of heat pulling moisture out of the ground rather than of skies failing to deliver rain — a shift with direct implications for how water and land are managed on the continent.

Sources: World Weather Attribution, Euronews
Biomedicine · Oncology

Chemotherapy leaves a readable DNA “signature” in childhood tumors — a SickKids study points to catching resistance earlier

+

A study led by Toronto’s Hospital for Sick Children (SickKids), published in Nature on July 23, finds that nearly half of childhood tumors treated with common chemotherapies carry detectable DNA changes linked to the treatment within 18 months. By combining whole-genome sequencing with detailed medical records and computational methods developed at SickKids, the team identified distinct genomic “signatures” left by different chemotherapy drugs, with some patterns appearing as early as 91 days after treatment began. Platinum-based chemotherapy — given to nearly half of all treated patients — was among the most striking: within 18 months, 48% of tumors showed a detectable platinum-related signature on surviving cancer cells.

The researchers examined more than 600 tumors from 544 patients across Canada, Australia and the United States, and say the signatures could help clinicians spot treatment resistance earlier — enabling more precise use of chemotherapy and potentially reducing its toxic effects on young patients. It is a step toward reading, in the tumor’s own genome, when a therapy is starting to fail.

Sources: Nature, GenomeWeb, Mirage News
