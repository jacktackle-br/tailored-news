The Shutdown · The Layoff Turn

Day six, no exit: the Senate fails again as the White House moves to make the firings permanent

+

The stalemate hardened Tuesday. The Senate again failed to advance a stopgap — the chamber has now churned through more than nine failed votes since the lapse began on October 1, with Democrats still withholding the votes needed to clear the 60-vote threshold until Republicans negotiate on the expiring Affordable Care Act subsidies, and Majority Leader John Thune still short of the handful of Democratic defectors it would take to reopen the government. Neither leader blinked, and no new vote was scheduled that looked likely to pass.

What is new since this morning is the sharpening of the administration’s threat into action: budget director Russ Vought signaled the White House is preparing to convert shutdown furloughs into permanent reductions-in-force, turning a funding lapse into a tool to shrink agencies Democrats favor — and federal unions raced back to court to block it, arguing mass firings during a shutdown are unlawful. Meanwhile the real-world toll kept mounting: roughly 2 million federal workers face their first missed checks within days, troops and air-traffic controllers are working unpaid, and nutrition and safety-net programs are running down reserves. (Partial — layoff figures are administration estimates and the reduction-in-force litigation is moving in real time.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left (Vox, Slate, Mother Jones, Guardian opinion) reads the layoff pivot as the mask slipping: an administration that wanted to gut the civil service is using the shutdown as cover to do it, and the people “hostage” here are the workers it is firing, not the Democrats refusing to fund a subsidy cliff. Caving now, they argue, would reward exactly that hardball. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right (WSJ editorial, National Review, The Dispatch) keeps the blame on Democrats, who could reopen the government today with a clean bill and instead are holding it shut over an unrelated health-care demand; if the lapse drags on, they say, the resulting cuts are the minority’s doing. Libertarians (Reason) see a budget process broken by design, and warn that governing-by-RIF is as lawless as governing-by-shutdown. (Directional.)

Sources: AP, Reuters, NPR, PBS NewsHour
Troops in the Cities · The Court Fight

Chicago sues, Portland is blocked: the Guard deployments run into a wall of judges

+

The president’s drive to put National Guard troops into Democratic-run cities as part of his immigration crackdown collided with the courts. Late Sunday, U.S. District Judge Karin Immergut issued a temporary restraining order barring the administration from deploying any federalized Guard units to Portland, Oregon — extending an earlier block to cover out-of-state troops after the White House tried to route in California soldiers instead. On Monday, Illinois and the city of Chicago went to court too, suing to stop the federalization of roughly 300 Illinois guardsmen and the deployment of some 400 more from Texas, as Gov. JB Pritzker accused the administration of manufacturing an emergency.

The legal theory is the same in both cities: the Democratic governors argue the administration is deliberately recasting largely peaceful protest as insurrection to justify a domestic troop deployment the law does not authorize, while the Justice Department says the troops are needed to protect federal immigration agents and facilities. Protests flared again around an ICE facility outside Chicago as the suits were filed, and the dispute is now racing toward the appeals courts. (Partial — the orders are preliminary and the appellate rulings are still pending.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees an authoritarian tell: sending soldiers into American cities over the objections of their elected governors, on a pretext of “insurrection” that the footage doesn’t support, is the use of the military as a political weapon. They credit the judges as the guardrail holding — for now. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right is split. Many back the president’s authority to protect federal agents and property when local officials won’t, and call the lawsuits obstruction. But Reason and parts of the legal right warn that federalizing the Guard over a governor’s veto is a dangerous precedent whoever holds the White House, and The Bulwark (anti-Trump right) casts it as exactly the overreach the courts exist to check — an intra-right view, not the party line. (Directional.)

Sources: NPR, PBS NewsHour, AP, Reuters

France · Government Collapse

Twenty-seven days and out: Lecornu quits, and Macron’s France lurches deeper into crisis

+

French Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu resigned — just 27 days into the job and only hours after unveiling his cabinet, making him the shortest-serving prime minister in modern French history. President Emmanuel Macron accepted the resignation. The government unravelled almost on arrival: Lecornu’s line-up looked nearly identical to that of his ousted predecessor François Bayrou, and the conservative Les Républicains — whose roughly 50 seats he needed — recoiled, objecting in particular to the choice of defense minister. Lecornu said “the conditions” for him to govern “were not met,” blaming the parties’ “partisan fervour.”

It is the latest spasm of a crisis that has run since Macron’s snap election left the National Assembly split three ways, with no bloc able to pass a budget. The president is cornered: he has so far refused to dissolve parliament for fresh elections — which polls suggest would only strengthen Marine Le Pen’s National Rally — and has flatly ruled out resigning before his term ends in 2027. That leaves him naming a fourth prime minister in little more than a year while a stalled 2026 budget, a ballooning deficit and jittery bond markets wait on a government that can actually command a majority. The impasse is now as much about France’s institutions as about any one premier.

Sources: AP / PBS NewsHour, Reuters, BBC, Guardian
Ukraine · The War on the Grid

A long-range autumn: Russia pounds Ukraine’s energy system as Kyiv strikes deep into Russia

+

Beyond the money fight in Brussels, the war itself is escalating in the air. As winter approaches, Russia has again turned its long-range arsenal on Ukraine’s energy infrastructure, launching some of the heaviest combined missile-and-drone barrages in months — overnight strikes hit Kharkiv and other cities, sparking fires and casualties, and Moscow has been firing missiles at a tempo Ukrainian officials describe as the highest since early in the war. The aim, as in previous Octobers, is to black out the grid before the cold sets in.

Kyiv is answering in kind, reaching ever deeper into Russia: Ukrainian drones struck targets in occupied Crimea and Russian energy and refining sites, part of a sustained campaign to degrade Moscow’s oil revenue and fuel supply. The ground line around Kupiansk and the eastern front has barely shifted, but the escalating strikes on each side’s infrastructure underscore why Europe’s scramble to finance Ukraine’s air defenses and energy repairs has become so urgent — the question of who can outlast the other through the winter may matter as much as any battlefield map. The Shutdown · Day Six

No deal, no paychecks: the shutdown grinds into day six as the Senate falls short again

+

The federal shutdown that began at midnight on October 1 — the first day of the new fiscal year — pushed into its sixth day with no resolution. On Monday the Senate again failed to advance the Republicans’ “clean” stopgap, falling short of the 60 votes needed to break a filibuster for what news desks tallied as roughly the ninth failed attempt. Nearly all Democrats are holding out for an extension of the expiring Affordable Care Act premium subsidies; Majority Leader John Thune says a “handful” of Democrats could reopen the government today, while Minority Leader Chuck Schumer argues the repeated failures prove Republicans must negotiate on health care. Neither side has moved.

The human cost is mounting: pay has been suspended for roughly 2 million federal employees, about 750,000 of them furloughed, while troops and air-traffic controllers work without pay and the first missed paychecks come due within days. The administration’s separate threat to convert furloughs into permanent layoffs remains tangled in federal court. (Figures are administration and CBO estimates; the reduction-in-force litigation is ongoing.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left (Vox, Slate, Mother Jones, Guardian opinion) frames this as a fight worth having: Democrats are using their one point of leverage to stop a subsidy cliff that would spike premiums for millions, while an administration that flirted with mass firings and withheld pay is the one manufacturing pain. Caving now, they argue, would squander the only moment Republicans feel real pressure. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right (WSJ editorial, National Review, The Dispatch) says Democrats own the lapse: they are blocking a no-strings bill that would fund the government today in order to bolt on an unrelated health-care demand, and federal workers are the hostages. Libertarians (Reason) see a budget process broken by design and a two-party failure to govern. (Directional.)

Sources: CBS News, AP, Reuters, AOL/Wire
Senate Rules · The Nuclear Option

Trump tells Republicans to blow up the filibuster — and his own majority tells him no

+

With the stopgap stuck, President Trump pressed Senate Republicans to detonate the legislative filibuster and pass the bill by simple majority, posting that it was time to play the “TRUMP CARD” and “go for what is called the Nuclear Option — Get rid of the Filibuster, and get rid of it, NOW!” The move would let the party’s 53-seat majority rewrite the chamber’s rules without any Democratic votes.

It ran straight into his own party. Majority Leader John Thune, whose office said his position is “unchanged,” has long called the 60-vote threshold a “bulwark” that protects the minority — and a critical mass of Senate Republicans stayed publicly opposed, a rare on-the-record break with the president. Trump’s counter: Democrats would scrap the rule the instant they held the majority, so Republicans should strike first.

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads the all-caps demand as the quiet part out loud: the supermajority rule is an obstacle to one-party rule and the president wants it gone. The irony they note is that the lone guardrail here is a handful of Republican institutionalists — and even that, they argue, is self-interest, since the filibuster is the GOP’s own favorite tool in the minority. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right (WSJ editorial, National Review, The Dispatch) mostly sides with Thune: killing the filibuster to win one shutdown would hand a future Democratic majority a loaded weapon, and institutional guardrails are conservative by nature. The Bulwark (anti-Trump right) casts the standoff as a healthy limit on the president — an intra-right view, not the party line. Libertarians (Reason) warn against trading durable rules for momentary advantage. (Directional.)

The Supreme Court · October Term

A blockbuster term opens: tariffs, the president’s firing power, and the Fed all on the docket

+

The Supreme Court opened its new term on Monday facing an unusually consequential slate of cases testing presidential power. The marquee dispute is a challenge to Trump’s sweeping “Liberation Day” tariffs, imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act; after the Federal Circuit ruled 7–4 that the levies were unlawful, the justices set argument for the first week of November, with a ruling that could reshape the administration’s economic agenda.

Close behind are cases on the president’s authority to fire the heads of independent agencies — including the removal of an FTC commissioner, a vehicle that could overturn the 1935 precedent Humphrey’s Executor, and a parallel bid to oust Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook — along with disputes over transgender athletes in school sports, Louisiana’s congressional map and the Voting Rights Act, and a First Amendment challenge to a state ban on “conversion therapy.” (Legal analysis drawn in part from Lawfare and the Court’s own calendar.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a term that could ratify an enormous expansion of executive power — letting a president tax through tariffs by decree and fire the officials meant to be independent of him, the Fed included. They warn a conservative supermajority has already shown deference to this White House, and fear the guardrails of the administrative state are what’s actually on trial. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right is split by issue: many cheer a possible reckoning with Humphrey’s Executor and a stronger unitary executive, yet WSJ editors and others are wary of the tariff theory, seeing an end-run around Congress’s power of the purse. Libertarians (Reason) want the tariffs struck down outright as executive overreach, whatever the party in power. (Directional.)

The Middle East · One Year On

A year after the guns fell quiet, Gaza’s ceasefire holds — but the peace plan barely moves

+

Roughly a year after the ceasefire took effect in October 2025, the truce between Israel and Hamas has largely held and the heaviest fighting has stopped — but the broader peace plan it was meant to launch has stalled. Hamas has not disarmed; Israel has made no further withdrawals since its initial pullback and still controls a majority of the territory, reinforcing fortifications along the ceasefire line; and the promised international stabilization force and new Palestinian administration remain largely notional, with few countries publicly committing troops.

Reconstruction has scarcely begun. By recent assessments only a sliver of the rubble has been cleared and most donor pledges have yet to reach the World Bank-administered fund, even as the full rebuild is estimated at roughly $35 billion over the medium term and far more over a decade. The U.S.-backed “Board of Peace” has floated a six-month, roughly $2.45 billion blueprint to jump-start governance and rebuilding — an attempt to keep a fragile plan from unraveling. The Nobel Prize · Physics

Caught in Antarctic ice: Francis Halzen wins the physics Nobel for the neutrinos nobody could see

+

The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Francis Halzen, the Belgian-American physicist at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, “for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin,” the committee announced in Stockholm on Tuesday. Halzen, 82, is honored for turning a wild idea into one of the strangest instruments in science.

That instrument is a cubic kilometre of ice at the South Pole, threaded with thousands of light sensors that catch the faint flashes thrown off when a neutrino — a nearly massless particle that streams through matter almost unimpeded — finally collides with an atom in the ice. Halzen laid out the vision as far back as 1988 and shepherded it to completion; IceCube’s detection of astrophysical neutrinos has opened a genuinely new window on the violent cosmos, from shredded stars to the hearts of distant galaxies.

Sources: NobelPrize.org, CNN, France 24, Reuters
The Nobel Prize · Medicine

Switching neurons on with light: the medicine Nobel honors the inventors of optogenetics

+

A day earlier, the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded jointly to Karl Deisseroth of Stanford, Peter Hegemann of Humboldt University in Berlin, and Georg Nagel of the University of Würzburg, “for their discoveries concerning light-gated ion channels and optogenetics.”

The prize rewards one of the most transformative tools in modern neuroscience. By borrowing light-sensitive proteins from algae and installing them in neurons, researchers can switch specific brain cells on and off with pulses of light — letting them map which circuits drive movement, memory, appetite or fear with a precision drugs and electrodes never allowed. Optogenetics has become a workhorse in thousands of labs and underpins efforts to understand — and perhaps one day treat — disorders of the brain.

Energy · The AI Power Crunch

Powering the machines: a record quarter for batteries, new geothermal, and a first-of-its-kind reactor restart

+

As AI data centers drive the sharpest jump in electricity demand in a generation, the U.S. clean-energy buildout is accelerating to keep pace. New figures put U.S. battery storage installations at a record of roughly 20 GWh in a single quarter, most of it utility-scale — the fastest-growing piece of the grid and the glue that lets solar and wind firm up around the clock. Tech buyers are chasing firmer power too: Google has agreed to take nearly 400 MW from Fervo Energy’s next-generation geothermal project in Utah.

The most striking signal is nuclear’s revival: Holtec has begun loading fuel at Michigan’s Palisades plant, aiming to become the first-ever restart of a shuttered U.S. reactor — a milestone unthinkable a few years ago, now pulled forward by surging, round-the-clock demand from the computing boom. Together the moves sketch an energy transition increasingly shaped by the appetite of the machines it powers. (Partial — project timelines and exact figures continue to be updated.)
