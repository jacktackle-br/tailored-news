Economy · Jobs Report

The U.S. economy unexpectedly sheds 23,000 jobs in July — with 103,000 cut from prior months’ totals — as a stalling labor market hands Trump an economic liability three months before the midterms

+

The Bureau of Labor Statistics released the July employment report on Friday, Aug. 7. Nonfarm payrolls fell by 23,000 — against a consensus forecast of roughly +83,000 — the first outright monthly loss in years, driven by a decline of more than 50,000 government jobs plus weakness in leisure and hospitality (down about 40,000). The unemployment rate ticked down to 4.1% from 4.2%, but largely because people left the labor force: participation slid to 61.4%, a level not seen in over five years. Average hourly earnings were up 3.2% over the year. Compounding the picture, BLS revised May down by 66,000 (to 63,000) and June down by 37,000 (to 20,000) — a combined 103,000 fewer jobs than previously reported.

The report lands amid President Trump’s August tariff increases and the strain the Iran war has put on oil and gas prices. The Federal Reserve held rates at its last meeting with three officials dissenting in favor of a hike; analysts say the weak print may forestall further hikes and revive pressure for cuts. The administration emphasized bright spots — construction and factory payrolls each edged up — while critics note manufacturing has shed jobs on net since January 2025. For the first time since 2010, polling now shows Democrats more trusted than Republicans on the economy.

The left reads the numbers as a self-inflicted squeeze: tariffs plus the Iran war producing higher prices and stalling hiring. 2026 Midterms · GOP Rift

Trump declines to endorse Majority Leader John Thune for another term — “it’s unfortunate that he doesn’t have the votes” — as an intraparty fight over the SAVE Act and the filibuster hardens before the midterms

+

In a Punchbowl News interview that aired Friday, Aug. 7, President Trump refused to say whether he supports Senate Majority Leader John Thune (R-S.D.) for another term as Republican leader — “I don’t want to get into that necessarily” — adding, “It’s unfortunate that he doesn’t have the votes,” a reference to Thune’s position that Republicans lack the votes to pass the Safeguard American Voter Eligibility (SAVE America) Act. Trump reiterated that he “strongly” favors ending the filibuster and wants the SAVE Act “approved immediately,” while giving a full endorsement to Speaker Mike Johnson. Thune responded that the non-endorsement was “probably expected” and that Republicans “have done a lot for” the president.

Trump had pressed Thune to cancel the August recess to force votes on both items — a move that would also have kept several vulnerable GOP incumbents off the campaign trail; the Senate recessed anyway. Trump separately distanced himself from voter frustration, insisting Republican voters are “very angry” at Congress but “not angry at me.” Republicans trail Democrats by roughly 8 points on the generic congressional ballot, and reporting indicates Trump’s own RNC chair has privately warned of a “looming disaster” in November. Roughly a dozen MAGA-aligned senators side with Trump; most of the conference backs Thune.

The left reads the rift as Trump preemptively scapegoating Congress for an agenda and economy voters dislike — pointing to the same generic-ballot deficit and approval slide, and to the RNC chair’s reported alarm, as evidence the president is bracing for midterm losses he can pin on his own leadership. Middle East · The Iran War Endgame

Iran says a Strait of Hormuz deal with Oman is “close” — one that would bar U.S. and Israeli ships and fine violators — but insists the waterway won’t reopen until Washington ends the war, withdraws troops, and pays compensation

+

On Saturday, Aug. 8, Iranian officials said a framework with Oman on navigation through the Strait of Hormuz — the passage for roughly a fifth to a quarter of the world’s seaborne oil — was close, but “not enough on its own” to reopen the waterway. Under the reported terms, outbound vessels would transit a route between Iran and Oman with exit clearance issued via Oman after notifying Tehran, while Iran would gain the right to intervene against inbound traffic; the arrangement would bar American and Israeli vessels and threaten fines of up to 20% of cargo value on violators, with Iran committing not to fire on commercial ships using the approved route. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said reopening is “subject to other conditions,” and Iran’s national-security chief said normal traffic will not resume until the U.S. ends the war, withdraws its forces, and pays war-damage compensation.

The parties remain far apart on money: Iran wants tolls of 5–7% of cargo value, Oman is floating around 3%, and Washington wants zero fees and says it will “never” accept Iran controlling access. The blockade and toll fight have pushed oil above $100 a barrel at points, making this the day’s highest-salience foreign story. Two things cut against the optimism: shipping-industry sources call the proposed passage scheme unworkable in practice, and Washington and Tehran give conflicting accounts of whether direct talks are even happening — Iran’s lead negotiator accused Trump of “theater diplomacy.” Separately on Aug. 8, U.S. CENTCOM commander Adm. Brad Cooper visited Israel and met IDF chief Lt. Gen. Eyal Zamir. (Direct article pages were largely unreachable in-window; facts are cross-confirmed across search summaries of the cited outlets.)

Middle East · Gaza

Trump’s “Board of Peace” says Israel need not withdraw until Hamas fully disarms — even as strikes kill at least 19 Palestinians in a day and mediators call the toll a “flagrant violation of international law”

+

Space · Solar Physics

The sharpest-ever images of the Sun’s surface reveal 20-kilometre plasma whirlpools that may help power tiny “nanoflares” and speed magnetic fields through the solar atmosphere

+

Using the U.S. National Science Foundation’s Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope in Hawaii, researchers from the NSF National Solar Observatory, Germany’s Max Planck Institute for Solar System Research, and the High Altitude Observatory imaged whirlpool-like plasma structures only about 20 km (12 miles) across on the Sun’s surface — features never before resolved. They identified them as Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities: vortices that form where layers of plasma moving at different speeds shear past one another, much as ocean waves curl.

The team argues these vortices may twist magnetic fields and help store the energy released in small eruptions called nanoflares, and could spread magnetic fields through the solar atmosphere faster than current models predict. The work is peer-reviewed, published in Nature (online Aug. 5). The caveat is that the nanoflare and magnetic-transport role is an inference drawn from the imaging, not yet a settled mechanism.

Sources: Phys.org, Sci.News, ScienceDaily, Popular Science

Energy · Electrification

California’s $3,500 “MyFirstEV” instant rebate goes live — applied at the dealership, with automakers matching the state for up to $270 million in combined savings

+

On Aug. 7, Gov. Gavin Newsom announced that the MyFirstEV instant rebate is now available: $3,500 off at the dealership for first-time zero-emission-vehicle buyers, applied at the point of sale rather than as a delayed tax credit. It covers new battery-electric and hydrogen fuel-cell vehicles (model year 2026 or newer) priced under $50,000, plus used EVs under $25,000 at up to $1,750, with no income cap on the base rebate (lower-income buyers can stack larger incentives via Clean Cars 4 All).

The state committed $135.5 million — enough for roughly 73,000 vehicles — with participating automakers matching for a combined total of about $270 million, and framed the program explicitly as a state backstop after the rollback of the federal EV tax credit. Caveats: this is a policy launch, not a market outcome; the 73,000-vehicle figure assumes full uptake, and earlier reporting flagged a manufacturing carve-out favoring in-state assembly that courts could challenge.

Sources: Office of the Governor of California, KQED, Electrek
Science · Biomedicine

The FDA grants accelerated approval to Replimune’s Tudriqev for advanced melanoma — a first-ever approval for the twice-rejected cancer-killing viral therapy

+

On Aug. 6, the FDA granted accelerated approval to Tudriqev (vusolimogene oderparepvec, formerly RP1), an oncolytic viral immunotherapy from Woburn, Mass.-based Replimune, for advanced melanoma — the company’s first approved product since its 2015 founding. The clearance came days after the Aug. 2 target decision date and follows two prior FDA rejections; the drug had recently cleared an advisory committee. An oncolytic virus is engineered to infect and burst tumour cells while rousing the immune system against the cancer.

The caveat is built into the pathway: accelerated approval is contingent on a confirmatory Phase 3 trial, with results expected in 2027, so continued approval is not guaranteed. It was one of two FDA clearances that week, per STAT’s roundup of biotech news.

Sources: STAT News, STAT News (roundup)

American Sport · MLB

Shohei Ohtani’s go-ahead 10th-inning single lifts the Dodgers to a 2–1 win at Arizona, snapping a seven-game losing streak — even after Edwin Díaz blows a second straight save

+

Los Angeles finally halted its skid in extras on Saturday night in Phoenix. With two outs in the 10th, Ohtani grounded a go-ahead infield single to the right side that first baseman Tim Tawa dove to knock down but couldn’t convert, plating the winning run for a 2–1 victory.
