Public Health · Executive Power

Trump signs an executive order slashing the recommended childhood vaccine schedule — cutting the diseases every child should be immunized against from 18 to 11 and reviving the debunked vaccine-autism link, with RFK Jr. at his side

+

In the Oval Office on Monday, Aug. 10, President Trump signed an executive order remaking the federal government’s childhood-vaccine recommendations, with Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. looking on. The order recommends cutting the list of diseases that all children should be vaccinated against from 18 to 11, with the remaining shots recommended only for high-risk groups or left to “shared clinical decision-making” between parents and doctors; it also advises splitting the combined measles-mumps-rubella (MMR) shot into three separate injections. Trump called it the “gold standard” recommendation and claimed spreading out vaccines would “have a huge impact on autism” — a link that extensive scientific study has repeatedly failed to find.

The move follows months of pressure Trump had put on Kennedy for more aggressive action, and came despite warnings from the president’s own advisers and pollsters that touching childhood vaccines is broadly unpopular heading into November’s midterms. It drew immediate pushback from within the GOP: Sen. Bill Cassidy of Louisiana, a physician, wrote that “This executive order is wrong. The President does not have the expertise to make these changes… Vaccines DO NOT cause autism.” The administration has already been blocked in court from unilaterally rewriting the vaccine schedule, and the order is expected to face fresh legal challenges. (Facts from AP/NPR, CNN, NBC and STAT; several source pages were reachable only in summary form in-window.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The left casts the order as junk science elevated to federal policy — a president with no medical training overriding decades of evidence to indulge a conspiracy theory, endangering herd immunity and inviting the return of measles and other preventable diseases. The framing: Kennedy’s anti-vaccine movement has captured public-health machinery, and children will pay for it. (Directional; sourced to news reporting and the scientific consensus, as no clean in-window Mother Jones / Vox / Slate / Guardian column was reachable here.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The right is visibly split. The MAHA-aligned base frames the order as medical freedom and parental choice — skepticism of a one-size-fits-all mandate schedule. But the institutional and medical right recoils: Sen. Bill Cassidy’s flat rejection (“Vaccines DO NOT cause autism”) marks an open intra-right fracture, and Trump’s own political advisers reportedly warned the policy is a midterm liability. A libertarian read (in the spirit of Reason) is ambivalent — wary of federal mandates, but no friendlier to policy built on discredited claims. (Directional; anchored to Cassidy’s on-record statement, as no in-window National Review / Dispatch / WSJ-editorial column surfaced here.)

Justice Department · Confirmations

Todd Blanche is sworn in as attorney general — Trump’s former defense lawyer takes the helm of a Justice Department he has already reshaped, after a 50–49 overnight confirmation with two Republicans defecting

+

Todd Blanche was sworn in as the nation’s attorney general at the White House on Monday, Aug. 10, with Judge Emil Bove administering the oath as Trump looked on. The Senate had confirmed him early Saturday, Aug. 8, in a 50–49 vote after a late-night session ran past 4 a.m. ahead of the recess. Only two Republicans — Sens. Susan Collins of Maine and Lisa Murkowski of Alaska — joined every Democrat in opposition, with Collins citing concern that “the Department of Justice has become increasingly political.” Blanche, who served as Trump’s personal criminal-defense lawyer, had run the department as acting attorney general since April after the removal of Pam Bondi.

He inherits — and has substantially built — a department transformed: reporting cited during the fight noted a mass exodus of career staff, the termination of prosecutors tied to the Jan. 6 cases and the Jack Smith investigations, and more than 1,200 former DOJ employees signing on to oppose his nomination. Blanche has argued that high-profile cases against Trump’s opponents are only a small share of the department’s work and that Article II gives the president authority to direct it. His next tests, per the Washington Post, are the twin pressures of an assertive White House and increasingly skeptical courts.

The Left-Leaning Take

The left sees the capstone of DOJ’s conversion into a presidential instrument: the president’s own defense attorney now runs the department that once prosecuted him, career prosecutors have been purged, and Blanche’s Article-II theory reads as a license to turn law enforcement on Trump’s enemies. The 1,200-signatory revolt is the story. (Directional; drawn from reporting and former-DOJ statements, as no clean in-window liberal opinion column surfaced here.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right frames Blanche as restoring accountability to a department it argues was “weaponized” against Trump — a loyal, competent litigator finally in charge. But the Collins–Murkowski defection is a tell: even parts of the institutional GOP are uneasy about a personal lawyer atop the DOJ, an intra-right worry about norms rather than a defense of the previous department. (Directional; anchored to the two Republican “no” votes, as no in-window National Review / Dispatch / WSJ-editorial column was reachable here.)

Middle East · Gaza Diplomacy

Netanyahu publicly rejects Trump’s 15-point Gaza peace plan — a rare open break with Washington — even as Hamas says it remains committed to the deal

+

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu told his cabinet that “Israel rejects the 15-point document,” publicly repudiating the U.S.-backed framework Trump has been pushing to end the war in Gaza. The plan would require Hamas to disarm and Israel to withdraw in phases, with a pathway toward Palestinian statehood; Netanyahu demanded full disarmament up front before any withdrawal, insisting the military “will not carry out any withdrawal until Hamas is genuinely disarmed,” and vowing that “as long as I am Prime Minister, a Palestinian state will not arise — not in Gaza and not in the West Bank.” The rejection followed more than a week of escalating criticism from Netanyahu and pressure from his far-right coalition partners.

The break is notable because it puts Netanyahu openly at odds with Trump, who had championed the plan and pressed both sides to accept it. In a mirror image of the usual dynamic, a Hamas official said the group “reaffirms its commitment” to the agreement and was ready “to engage seriously and responsibly” in implementing it — leaving the U.S. proposal accepted by the militant group it targets and rejected by America’s closest regional ally. The standoff tests how much leverage Washington is willing to spend on a prime minister whose governing majority depends on rejecting exactly this kind of deal. (Facts from Washington Post, NBC, France 24; some pages reachable only in summary in-window.)

Sources: Washington Post, NBC News, France 24
Middle East · Energy Security

Oil jumps ~5% as the Strait of Hormuz standoff hardens — Trump declares the U.S. Navy alone “controls” the waterway while Iran says it stays closed until the blockade lifts

+

Crude prices surged Monday, Aug. 10, as hopes faded for a near-term U.S.-Iran understanding to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. U.S. West Texas Intermediate futures climbed roughly 5% to close near $82 a barrel. At a White House event, Trump declared that “the only one that has control of the Strait of Hormuz right now is the United States Navy,” describing an “infallible” blockade and saying the Navy had swept the entire waterway for mines. Iran’s foreign-ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei countered that “as long as the U.S. naval blockade continues, the necessary conditions for the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz do not exist.”

The escalation builds directly on this morning’s reporting of Iran’s hardening posture after the summer war. Alongside the rhetoric, Tehran reshuffled its military command — naming a new armed-forces chief of staff, IRGC commander-in-chief and IRGC Navy commander — consolidating a hawkish, security-first leadership as it ties any reopening of Hormuz to demands that Washington lift the blockade, withdraw forces and pay war reparations. With roughly a fifth of the world’s seaborne oil normally transiting the strait, markets are pricing the risk that the closure and the rival “who controls it” claims persist. (Facts from CNN, CNBC and Gulf News wire summaries.)

Sources: CNN, CNBC, Gulf News
Latin America · Colombia

Colombia swings hard right as Abelardo de la Espriella takes office — vowing to crack down on armed groups, slash spending and revive oil, with Milei, Noboa and Kast on hand

+

Abelardo de la Espriella, a right-wing lawyer, was sworn in as Colombia’s president — a decisive rightward turn from outgoing leftist Gustavo Petro. In a break with tradition, the inauguration was held in Cali rather than the capital, Bogotá, in a ceremony that emphasized a hard line on the country’s armed groups. De la Espriella won a runoff on promises to crack down on guerrilla and criminal factions, cut government spending by as much as 40%, and revive Colombia’s oil and gas sector — explicitly rejecting Petro’s strategy of negotiated peace.

The guest list underscored the ideological shift and its regional resonance: Argentina’s Javier Milei, Ecuador’s Daniel Noboa and Chile’s José Antonio Kast attended, alongside Spain’s King Felipe VI and U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche — a signal that the new government arrives with a head start in Washington. The pivot sets up friction over Colombia’s fragile peace process and a warmer alignment with the U.S. and the region’s ascendant right, even as critics warn a militarized approach risks reigniting conflict. (Facts from NPR, Washington Post, Al Jazeera and the Atlantic Council.)

Sources: NPR, Washington Post, Al Jazeera, Atlantic Council

2026 Primaries · Party Direction

Four states vote Tuesday in a run of primaries read as a progressive-vs-establishment showdown for the Democrats — alongside a special GOP scramble to replace the late Lindsey Graham and a fresh test of Trump’s grip on his party

+

On Tuesday, Aug. 11, voters in Minnesota, Wisconsin, Connecticut, Vermont and South Carolina go to the polls in contests widely framed as a proxy fight over the Democratic Party’s direction. The marquee race is Minnesota’s Democratic Senate primary to succeed retiring Sen. Tina Smith, where progressive Lt. Gov. Peggy Flanagan leads centrist Rep. Angie Craig — a recent poll put her up 46% to 32%, roughly double her lead of a month earlier — in a contest that has split the party’s heavyweights. Sen. Bernie Sanders and Reps. Ilhan Omar and Ro Khanna back Flanagan; Reps. Nancy Pelosi and Hakeem Jeffries and former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg back Craig. In Wisconsin, a crowded Democratic field — Francesca Hong, David Crowley, Kelda Roys and Joel Brennan — is vying to succeed retiring Gov. Tony Evers and face Republican Rep. Tom Tiffany.

South Carolina holds a special Republican primary of a different character: Sen. Lindsey Graham died suddenly on July 11 — reportedly from an aortic tear — after 23 years in office and just months after winning his own primary, forcing the state to choose a new GOP nominee. Twelve candidates filed, the state party decertified three, and a runoff is set for Aug. 25 if needed. The backdrop is a Democratic insurgency: last week progressive Abdul El-Sayed won Michigan’s Democratic Senate primary — which NPR called progressives’ “biggest win yet” — though insurgents lost in Missouri and Washington. NPR’s Aug. 9 politics roundup noted Trump “continued to flex his influence” over Republican nominations, even as analysts warned his voters may stay home in a midterm where he is not on the ballot.

On the left the fight is largely internal. Energy & Affordability · 2026 Campaign

The data-center boom becomes a rare bipartisan flashpoint — in Ohio’s governor’s race, every candidate now backs a moratorium as rising electric bills turn AI’s power appetite into a pocketbook issue

+

The AI data-center buildout — and the electricity-bill increases it is driving — has crossed party lines into a defining 2026 pocketbook issue, NPR reported Aug. 8. The clearest case is Ohio’s governor’s race, where the Ohio Capital Journal reported Aug. 10 that all three candidates now favor some form of conditional moratorium on new data centers, each unveiled within about two weeks of one another. Republican Vivek Ramaswamy — who a year earlier had championed the buildout — now says he would sign a Day 1 executive order halting new approvals until the legislature acts, and wants nearby families to get free electricity, full property-tax payments from operators, and strict air-and-water standards. Democrat Amy Acton wants operators to cover 100% of their utility costs, build with union labor, and meet environmental and transparency rules, warning Ohio “cannot trust Vivek Ramaswamy to protect communities from his fellow big-tech billionaires.” Libertarian Don Kissick argues that a project which “cannot succeed without secrecy, eminent domain, taxpayer subsidies, or shifting its infrastructure costs onto Ohio families… shouldn’t be built.”

The convergence is striking because it cuts across the usual lines: a tech-friendly Republican, a public-health Democrat and a libertarian all landing on restriction, driven by voter anger over bills rather than ideology. The issue is surfacing beyond Ohio — Wisconsin gubernatorial candidate Francesca Hong has called for a moratorium too — and it lands the same week as Tuesday’s primaries, hinting that energy affordability could be a sleeper theme of the midterms.

The Left-Leaning Take

Democrats cast it as consumer protection and corporate accountability: households shouldn’t subsidize trillion-dollar tech firms’ power and water, and operators should pay their own way, hire union labor and meet environmental standards — Acton’s “we’re not for sale” pitch. (Directional; affordability framing drawn from candidate statements, as no in-window Mother Jones / Vox / Slate / Guardian column on data-center politics was reachable here.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The populist right (Ramaswamy’s reversal) and the libertarian right (Kissick) arrive from different doors: the former vows to force data centers to lower residents’ bills and pay full freight; the latter objects to the subsidies, eminent domain and cost-shifting the buildout relies on — a market-first critique in the spirit of Reason’s hostility to corporate welfare. (Directional; sourced to candidate statements and reporting, as no in-window National Review / Dispatch / Reason column surfaced here.)

Middle East · Gulf Security

Yemen’s Houthis strike a Saudi Aramco refinery and a Red Sea port — days after Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan signed a NATO-style mutual-defense pact — in the first real test of the new alliance’s deterrence

+

At dawn on Sunday, Aug. 9, Yemen’s Houthis claimed a drone attack on Saudi Aramco’s Jazan refinery — a facility that processes about 400,000 barrels of crude a day — in southwestern Saudi Arabia. Aramco said industrial-security teams extinguished the resulting fire with no injuries. The group framed the strike as retaliation for Saudi drone incursions over Yemen’s Saada and Hajjah governorates, and separately claimed a “large-scale” operation against Saudi-aligned forces in the Red Sea port of Mokha, where a local medical source reported at least 11 killed (wire counts were still firming up).

The strikes are the first significant kinetic test of a new trilateral pact: on Aug. 7 in Mecca, Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan and Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif signed a “Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement” under which an armed attack on any one of the three is treated as an attack on all. It followed nearly a year of talks and came roughly two months after the U.S.-Israel war on Iran; a senior Saudi official had told Reuters the kingdom was bracing for attacks from Iran-backed proxies in Yemen and Iraq. The open question is whether the pact buys deterrence or simply raises the stakes — and whether renewed fire on Aramco assets moves oil markets already strained by the Hormuz standoff. Technology · Semiconductors

Samsung unveils “zHBM,” stacking high-bandwidth memory directly atop AI processors — a bid to break the “memory wall” throttling data centers

+

At the Future of Memory and Storage conference in Santa Clara, Calif. (reported Aug. 4–5), Samsung revealed a 3D-memory roadmap headlined by “zHBM,” which stacks high-bandwidth memory vertically on top of an AI accelerator’s logic die rather than beside it — attacking the “memory wall” that bottlenecks moving data to AI chips. Samsung claims zHBM would offer roughly 8× the performance and about 10× the density of the future HBM5 standard, with markedly lower thermal resistance. It also showed “zNAND-O” and “BV-NAND” wafer-bonding technologies and a 400-layer NAND prototype.

The caveat is that these are roadmap-and-concept reveals, not shipping products — Tom’s Hardware notes most remain concept-stage with no production timeline, and the performance numbers are vendor claims. Still, the announcement signals that competition in AI memory (SK Hynix is pursuing a rival tiered approach) is shifting from raw speed toward how memory is physically integrated with compute. Energy · Grid & Storage

California’s grid battery fleet passes 21,000 megawatts — up more than 2,500% in seven years — as storage displaces natural gas

+

The California Energy Commission announced in early August that battery energy storage connected to the state’s grid has surpassed 21,000 MW (about 21,112 MW), one of the largest battery fleets in the world. That is up more than 2,500% since Gov. Gavin Newsom took office in 2019, when fewer than 700 MW served the grid. Nearly 16,000 MW comes from 310 utility-scale systems inside California, with about 2,000 MW more from facilities in Nevada and Arizona.

The commission paired the milestone with striking substitution figures: comparing the first halves of 2024 and 2026, natural-gas use fell 51% while solar rose 22% and grid battery storage grew about 80%, and clean resources met 100% of demand for at least part of the day on 92% of days in the first half of 2026. The caveats: 21,112 MW is a power rating, not energy or duration, and the substitution figures are the commission’s own. Science · Biology & Environment

Fed a dose of glycerol, mine-water microbes lock 95% of dissolved uranium into a rare, stable form — hinting at a low-cost cleanup for radioactive contamination

+

Researchers at Germany’s Helmholtz-Zentrum Dresden-Rossendorf, with Wismut GmbH and the University of Granada, reported that microbes native to contaminated mine water — when fed glycerol as a food source — removed about 95% of dissolved uranium within 130 days. Much of it was converted into pentavalent uranium, U(V), an unusual oxidation state long thought to exist only fleetingly before shifting to more common forms. The work was published in Nature Communications, with the institutional press wave circulating around Aug. 7.

Because the uranium is locked into a comparatively stable compound, the finding points toward a cheap, biology-driven route to remediate uranium-contaminated water. The caveat is built in: results come from mine-water samples under controlled conditions, and the authors say field-scale viability still needs study. (Peer-reviewed; the underlying work is a few weeks old, resurfaced this week. Pages unreachable in-window; from ScienceDaily, Phys.org and SciTechDaily summaries.)

Science · Chemistry

A catalyst that “sets electrons free” into solution overturns a decades-old rule about which molecules get reduced — and may unlock previously unreachable chemistry

+

Chemists led by Zachary Wickens at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, with collaborators at Colorado State and CU Boulder, built a catalyst that — instead of handing an electron to the molecule best able to stabilize it — ejects a “free” electron directly into the solvent, where it attaches to the first molecule it meets. Wickens called it “the strongest reductant and the most aggressive source of electrons you could possibly have.” The result, roughly five years in the making, overturns a long-standing rule about reaction selectivity.

The paper, “Selectivity Emerges from Indiscriminate Photoreduction,” was published in Nature on July 15, with the press cycle landing around Aug. 7. By reaching molecules that conventional catalysts can’t reduce, the approach could open routes to compounds previously out of reach — though the practical applications are, for now, prospective. (Peer-reviewed. Pages unreachable in-window; from ScienceDaily, EurekAlert and SciTechDaily summaries.)

Sources: ScienceDaily, EurekAlert (UW-Madison), SciTechDaily
