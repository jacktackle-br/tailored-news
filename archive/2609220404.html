White House · United Nations

Trump takes the UN podium today, his standing battered at home and the Iran war unresolved

+

President Trump is set to address the 81st UN General Assembly in New York on Tuesday, Sept. 22, speaking second by tradition after Brazil’s Lula. Aides say he will tout the peace deals he claims credit for, U.S. actions in the Western Hemisphere and his 20-point Gaza plan, and press the case that Iran must never get a nuclear weapon. The speech comes as Trump remains, in his own phrase, in “deciding mode” on the roughly eight-month US–Iran war, with Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian due to speak Wednesday and a Trump–Pezeshkian encounter floated but unconfirmed.

He arrives politically bruised. A new NBC News poll puts his approval at a second-term low around 41% (56% disapprove), with a majority blaming his policies — and the war’s pressure on energy prices — for a shakier economy. After the address, Trump is expected to hold talks with new British Prime Minister Andy Burnham, Japan’s Sanae Takaichi and, more informally, Venezuela’s acting president Delcy Rodríguez, with a Xi Jinping state visit to Washington following on Sept. 24. (Speech themes are drawn from administration previews; bilateral meetings remain provisional until confirmed.)

The directional left sees a diminished president lecturing the world on order while his own war drives up prices and his approval sinks. They want the UN stage used for an Iran off-ramp — Tehran has floated terms through Qatar — not another round of “wipe them out” bravado, and read the peace-deal victory lap as branding papering over a chaotic, isolating foreign policy. (Directional.)

The populist-MAGA and libertarian lane (Reason) never wanted an open-ended war and presses him to close a deal and get out; The Bulwark (anti-Trump right, an intra-right dissent) argues his weakened numbers show the war and economy have caught up with him. 2026 Midterms · Polling

A record share of voters say their midterm vote is a vote against Trump, NBC poll finds

+

A NBC News survey released around Sunday, Sept. 21 found 43% of registered voters view their vote for Congress this fall as a way to signal opposition to President Trump — the highest such figure in NBC’s pre-midterm polling in three decades — against 27% who said their vote would signal support. Trump’s job approval sits at a second-term low of about 41%, with 56% disapproving, and 55% now say his policies have hurt the economy, up from 48% in March. Democrats hold a 5-point edge on the generic congressional ballot, 50%–45%.

The poll surveyed 1,000 registered voters Sept. 11–15 (margin of error ±3.1 points). Corroborating reporting notes the anti-Trump intensity is real but that the Democratic advantage — five points — is far short of the 12-point lead the party enjoyed at the same stage before its 2018 wave. Separately, two Trump-aligned super PACs have reserved more than $150 million in ads, much of it in once-safe red districts that have turned competitive. (A single poll and one point in time; turnout, candidates and events between now and November will move these numbers.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads this as the backlash taking shape: a war spiking prices, an economy voters blame on Trump, and a historic share of the electorate motivated to check him. The scramble by GOP super PACs to defend deep-red seats, they argue, is the tell that the map is tilting and 2026 could be a rebuke. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right cautions against over-reading a single poll a year of politics away, noting the generic-ballot lead is less than half of 2018’s and that midterm intensity is often a mirage by Election Day. The honest read on the right, though, concedes the economy numbers are a genuine warning and that the ad reservations show the party knows the terrain has shifted. (Directional.)

Sources: NBC News, Washington Post, Yahoo News / NBC
Immigration · Detention

AP: ICE is concealing the whereabouts of thousands of detainees facing deportation

+

An Associated Press investigation published Sept. 21 reports that Immigration and Customs Enforcement has been hiding the locations of thousands of detainees who have received final orders of removal — a departure from longstanding practice in which a detainee’s facility was searchable in a public online locator. The change, the AP found, has left lawyers and family members unable to determine where people are being held, complicating last-minute legal challenges in the narrow window before deportation.

The reporting lands amid an intensifying enforcement drive: ICE says it has signed more than 2,500 “287(g)” agreements deputizing state and local officers across 39 states, and the agency has publicized weekend sweeps it describes as targeting violent offenders. Immigrant-rights lawyers say the opacity around final-order detainees is the more consequential shift, making judicial review harder to obtain. (The account is the AP’s; ICE has defended its operations broadly as lawful, and had not detailed a response to the specific findings as of publication.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left calls this due process by disappearance: if the government can hide where it holds people, it can deport them before a lawyer or a judge ever intervenes. The secrecy, they argue, is the point — a system built to move faster than the courts, insulated from the accountability a public locator once forced. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right frames it as removing people who have already exhausted their appeals and been ordered out, and warns that publishing detainee locations can tip off networks and endanger officers. Libertarians and legal conservatives (Reason) are uneasier: opacity that thwarts habeas review and access to counsel is a due-process problem no enforcement goal should override. (Directional.)

Sources: Associated Press, AILA (news clips), ICE (287g program)

Tech · AI & Markets

SoftBank launches an $11 billion junk-bond sale to bankroll its OpenAI bet

+

On Sept. 21, SoftBank Group launched a bond sale of more than $11 billion equivalent — roughly $10 billion in dollar notes across three maturities plus about €1 billion in euro notes — in what would rank among the largest high-yield (“junk”) corporate deals ever, and the biggest non-financial corporate bond sale on record in Asia-Pacific. Proceeds will help fund a $10 billion tranche of Masayoshi Son’s follow-on investment in OpenAI, expected to close around Oct. 1; the notes are due to price Sept. 24 and settle Sept. 29.

The financing is a vivid illustration of how the AI build-out is now reshaping global credit markets: one of OpenAI’s biggest backers is borrowing at scale to keep pace with the company’s enormous capital needs, tying the fortunes of a private AI lab directly to the bond market. Skeptics see echoes of past tech-cycle exuberance in the sheer size of the leverage; bulls counter that demand for AI compute remains real and growing. (A launched offering can be resized or repriced before it settles.)

Sources: Bloomberg, The Japan Times, Reuters
Energy · The Grid

140,000 home batteries push a record 580 MW onto California’s grid in a heat wave

+

Sunrun and Tesla said on Sept. 21 that their coordinated fleet of home batteries dispatched a record 580 megawatts to California’s grid during a Sept. 9 heat wave — the largest residential “virtual power plant” event ever recorded. The three-hour evening dispatch pooled more than 140,000 batteries statewide (about 110,000 of them Tesla Powerwalls, which supplied 517 MW), enough to power every household in Sacramento County at peak. The next evening the fleet added another 140-plus MW at Southern California Edison’s request.

The milestone is a marker for a fast-scaling model: aggregating thousands of behind-the-meter batteries into a dispatchable resource that firms the grid when air-conditioning load spikes, without firing up peaker plants. A Brattle Group analysis cited by the companies estimated such programs could deliver up to $206 million in net savings to Californians by 2028. (Figures come from Sunrun and Tesla; independent grid-operator data typically follows.)

Science · Exoplanets

Astronomers confirm the youngest planet ever found — a Jupiter-size ‘newborn’ still in its birth disc

+

Astronomers have confirmed Elias 2-24 b, a roughly Jupiter-mass planet less than a million years old — the youngest exoplanet on record by a wide margin, shattering a previous mark shared by worlds more than 5 million years old. Lying about 450 light-years away, the planet is still embedded in the swirling disc of gas and dust around its infant star, where it is carving one of the distinctive gaps long seen in such discs. The confirmation drew on data from facilities including ALMA and the Keck observatory.

Catching a planet this early is rare and valuable: it lets researchers watch planet formation essentially in progress, testing competing theories of how giant worlds assemble. A newborn already as massive as Jupiter is itself a puzzle, pressing on models that expect such heft to take longer to build. (Announced in the days before this brief; peer scrutiny and follow-up observations will refine the planet’s mass and age.)

Sources: NASA Science, Space.com, ScienceDaily
