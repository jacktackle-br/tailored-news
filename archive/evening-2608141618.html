Iran War · New Today

Trump says he’ll declare the Strait of Hormuz a U.S. ‘territory,’ and vows he’ll ‘never apologize’ for the war — Tehran says the strait can’t be seized ‘by a tweet’

+

The morning brief covered Washington’s tightening sanctions and the pledge of an “indefinite” blockade; the evening’s new development is rhetorical escalation from the president himself. Speaking Friday, Aug. 14, at the Nassau County Police Academy in Garden City, N.Y. — an appearance ostensibly about U.S. crime — Trump said that “after we finish defeating Iran, which is being very badly defeated, pretty soon I’ll be declaring the Hormuz Strait a territory of the United States.” He offered no legal mechanism; the strait’s waters fall under the shared jurisdiction of Iran and Oman, and the claim drew comparisons to his earlier musings about annexing Greenland and Canada.

Trump separately defended the roughly five-month war without reservation, saying he would “never apologize” for the military action, that he “did the right thing,” and that higher gasoline prices have been a worthwhile cost of preventing an Iranian nuclear weapon. Iran’s representative rejected the territory claim outright, saying the strait “cannot be taken over by a tweet, nor by an aircraft carrier, nor by issuing a decree, nor by an election speech.” The remarks land as commercial traffic through Hormuz sits near multi-month lows, cutting against the administration’s “total control” messaging. (Facts from Al Jazeera, NBC News, The Hill, The Washington Times and CNN.)

+

Americas · Intelligence

A Washington Post investigation ties the mysterious drone strikes on Ecuadorian fishing boats near the Galápagos to a covert CIA program authorized by Trump

+

The Washington Post reported Aug. 13 that a string of unexplained attacks this year on Ecuadorian fishing boats near the Galápagos Islands was carried out under a covert CIA counternarcotics program authorized by President Trump. According to the investigation, a surveillance plane — its ownership hidden behind an LLC — flew from El Salvador and oriented toward each vessel before quadcopter drones dropped munitions or crashed into the boats. Crucially, the Post said it found no evidence that the three boats were carrying drugs. Trade & Economy · Tariff Enforcement

A White House ‘Great Transshipment Scam’ report accuses 40-plus nations — Canada and Mexico included — of helping China dodge tariffs, pegging losses as high as $26 billion

+

On Aug. 13–14 the White House Office of Trade and Manufacturing Policy, led by trade adviser Peter Navarro, released a roughly 25-page report titled “The Great Transshipment Scam,” alleging the U.S. loses about $19 billion to $26 billion a year in tariff revenue (the report cites a wider $10 billion–$100 billion range) to goods — largely Chinese-origin — that are rerouted, relabeled or minimally processed in third countries to secure lower U.S. duty rates. It identifies more than 40 countries at “elevated risk” of serving as hubs in what it calls a “global Shadow Transshipment Network,” naming Mexico and Canada on the U.S. land borders plus the EU, India, Japan, South Korea, Israel and Malaysia, and singling out Canada among China’s “biggest enablers.” It warns that Chinese goods routed through Mexico or Canada can improperly claim USMCA treatment and drop the China-specific duty to zero; separately, U.S. Customs and Border Protection said it is deploying AI tools — analyzing container markings, packaging patterns and X-ray imaging — to flag mismatches between declared and actual cargo.

Canadian trade officials signaled they could walk away from USMCA talks if Trump triggers threatened 50% tariffs. On the analysis, economist Daniel Drezner said Navarro is correct that U.S. tariffs triggered a spike in transshipment — but Navarro was widely mocked for treating tariff arbitrage as a scandal rather than the predictable result of the tariffs themselves. (Facts from BNN Bloomberg, Fortune, The Hill, Global News and Reason.)

Climate · Oceans

How fast we warm — not just how much — may decide whether the Atlantic’s overturning circulation collapses, a new study finds

+

A study published Aug. 13, 2026 in Nature Climate Change by researchers at Utrecht University finds that the rate of global warming is a decisive factor in whether the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC) — the current system that carries tropical heat northward and keeps Western Europe mild — tips into collapse. Using climate-model experiments that ramp CO₂ at different speeds, the team reports that under rapid warming the AMOC can collapse at as little as +2 °C, whereas under slow warming it stayed stable past +5 °C. They identify a critical pace of roughly 0.3 °C per decade — a rate the world is already approaching — arguing the system can “fail to track” a stable state when the forcing changes too quickly. The paper is titled “Failure to track a stable AMOC state under rapid climate change.”

The caveat is inherent to the method: these are model-based results, and real-world AMOC collapse thresholds remain highly uncertain — prior estimates put the temperature threshold near +4 °C with a wide 1.4–8 °C range — so the finding reframes the debate around the speed of warming rather than resolving it. (Facts from Nature Climate Change and Phys.org.)

Sources: Nature Climate Change, Phys.org
Science · Space

JWST spots a ‘black hole star’ at cosmic dawn — a possible key to the early universe’s giant black holes

+

Astronomers report MoM-BH*-1, described as a “black hole star”: a rapidly growing black hole, on the order of about 100,000 solar masses, so thoroughly wrapped in a dense, turbulent envelope of hydrogen gas that it radiates like an enormous star — roughly 100 billion times brighter than any single star can physically shine. It is seen as it was only about 660 million years after the Big Bang. The object was found in the JWST-based “Mirage or Miracle” survey and is presented as a candidate explanation for JWST’s mysterious “little red dots.” The work appeared in Nature (“A gas-enshrouded and gas-reddened black hole at cosmic dawn”), from an international team led by Rohan Naidu.

A note on timing: the paper posted Aug. 12 and drew heavy coverage Aug. 12–13, placing it at the older edge of the 24-hour window — included here as the week’s marquee science result. The caveat is that the interpretation — a “new class” of object, and its link to the little red dots — is a leading hypothesis rather than settled science; how common and how long-lived such gas cocoons are remains open. (Facts from Scientific American and the team’s Nature paper.)

Sources: Scientific American
