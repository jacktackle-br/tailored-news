The Iran War · Reignited

Trump declares his own Iran ceasefire “over” and reopens the war after Strait of Hormuz ship attacks

+

The three-week-old ceasefire collapsed on July 7–8. After Iran struck three commercial vessels transiting the Strait of Hormuz — among them the Qatari-owned LNG tanker Al Rekayat, which suffered an engine-room fire that put it at risk of exploding, and the Saudi-flagged supertanker Wedyan — U.S. Central Command launched retaliatory strikes on more than 80 targets, hitting Iranian air-defense systems, command-and-control networks, coastal radar, anti-ship missiles and more than 60 IRGC small boats in and around the strait. The Trump administration also reimposed sanctions on Iranian oil (effective for new sales from July 7). Speaking at the NATO summit in Ankara, President Trump declared the memorandum of understanding he and Iran’s president had signed on June 17 to be “over” and called further peace talks “a waste of time.” Global oil prices jumped more than 5%.

Iran’s top joint military command vowed a “crushing response” to what it called “US aggression and terrorist action.” The EU’s Kaja Kallas and the International Maritime Organization both urged “maximum restraint.” Domestically the reversal is a squeeze the president cannot easily escape (The Washington Post): Republicans head into the fall midterms tied to a conflict most voters oppose, unable to end it and mostly unwilling to break with the man who restarted it, while Democrats hammer the abandonment of a deal barely a month old.

The Left-Leaning Take

The antiwar left casts this as a reckless, avoidable escalation. Sen. Bernie Sanders led the charge: “After getting the United States into a war based on lies, Trump has now declared the ceasefire with Iran ‘over’ after less than a month,” adding that “restarting his reckless war with Iran won’t make America stronger. It will cost more lives and waste more taxpayer dollars.” Progressives frame the strikes as a president manufacturing a forever-war to distract from a deal he could not deliver.

The Right-Leaning Take

The right is split. The hawkish mainstream (Fox News) treats the strikes as a proportionate defense of freedom of navigation after Iran’s “clear violation of the ceasefire,” arguing Tehran chose escalation by attacking civilian shipping. But the realist/restraint right — The American Conservative, and libertarians at Reason — warns against being dragged back into another open-ended Gulf war; treat that as an intra-right disagreement, not the hawkish consensus. (No National Review, Dispatch or WSJ editorial specifically on the July 7–8 strikes surfaced in the window; none is invented here.)

The Maine Senate Race

Platner still won’t say whether he’ll quit, as Maine Democrats descend into a civil war over his replacement

+

More than 48 hours after signaling he might exit, Graham Platner as of July 8 still had not said whether he will end his campaign against Sen. Susan Collins following the sexual-assault allegations he denies. Under Maine law he must withdraw by Monday, July 13, for the party to substitute a replacement (to be named by late July) — roughly 100 days before the election. A behind-the-scenes scramble is already under way, with Secretary of State Shenna Bellows, former state Senate President Troy Jackson and former Maine CDC director Nirav Shah seen as front-runners, alongside former Senate candidates Dan Kleban and Jordan Wood.

The fight has turned factional. Maine’s Democratic chair has accused Platner’s team of trying to play kingmaker — conditioning his exit on steering the succession toward an ideological ally — while progressive groups warn the establishment against using the opening to purge the left. NBC’s Ryan Nobles reported Platner was recruited by “a more progressive consulting firm that… did not do a very good job of vetting his entire background.” Party officials fear that whichever way they move, an already-exhausted base fractures.

The Left-Leaning Take

Progressives insist the seat must not become the establishment’s chance to bury the populist wing (Common Dreams, The Intercept). Our Revolution’s message to party leaders — “This is not your opening” — captures the demand for a better-vetted working-class candidate “who has actually lived the fight,” not a retreat from the economic-populist message that won the primary by 40 points.

The Right-Leaning Take

Conservatives are enjoying the meltdown as vindication (consistent with earlier Free Beacon and Reason framing): the Sanders wing elevated an unvetted “authentic” candidate on vibes, and Democrats now cannot even organize a clean hand-off in a seat they thought was winnable. The subtext — that progressive vetting prized aesthetics over character — is the same case the right made when the story broke. (No fresh National Review or Dispatch column on the replacement fight surfaced in the window.)

The Courts · Presidential Branding

Appeals court again refuses to put Trump’s name back on the Kennedy Center

+

On July 8 a three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit denied the Trump administration’s request to pause a lower-court order that had stripped the president’s name from the building he had rebranded the “Trump Kennedy Center.” The panel found the administration had failed to show the center would be “irreparably injured,” rejecting its claim that dropping the name would dampen fundraising as unsupported by “any specific facts or evidence.” The suit was brought by Rep. Joyce Beatty (D-Ohio), a Kennedy Center board member; U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper had earlier ruled that “Congress gave the Kennedy Center its name, and only Congress can change it.”

It is the second time the appeals court has declined to intervene, and it lands the naming dispute squarely within a broader second-term pattern of presidential branding — Trump’s name has been affixed to federal buildings, battleships and even the new passport. Beatty said the ruling “again affirms that this administration’s efforts to rename the Kennedy Center were unlawful.” Trump has said he is “washing his hands” of the institution.

The Left-Leaning Take

Liberals read the ruling as a court checking vanity branding and a bid to displace John F. Kennedy’s name from a national memorial that “belongs to the American people” (Beatty); commentary (MSNBC) argues Trump’s real fixation was never the institution but the Kennedy name itself, which is why he lost interest once the courts blocked him.

The Right-Leaning Take

The administration’s position is that renaming a federally chartered institution is a lawful executive prerogative and that the fundraising stakes are real; sympathetic conservatives cast the litigation as a petty Democratic crusade and a district judge’s meddling in a trivial naming fight. (No National Review, Dispatch or WSJ editorial specifically on the July 8 order surfaced in the window; none is invented here.)

Sources: CNN, NPR, Forbes, Washington Times

NATO · The Ankara Summit

Trump upends the Ankara NATO summit — Iran “over,” Spain a “terrible partner” — yet allies reaffirm Article 5

+

The 36th NATO summit — the second hosted by Turkey after Istanbul in 2004 — convened all 32 members in Ankara on July 7–8 under host President Recep Tayyip Erdogan. Secretary General Mark Rutte had set three priorities: higher allied defense investment, more transatlantic defense-industrial production, and continued support for Ukraine. President Trump overshadowed the agenda, using the summit to declare his Iran ceasefire “over,” to single out Spain as a “terrible partner in NATO,” and to press European allies over spending. On the final day he held talks with Ukraine’s Volodymyr Zelenskyy and with Syria’s interim leader, Ahmed al-Sharaa.

Despite the friction, the summit declaration reaffirmed NATO’s “ironclad commitment” to collective defense under Article 5 — “an attack on one is an attack on all” — and Trump ultimately hailed a “very successful” gathering and praised Erdogan for hosting. The subtext, though, was the same strain that has shadowed the alliance all year: Trump’s transactional pressure and public rebukes against a backdrop of allied anxiety over cohesion and American reliability, papered over rather than resolved.

Sources: Al Jazeera, NBC News, Bloomberg, Council on Foreign Relations, NATO
Israel · Gaza

Hamas dissolves its Gaza government, ceding civil rule to a technocratic committee — but keeps its weapons

+

Hamas announced it is dissolving the body that has governed Gaza since it seized control from Fatah in 2007, clearing the way for a new National Committee for the Administration of Gaza (NCAG) — a technocratic committee meant to run civilian affairs under the U.S.-backed ceasefire framework. Spokesman Hazem Qassem said the move was intended to “remove any pretexts for the occupation, which continues its aggression.” Crucially, Hamas is not relinquishing its political or military role; it is only “stepping back from direct civilian government,” and its statement made no mention of disarmament — the key second-phase requirement it has so far refused.

The step is as much symbolic as structural, and it shifts the spotlight of the stalled ceasefire back onto Israel and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, whom President Trump has pressed to advance the plan. Who actually staffs and funds the NCAG, whether Israel accepts it, and above all the unresolved question of Hamas’s arms all remain open — leaving the governance of the war-ravaged territory far from settled.

Sources: Al Jazeera, CNN, Washington Post, Times of Israel, TIME

The Maine Senate Race

Democratic support collapses for Maine nominee Graham Platner after assault allegations; he has until July 13 to quit

+

Graham Platner — the left-populist, Bernie Sanders–backed oyster farmer and combat veteran who won Maine’s June 9 Democratic Senate primary with roughly 72% to take on Republican Sen. Susan Collins — saw his campaign implode this week. A Politico report Monday, July 6, carried an allegation by a former girlfriend, Jenny Racicot, that Platner entered her home uninvited and drunk in late 2021 and forced himself on her after she told him to stop; The Washington Post then reported a second former partner’s allegation that he repeatedly removed condoms without consent during a 2013–2015 relationship. Platner denies the allegations, calling them “troubling, serious and false,” and says he is weighing “the best path forward.”

By July 7–8 the Democratic exodus was near-total: Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, Elizabeth Warren, Ruben Gallego and even early backer Bernie Sanders urged him to step aside; the DSCC said it would spend nothing on the race if he stays the nominee; and Maine Democratic chair Ken Martin said the party should pick someone else. Under Maine law, Platner must withdraw by July 13 for the party to substitute a replacement, who must be named by July 27. Reported possible successors include Secretary of State Shenna Bellows, former state Senate President Troy Jackson and former Maine CDC director Nirav Shah. As of July 8 Platner had not made a final decision, and Maine Democrats accused him of trying to steer the succession toward an ideological ally as a condition of exiting.

Progressives are treating this as a self-inflicted vetting failure, not merely a scandal (Slate). The argument: the left must hold its candidates to a higher standard of character, not lower — earlier red flags were waved away because Platner’s blue-collar, “authentic” persona was mistaken for electability. The lesson is to stop confusing working-class aesthetics with integrity.

The Right-Leaning Take

Conservatives are savoring the collapse as an indictment of media and progressive boosterism (Washington Free Beacon, Fox News): elite tastemakers and the Sanders wing elevated an unvetted candidate for his manly, populist image and are now scrambling to disown him. Reason (libertarian) framed the fallout as emblematic of the Democrats’ “Maine problem.” (No clearly attributable National Review or Dispatch column surfaced in the window.)

National Security · Immigration

Lawsuit alleges the U.S. shared Iranian asylum seekers’ confidential files with Tehran; DHS calls the claim “false”

+

On Tuesday, July 7, the Public Citizen Litigation Group and the Iranian American Legal Defense Fund sued in Washington, D.C., alleging that U.S. immigration agencies illegally shared confidential asylum information about Iranian nationals with the Iranian government. The complaint says that beginning around March 2025 the State Department arranged roughly monthly meetings — using the Iranian Interests Section housed in Pakistan’s embassy as intermediary — in which U.S. officials disclosed sensitive details from asylum and deportation-relief applications, including religious and political beliefs, sexual orientation, family relationships and the reasons applicants feared Iran. Plaintiffs say ICE also periodically mailed or hand-delivered immigration files, and that document-sharing continued even after in-person meetings reportedly stopped following U.S. strikes on Iran. Named defendants include DHS, the State Department, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin; the suit seeks to halt the disclosures and appoint an independent monitor.

Federal asylum-confidentiality regulations dating to the late 1990s bar the government from revealing that someone applied for asylum, particularly to the very government they fled — the legal spine of the case, and the requested court-supervised monitor would be an unusual check on interagency information-sharing. On the facts, the two sides diverge sharply: DHS/ICE flatly denied the core allegation, calling the claim that ICE shared asylum records with Iran “false.” The suit unfolds against the backdrop of, but is distinct from, the ongoing U.S.–Iran military conflict.

The Left-Leaning Take

Left outlets frame this as a moral and legal betrayal that endangers lives (The New Republic, Truthout): handing Tehran the identities, beliefs and orientations of asylum seekers could expose them and their families to persecution or death. Advocates quoted include the National Iranian American Council’s Jamal Abdi (“a betrayal of the asylum process and basic human rights”) and Public Citizen’s Michael Kirkpatrick, who charged the administration “seems more committed to mass deportation than protecting human lives.”

The Right-Leaning Take

Conservative and libertarian outlets largely did not engage the specific allegation within the window; the administration’s position is DHS’s outright denial that any such sharing occurred, casting the suit as unproven advocacy litigation. (No National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason piece on this claim surfaced in the window; none is invented here.)

Immigration · The Courts

A federal judge orders USCIS to restart frozen green-card and work-permit processing, the latest court loss for Trump’s slowdown

+

U.S. District Judge Algenon L. Marbley (S.D. Ohio) issued a preliminary injunction, reported around July 6–7, ordering USCIS and DHS to resume adjudicating applications that had been indefinitely paused for nationals of countries covered by President Trump’s travel restrictions. The case was brought by 25 foreign nationals already in the U.S. — among them a hospital pharmacist, a nurse, a federally funded cancer researcher and a professor — whose adjustment-of-status (I-485), travel-document (I-131) and work-authorization (I-765) applications had stalled. Marbley wrote that the government “fail[ed] to explain how a delay… could ameliorate any public safety or national security concern,” and directed officials to adjudicate the plaintiffs’ pending work-authorization applications within 30 days.

The ruling adds to a broader wave. On July 2 the Fifth Circuit ruled 2–1 that detained immigrants are entitled to bond hearings within 90 days, rejecting the administration’s mandatory-detention practice — one of at least four recent rulings against that policy. DHS said it disagrees and remains “confident in its legal position.”

The Left-Leaning Take

Progressive and immigrant-advocacy voices (American Immigration Council and allied outlets) cast these rulings as courts reasserting due process against an administration using bureaucratic paralysis as a de facto immigration ban — punishing lawful residents, workers and researchers with no security rationale, in the judge’s own words.

The Right-Leaning Take

The administration and its defenders argue the executive holds broad authority over immigration adjudication and detention, and that a district judge second-guessing case-by-case processing priorities — and the Fifth Circuit imposing bond-hearing deadlines — amounts to judicial overreach into core executive functions, echoing DHS’s stated confidence. (No specific NR/Dispatch/WSJ editorial on the Marbley order surfaced in the window.)

Iran · The Gulf

Kuwait and Bahrain hit by Iranian missiles and drones as Gulf Arab states condemn “heinous aggression”

+

Iran launched fresh drone and missile attacks on Kuwait and Bahrain at dawn on July 8, retaliation Tehran tied to renewed U.S. strikes. Kuwait’s Armed Forces (Maj. Gen. The episode widens the Gulf regional dimension of the Iran conflict, distinct from the U.S. strike campaign: the Arab governments that host American bases are increasingly the ones absorbing Iranian fire, straining their own efforts to de-escalate. Kuwait said the attacks undermine regional de-escalation, and Tehran has separately threatened a “complete halt” to talks.

DR Congo · Ebola

Ebola death toll in eastern DRC passes 500 as WHO warns the vaccine-less outbreak is still expanding

+

The Democratic Republic of the Congo’s 17th Ebola outbreak — declared May 15 and caused by the Bundibugyo species — has surpassed 500 deaths. As of July 4, DRC authorities recorded 1,561 confirmed cases, 506 deaths and 254 recoveries, with Ituri province the epicenter (roughly 1,417 cases and 424 deaths across 24 of 36 health zones); neighboring Uganda reported two deaths and about 20 cases. WHO’s DRC representative, Dr. Anne Ancia, cautioned that the outbreak’s “true scale has not yet been fully established,” and specialist reporting (CIDRAP) noted WHO revisions to case counts, so figures remain in flux. More than 10,000 contacts are being monitored, and health workers have threatened to strike, complicating the response.

There is no licensed vaccine or approved therapy for the Bundibugyo strain. A clinical trial launched July 2 is evaluating two candidates — the monoclonal antibody MBP134 and the antiviral remdesivir — offering the first real treatment hope. WHO continues to flag this as one of the deadliest starts to any Ebola outbreak on record.

Sources: NPR, UN News, Al Jazeera, WHO
France

Le Pen vows to run in 2027 despite an appeals-court embezzlement conviction and an electronic-monitor order

+

A Paris appeals court on Tuesday, July 7, upheld the embezzlement conviction of far-right National Rally leader Marine Le Pen, finding she oversaw a years-long scheme misusing European Parliament funds — roughly €2.8 million (about $3.2 million) over more than 11 years to pay party staff with money meant for EU parliamentary assistants — and ordered her to wear an electronic ankle monitor for one year. In a Tuesday-night television interview Le Pen said she will appeal to France’s highest court, the Cour de cassation, which suspends the monitoring order, declaring she will “campaign without an electronic bracelet.”

The ruling is a pivotal moment for French and European politics. Le Pen intends to contest the next presidential election, whose first round is set for April 18, 2027 (runoff May 2). The case keeps alive the question of whether legal jeopardy will ultimately bar France’s leading far-right figure from the ballot, even as she presses ahead with a campaign.

Semiconductors · AI

DeepSeek is designing its own in-house AI inference chip to cut reliance on Nvidia and Huawei

+

On July 7, Reuters reported (citing unnamed sources) that Chinese AI lab DeepSeek is developing a proprietary chip aimed at inference — the stage where a trained model generates responses — rather than at training new models. The move is intended to reduce dependence on Nvidia’s GPUs and on domestic alternatives from Huawei, which DeepSeek has relied on to train and run the models that jolted global markets in early 2025. Per the reporting, the company has been in talks with chip-design, foundry and memory partners for roughly a year and has been quietly hiring chip-design engineers.

The effort remains at an early discussion-and-hiring stage: no foundry partner, prototype or performance benchmark has been disclosed. The report situates DeepSeek alongside other developers seeking more control over their hardware stack — OpenAI recently unveiled a first custom inference chip developed with Broadcom, and Anthropic has been weighing building its own silicon.

Climate Finance

The UN Loss and Damage Fund convenes in Manila to approve its first-ever project grants

+

The ninth meeting of the Board of the Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage (FRLD) opened July 8 at the Asian Development Bank headquarters in Manila and runs through July 10. It is the first meeting at which the board will review and approve actual funding requests — roughly three and a half years after the fund was created at COP27. Four proposals worth a combined $77.4 million are being presented, drawn from an initial $250 million allocation earmarked for country-led projects under the fund’s Barbados Implementation Modalities.

Among them is a Philippine project — “Archipelagic Loss and Damage Responses from Local to National Recovery” — that, if approved, would fund ecosystem restoration, livelihood recovery and community financing in Catanduanes and Sarangani from 2027 to 2032. As the meeting opened, civil-society groups warned that the fund remains severely undercapitalized relative to developing-country needs and pressed wealthy nations to accelerate contributions.

Fusion Energy

Realta Fusion demonstrates the first direct plasma-to-electricity conversion by a commercial fusion company

+

Madison, Wisconsin–based Realta Fusion announced what it calls the first demonstration by a commercial fusion company of direct energy conversion (DEC) — turning plasma kinetic energy straight into electricity rather than via a heat-and-steam turbine cycle. The company first ran its direct energy converter on the Wisconsin HTS Axisymmetric Mirror (WHAM) on June 19; in the demonstration the hardware drew multiple amperes of current at roughly 100 volts, enough to light several incandescent bulbs. DEC has been shown in academic and national-lab settings before, but Realta says no private fusion firm had previously applied it to a fusion plasma.

The milestone (formally announced June 30) kept drawing analysis through July 6–7, including a technical write-up from the American Nuclear Society. It is a proof-of-concept at a very small scale, not grid-relevant output; the company targets its first modular power plants in the mid-2030s.

Materials Science

Machine learning guides the discovery of two new superconductors, hinting at a faster route to thousands more

+

An international consortium led by Professor Päivi Törmä of Aalto University (Finland) reported using machine-learning prescreening combined with targeted quantum calculations to identify two new kagome-lattice superconductors, YRu₃B₂ and LuRu₃B₂, whose superconductivity arises from “flat” electronic bands. The approach lets researchers rapidly screen large numbers of elemental combinations and flag the most promising candidates for detailed analysis — a template, the team argues, for finding many more superconductors.

The work is a product of the SuperC consortium — spanning Aalto, Rice, Princeton, Ruhr University Bochum and the Donostia International Physics Center — which aims to identify a room-temperature superconductor by 2033. The underlying peer-reviewed paper appeared in Physical Review Research on June 17, with an accompanying Aalto release; the story moved through the broader July 7 science-and-tech news cycle.

Sources: Aalto University, Physical Review Research, Phys.org, The AI Insider
