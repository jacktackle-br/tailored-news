Justice Dept. · Protest Prosecutions

As the DOJ charged protesters with assaulting federal agents, juries and judges kept refusing to convict

+

An Associated Press analysis published Friday, Sept. 12, found the Justice Department has failed to secure felony convictions against most of the 102 protesters arrested in four major cities on charges of assaulting federal authorities. All 12 cases that reached trial — most of them in Los Angeles — ended in acquittal, mistrial or dismissal, and fewer than half of those charged with felony or misdemeanor assault were convicted overall.

That is far below the norm for a department known for winning at trial: in 2024, 82% of assault defendants were convicted at trial or by plea, per the Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts. The arrests came during protests last summer and fall in Los Angeles, Chicago, Portland and Washington — cities where President Trump sought to or did deploy the National Guard. The record has raised questions about rushed investigations and aggressive charging by prosecutors under pressure to show a crackdown. (Facts from AP’s analysis and wire reporting; individual case outcomes vary.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads this as prosecutorial overreach exposed: charges brought to project toughness, then collapsing under the scrutiny of ordinary juries and judges. It casts the low conviction rate as vindication of protesters’ rights and evidence that the “law-and-order” crackdown was more spectacle than substance. (Directional; opinion pages not individually reachable this cycle.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream directional right holds that assaulting federal officers must be prosecuted and faults sympathetic urban juries. But an intra-right and libertarian note (Reason, The Dispatch) is uneasy: pressure-driven overcharging that ends in acquittals wastes credibility and is itself a warning about politicized enforcement. (Directional.)

Sources: ABC News / AP, Local10 / AP, KSAT / AP
Republicans · Midterms

One year after Charlie Kirk’s assassination, the GOP turns its Dallas midterm convention into a tribute

+

Sept. 10 marked one year since the assassination of Turning Point USA co-founder Charlie Kirk, killed Sept. 10, 2025, at a Utah university event. At the Republican midterm convention in Dallas (Sept. 10–11), chants of “Charlie, Charlie” filled the hall as President Trump honored him: “Exactly one year ago tomorrow, a far-left extremist assassinated my great friend… because Charlie Kirk spoke the truth.” His widow, Erika Kirk, who now leads TPUSA, attended.

The anniversary lands as Republicans work to energize voters ahead of the midterms. The murder case against Tyler Robinson is heading to trial, with a possible death sentence; Utah District Judge Tony Graf sided with prosecutors who cited “overwhelming” evidence. (Convention events Sept. 10–11, carrying into the weekend.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left is wary of martyrdom politics — grief converted into a turnout engine — and of the year’s toxic aftermath, from neighbor-on-neighbor doxing to a coarsened discourse. The worry is that political violence is being harnessed for mobilization rather than defused. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The directional right sees a movement honoring a free-speech martyr and rallying the base, with the trial framed as overdue accountability. Emphasis falls on legacy, turnout and the stakes of the coming midterms. (Directional.)

Pentagon · Use of Force

The U.S. keeps striking alleged drug boats — the latest killing three — as the legal questions pile up

+

Under SOUTHCOM’s Joint Task Force Western Hemisphere, U.S. forces struck a go-fast vessel in the Caribbean on Sept. 9 (announced Sept. 10), killing three. By wire tallies, at least ~230 people have been killed since the campaign against suspected drug-trafficking vessels began in September 2025 — an escalation that culminated in the Jan. 3 seizure of Venezuela’s Nicolás Maduro.

Legal scholars question the strikes’ footing under domestic and international law — lethal force against suspects far from any battlefield, without charge or trial — and drug experts note Venezuela plays only a minor role in U.S.-bound narcotics. The administration frames the boats as terrorist threats warranting a wartime response. (Latest strike Sept. 9–10; cumulative casualty figures are Pentagon and wire tallies, provisional.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left calls these extrajudicial killings: summary strikes on suspects on shaky legal ground, normalizing military force in what is properly a law-enforcement problem — and stretching executive war powers with little oversight. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream directional right backs a hard line against cartels branded terrorist organizations, deferring to the commander-in-chief to protect Americans from deadly drugs. A libertarian note (Reason) breaks from that, wary of due-process-free killing and open-ended executive war power. (Directional.)

Iran War · Strait of Hormuz

A commercial ship is struck near the Strait of Hormuz, killing one, as attacks on Gulf shipping multiply

+

Early Sunday (around 5 a.m. local, 0130 GMT) an Iranian commercial cargo vessel was struck off Qeshm and Hengam islands near the Strait of Hormuz: one of the roughly 10-member crew was killed and four wounded. Iran’s Qeshm governor blamed a “terrorist enemy,” and authorities had not identified the projectile. A separate vessel was hit by an unknown projectile late Saturday, sparking a fire and a crew evacuation.

The strikes come with Hormuz effectively closed to Western-allied commercial shipping — Lloyd’s List Intelligence reports no Western-allied transits since early May — and an IRGC toll regime charging vessels up to about $2 million. The incident lands a day before Tehran is set to meet regional leaders in Oman to discuss managing traffic through the waterway, part of a war that began when the U.S. launched “major combat operations” against Iran on Feb. 28. (Attribution and casualty details rest on Iranian authorities and wire reports; provisional.)

Sources: Washington Post, ABC News / AP, CNN, Euronews
Diplomacy · BRICS

BRICS closes its New Delhi summit with a consensus declaration — and studied silence on the Iran war

+

The 18th BRICS summit wrapped in New Delhi with the unanimous adoption of a 140-paragraph New Delhi Declaration — a diplomatic win for India as chair. Diplomats bridged sharp UAE–Iran differences over the Gulf war but skipped any direct mention of U.S. strikes on Iran or Iranian attacks on Gulf Arab states. The text condemned the April 2025 Kashmir terror attack “in the strongest terms,” and pushed AI cooperation, local-currency trade and payment systems, energy security and Global South representation — while pointedly not launching a common currency.

On the sidelines, Modi and Xi Jinping committed to a “fair, reasonable and mutually acceptable” resolution of the India–China boundary question and to viewing ties strategically. Leaders present included Putin, Iran’s Pezeshkian, South Africa’s Ramaphosa, Malaysia’s Anwar Ibrahim and the UAE’s Mohamed bin Zayed. (Several outcome details rest on Indian-government and Indian-media accounts.)

Tech · Silicon

Apple’s A20 Pro debuts as the first 2nm phone chip, doubling on-device AI muscle

+

On Sept. 9, Apple introduced the iPhone 18 Pro and 18 Pro Max built around the A20 Pro — billed as the first smartphone chip on a 2-nanometer process. Its redesigned Neural Engine is now a dual 16-core (32 cores total) design delivering roughly 2× the AI compute of last year’s A19 Pro, with support for 8-bit floating-point math, 50% more memory bandwidth, a new 6-core CPU and a 7-core GPU up to 40% faster.

The bet is on on-device AI: doubling the neural compute available to Apple Intelligence so more models run locally on the phone rather than in the cloud — a strategic contrast with rivals leaning on data-center inference. (Specifications are Apple’s own disclosures.)

Sources: Apple Newsroom, MacRumors, The Apple Post
Energy · The Grid

A record year for the U.S. grid: 86 GW of new capacity in 2026, 93% of it clean

+

The U.S. Energy Information Administration forecasts developers will add a record ~86 GW of new utility-scale generating capacity to the grid in 2026, with 93% coming from renewables and storage — about 43.4 GW of solar (51%), 24.3 GW of battery storage (28%) and 11.8 GW of wind (14%). The build-out is powering ahead even after federal EV credits lapsed, as solar-plus-storage economics keep improving.

Globally, battery costs fell a further ~45% in 2025 while deployment grew about 46% to an estimated 250 GWh, per industry reviews — accelerating the shift from “daytime solar” to round-the-clock clean power. Co-located solar-plus-storage drew a record ~$25 billion in the first half of 2026. (EIA forecast plus industry (Ember-style) estimates.)

Science · Planetary Science

Mercury’s crust hints at a hotter, more violent volcanic past than thought

+

A new reanalysis of NASA MESSENGER data finds Mercury’s surface holds only about 37% silicon dioxide by mass — up to 25% lower than earlier estimates. That implies the planet’s ancient lavas welled up from deeper, more extensively melted mantle than assumed, pointing to a surprisingly hot, vigorous volcanic history that laid down lava plains more than a kilometer thick.

Researchers hope BepiColombo, the European–Japanese mission due to enter Mercury orbit in November 2026, will independently test the low-silica finding with its MERTIS infrared instrument at far higher precision. (Widely reported around Sept. 11; a reinterpretation of existing data, not new spacecraft measurements.)

Sources: ScienceDaily, Universe Today, SciTechDaily
Science · Fundamental Physics

If quantum ‘collapse’ is real, time itself may carry a tiny built-in uncertainty

+

Writing in Physical Review Research, an international team led by Nicola Bortolotti argues that if spontaneous-collapse models of quantum mechanics — in which wavefunctions collapse on their own, without any observer — are correct, then time must have a tiny intrinsic uncertainty. That would impose a fundamental ceiling on how precisely any clock could ever run, and hint at a hidden link between quantum mechanics, gravity and spacetime.

The effect is many orders of magnitude below what today’s best atomic clocks can detect, so it has no practical bearing on timekeeping — but it offers a possible experimental handle for testing whether collapse models describe reality. (Reported around Sept. 10; a theoretical result awaiting experimental probes.)

Sources: Physics World, ScienceDaily, Physical Review Research
