History · The Ancient Near East

A new fragment of the world’s oldest peace treaty surfaces in the Hittite capital — and reminds us that diplomacy is nearly as old as war

+

Turkey’s Ministry of Culture and Tourism announced in late September that excavators at Büyükkale, the citadel of the former Hittite capital Ḥattuša in northern Anatolia, had unearthed a new cuneiform fragment of the Treaty of Kadesh — the agreement struck around 1259 BCE between the Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses II and the Hittite king Ḥattušili III after their chariot armies fought to a bloody stalemate. Widely regarded as the oldest surviving peace treaty whose full text we possess, the accord is so foundational to the idea of international law that a replica hangs at the United Nations. The newly found piece is reported to correspond to roughly Articles 12–20 of the known text.

What makes this a “History & Ideas” item rather than a dig dispatch is the perspective it forces. We tend to narrate antiquity as an unbroken sequence of conquests, yet here, more than three millennia ago, two superpowers wrote down mutual non-aggression, extradition and a defensive alliance — and kept copies in two languages, each capital preserving the other’s version. A fresh fragment pulled from the earth is a small thing; the reminder it carries is not, namely that the machinery of negotiated peace is almost as ancient as organized violence, and that treaties, like the empires that sign them, survive mostly as fragments we are lucky to recover. (Details rest on the ministry’s announcement and specialist coverage; scholarly publication and full decipherment typically follow such finds.)

Social Sciences · Political Psychology

Where do our politics come from? A twin-family study finds authoritarianism is largely built at home — while the taste for hierarchy is forged alone

+

A multi-cohort twin-and-family study, published in Behavior Genetics and summarized by PsyPost this week, takes aim at a question usually fought over rather than measured: why do people end up with the political dispositions they do? By comparing identical and fraternal twins and their relatives from adolescence into adulthood, the researchers separate two traits often lumped together — right-wing authoritarianism (RWA), the disposition toward obedience, tradition and conformity, and social-dominance orientation (SDO), the preference for group hierarchy and inequality. The striking result is that the two have different origins. Authoritarian attitudes were shaped substantially by the shared family environment — the household one grows up in — and that influence appeared to grow stronger in adulthood, while dominance preferences were driven far more by the unique, non-shared experiences that make even siblings diverge.

The finding cuts against two tidy stories at once: the one that treats political temperament as simply inherited, and the one that treats it as pure social construction. Instead it suggests our deepest political reflexes are assembled from different materials — one trait absorbed from the air of the family home, the other improvised from a life’s idiosyncratic encounters. For anyone interested in how societies reproduce their divisions, that distinction matters: it implies that the appetite for authority and the appetite for hierarchy may respond to very different interventions, and may travel down the generations by very different routes. (Caveat: twin designs rest on modeling assumptions and estimate variance within a population, not the causes of any individual’s beliefs; self-report measures of RWA and SDO are imperfect proxies.)

Sources: PsyPost, Behavior Genetics, study (open access)
Religion & Ideas · Technology & Belief

Chatbots as oracles: an old human hunger to ask the inscrutable for answers is finding a very modern vessel

+

In an essay for The Conversation (published September 29), scholars of religion argue that the way people increasingly turn to AI chatbots for guidance — on careers, relationships, grief, even meaning — is not as novel as it looks. It revives a very old practice: consulting an oracle. From the Delphic priestess and the Chinese I Ching to Roman augury and the casting of lots, humans have long sought answers from sources deliberately opaque, trusting that something beyond ordinary reasoning speaks through the ambiguity. A system that answers in a confident, disembodied voice, whose inner workings its users cannot see, slots neatly into that ancient role.

The piece’s interest is less technological than anthropological. Oracles, the authors note, “work” partly because their very inscrutability invites the seeker to project, interpret and ultimately decide — the ritual externalizes a choice the person was already circling. That reframes today’s anxieties about people “trusting the machine”: the question is not only whether the model is accurate, but what psychological and spiritual need the consultation is actually serving, and what it means to outsource discernment to a source we are structurally unable to understand. It is a reminder that a technology this new is being poured into a vessel this old. (An interpretive essay in the humanities, not an empirical study; it argues an analogy rather than measuring behavior.)

Sources: The Conversation (Religion), The Conversation (Faith & ideas)
