Congress · The Iran War

The Senate kills a resolution to end the Iran war by a single vote, 49–50, as four Republicans break with Trump

+

The Senate on Thursday voted 49–50 to reject a war-powers resolution that would have directed President Trump to pull U.S. forces out of hostilities with Iran absent explicit congressional authorization. Four Republicans — Susan Collins (Maine), Lisa Murkowski (Alaska), Rand Paul (Kentucky) and Thom Tillis (North Carolina) — crossed to vote for it; one Democrat, John Fetterman (Pennsylvania), voted no. The measure was a concurrent resolution, which never reaches the president’s desk, so the vote was largely symbolic — the latest in a months-long Democratic effort to make the GOP go on record.

The defection is the newest sign of Republican unease over a conflict now in its seventh month. Last week the House passed its own version 220–204 with a record seven Republicans in favor. Much of the discontent is economic: a CBO analysis pins roughly 2.3 points of added inflation on the war, driven by disruption in the Strait of Hormuz, and regular gasoline is up about 52% since fighting began, to roughly $4.48 a gallon. Trump’s approval sat at 32% in late-September polling, a personal low, with the midterms weeks away. (Vote tally and roll call per Senate records and wire coverage; economic figures per CBO and reporting.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left casts the one-vote margin as a moral indictment: a war most Americans oppose, feeding inflation at the pump and grocery store, kept alive because all but four Republicans wouldn’t rein in a president they privately doubt. The symbolic-resolution mechanics, they argue, are cover — a way to duck accountability while gas prices climb into the midterms. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Hawks warn that tying the commander-in-chief’s hands mid-conflict signals weakness to Tehran and endangers troops. But the four GOP defectors — and libertarians like Paul (echoed at Reason) — frame it as constitutional hygiene: Congress, not the president, declares war. Never-Trump conservatives (The Dispatch, an intra-right dissent) read the growing rebellion as a party quietly deciding an unpopular, inflationary war is a midterm liability. (Directional.)

Press Freedom · The White House

+

Judge Timothy Kelly’s overnight ruling forced the administration to restore White House access for CNN, MS NOW and Politico, and the outlets were readmitted at noon Thursday. US–China · Summit

Trump and Xi trade small concessions — fentanyl tariffs halved, soybeans and rare earths back on — but punt on Taiwan and chips

+

The morning’s tarmac welcome gave way Thursday to substance — modest substance. In the bilateral meeting, the two sides converted the two-month trade-truce extension into a handful of tangible trades: U.S. “fentanyl tariffs” on China were halved to 10%; Beijing agreed to resume U.S. soybean imports and to postpone export controls on five rare-earth elements. Trump said the leaders had “made tremendous strides”; Xi framed the visit around “friendship” and cooperation and, per a Chinese readout, said the two powers have “great scope” to work together on AI.

The hard questions stayed unresolved. Xi invoked the “Thucydides Trap” — the notion that a rising and an established power tend toward war — while insisting the risk “can be overcome,” and pressed for a U.S. assurance opposing Taiwan independence that did not materialize; no new deal emerged on advanced semiconductors. Xi also raised Iran, saying he hoped Washington and Tehran would resume negotiations. The day closed with a state dinner studded with tech titans — Bezos, Musk, Cook, Pichai, Huang, Altman, Nadella and Zuckerberg among them — underscoring that the AI race sits at the center of the rivalry. Analysts read the summit as an uneasy truce that exposed U.S. economic dependence more than it delivered American wins.

Russia–Ukraine War

+

Foreign Minister Sergei Lavrov had already said Russia would not halt fighting during talks “in any format.” The picture is one of parallel escalation in the air even as both capitals gesture, unconvincingly, toward a narrow truce. Press Freedom · Federal Courts

A federal judge orders the White House to let CNN, MS NOW and Politico back in, rejecting Trump’s national-security rationale

+

U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly — a Trump appointee — ruled on Thursday, Sept. 24 that the administration must immediately reinstate the White House hard passes it had stripped from CNN, MS NOW and Politico. Kelly found the outlets “did not receive notice and an opportunity to be heard” before losing access, and held that the circumstances were not so “extraordinary” as to justify departing from due-process principles. At Wednesday’s hearing he had pressed the DOJ hard on its claim that the outlets’ reporting jeopardized national security, calling the government’s stated rationale — a vague charge that one outlet violated “standards of professionalism and decorum” — too thin to survive scrutiny.

Kelly noted he had presided over a 2018 version of the same fight, when the first Trump White House pulled CNN’s Jim Acosta after a testy exchange. The administration has already signaled it will appeal a parallel ruling reversing a similar ban on the Associated Press. The dispute lands amid a broader clash between the White House and the press corps over who controls access to the building. (Facts drawn from the ruling and court coverage; legal framing weighted toward courts reporting.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a straightforward First Amendment win: a president punishing outlets for coverage he dislikes, checked by a judge he himself appointed. The “national security” label, they argue, was a pretext to intimidate the press, and the ruling shows the guardrails still hold — for now. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Much of the mainstream right is muted, wary of defending viewpoint-based access bans; libertarians (Reason) welcome any judicial brake on executive overreach against the press. Populist voices counter that a president should control who works inside his own building. The Dispatch and other never-Trump conservatives (an intra-right dissent) frame it as a rule-of-law vindication, not a partisan loss. (Directional.)

US–China · The White House

Trump greets Xi at Joint Base Andrews as Bessent announces a modest two-month trade truce — far short of the deal markets wanted

+

Xi Jinping landed at Joint Base Andrews on Wednesday evening for a three-day state visit — his first to the United States in 11 years and a second summit with President Trump in under six months. In an unusual gesture, Trump greeted the Chinese leader personally, reportedly the first time in more than a decade that a U.S. president has met a foreign head of state at the base. Ahead of Thursday’s White House talks, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced the two sides had agreed to extend their trade truce by two months, through Jan. 10 — noticeably shorter than the six-month-or-longer extension many analysts had expected.

The summit — the second of four leader-level meetings planned for 2026 — is set to touch trade, technology, Taiwan and the Iran war, but few expect a major breakthrough: Xi arrives with China’s export engine humming and little urgency to concede, while Trump is constrained by a costly Iran conflict that has dented his poll numbers and raised prices at home. The short truce reads as a placeholder that keeps tariffs from snapping back before the midterms without settling the underlying fight. Energy · Grid & Storage

Renewables cross a third of global electricity for the first time — and cheap batteries are turning ‘daytime solar’ into ‘anytime solar’

+

Clean power hit a milestone: solar, wind, hydro and other renewables together supplied more than a third of global electricity for the first time in the modern grid era, while coal’s share slipped below a third — also a first. The engine is storage economics. Battery costs fell about 20% in 2024 and a further 45% in 2025, even as deployment jumped 46% to roughly 250 GWh, and new solar is increasingly paired with batteries — the shift analysts describe as moving from “daytime solar” to “anytime solar.”

The U.S. build-out mirrors the trend: forecasts point to a record ~86 GW of new generating capacity in 2026, of which roughly 43 GW is solar, 24 GW battery storage and 12 GW wind — some 93% of new construction. The caveat, echoing the IEA’s Climate Week message, is that soaring electricity demand from AI data centers, EVs and electrified heating means clean additions are increasingly racing to cover new load rather than displace existing fossil generation. (Figures from Ember, BloombergNEF and U.S. EIA outlooks.)

Science · Neuroscience

A textbook rewrite: the human brain appears to be two ancient nervous systems fused into one

+

A Stanford Medicine-led team reports that the human brain develops from two distinct cellular systems — evidence that evolution fused together two ancient nervous systems with different jobs. The forebrain and midbrain, which govern language, reasoning and consciousness, arise from a different progenitor cell than the hindbrain (the brainstem region controlling breathing and heart rate), with the front and back of the brain forming independently during embryonic development. “Evolution took two existing neural systems and pushed them together spatially,” said senior author Dr. Loh.

The same two-origin pattern turned up in chickens, zebrafish and acorn worms — sea-floor creatures that share a distant common ancestor with us — suggesting the split is deep in animal history. Beyond rewriting a chapter of developmental biology, the finding is practical: it let the researchers grow human hindbrain neurons in the lab, opening new ways to study brainstem diseases such as ALS and spinal muscular atrophy. The work was published Sept. 18 in Nature Neuroscience. (Peer-reviewed; mechanism inferred from developmental and comparative data.)

Science · Astronomy

Astronomers confirm the youngest planet ever found — a Jupiter-sized world still being born

+

Researchers have confirmed Elias 2-24 b, the youngest known planet — less than a million years old and still swaddled in the disk of dust and gas around its infant star, about 450 light-years away. Roughly twice the mass of Jupiter, it was pieced together from complementary instruments: ALMA in Chile first spotted a gap carved in the star’s dusty disk, the European Southern Observatory’s Very Large Telescope glimpsed a faint point of light inside that gap, and the Keck telescope’s coronagraph blocked the star’s glare to nail the confirmation.

Catching a giant planet this early — some 5,000 times younger than Earth — is more than a record. Its surprisingly rapid assembly, so far from its star, challenges leading theories of how gas giants form, which generally predict slower growth. The peer-reviewed findings were published Sept. 16 in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, giving theorists a rare real-time laboratory for planet birth.
