National Security · Iran War

Trump’s Iran “endgame” comes under Sunday-show pressure as the strikes stay paused a second night — even while the war jumps to a new front on the Red Sea

+

The pause the morning brief flagged has now held for a second straight night: U.S. Central Command announced no new strikes on Iran into Sunday, July 26, after Friday broke a streak of about 13 consecutive nights of bombing. In a tentative de-escalation of the direct fight, Bahrain, Jordan and Kuwait — the three countries that had absorbed the bulk of Iranian retaliation over the previous two weeks — also reported no incoming Iranian fire overnight. Diplomacy inched forward in parallel: Iranian and Omani deputy foreign ministers met in Tehran and reported “progress” toward an operational mechanism for safe passage through the Strait of Hormuz. Oil came off its peak — Brent briefly topped $102 on Thursday before sliding roughly 4% Friday to below $97 — though still far above pre-war levels.

But the lull in the Gulf was matched by escalation elsewhere (detailed in Global Affairs below): on July 25 Iran-aligned Houthis struck Saudi Aramco oil facilities at Jizan and Yanbu and declared a naval blockade of Saudi Arabia, widening the conflict to the Red Sea. That split screen sharpened the question dominating the Sunday shows — what, exactly, is Trump’s endgame. CNN reported that the CIA and Defense Intelligence Agency have both assessed the current bombing is unlikely to move Iran’s negotiating position, even as Trump kept a “massive attack — bigger than ever before” on the table while saying talks are “pressing forward.” The pause lands against Congress’s earlier, failed war-powers pushback (the House’s 214–208 nonbinding resolution and the Senate’s 47–49 vote), leaving open whether it is internal administration caution — not the legislature — that is actually restraining the campaign.

The Left-Leaning Take

The left reads the pause as confirmation that the strikes were always discretionary — a “war of choice” waged for leverage while U.S. intelligence itself concludes the bombing will not change Tehran’s posture. The recurring argument: if the campaign can simply stop for a weekend, its costs (troops, gas prices, a war spreading to the Red Sea and Caspian) were never worth it, and Trump owes Americans an exit, not another escalation threat. Middle East · Red Sea

The Iran war widens to the Red Sea: Houthis set an Aramco refinery ablaze and declare a blockade of Saudi Arabia — even as the Gulf goes quiet

+

On July 25 the Iran-aligned Houthis opened a new front in the conflict. Military spokesman Yahya Saree said the group fired ballistic missiles and drones at Saudi Aramco facilities in the Red Sea towns of Jizan and Yanbu, setting the Jizan refinery ablaze; at Yanbu — the kingdom’s main crude-export corridor now that Hormuz is constrained — two ballistic missiles were intercepted by a Patriot battery operated in Saudi Arabia by the Greek military under an agreement with Riyadh. The Houthis said the strikes retaliated for Saudi airstrikes on Hodeidah and Kamaran Island, declared a naval blockade of Saudi Arabia, and — via leader Abdul Malik al-Houthi — warned that all Saudi oil facilities are now targets. Brent crude briefly pushed back above $100 before easing.

The significance is geographic. With Iran interdicting shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and the Houthis choking the Bab al-Mandeb, two of the world’s critical oil chokepoints are simultaneously threatened, and Saudi Arabia — a U.S. partner not formally a combatant — is drawn deeper in, with analysts warning Yemen itself risks a renewed full-scale war. The paradox of the moment is stark: the direct U.S.–Iran axis went quiet for a second night and Oman-mediated Hormuz talks reported progress, yet the war is visibly metastasizing outward — to the Red Sea here and to the Caspian, where Iran is protesting a Ukrainian strike on a vessel it says killed one of its sailors. Whether this is a durable off-ramp or a fragile lull remains genuinely ambiguous across desks.

Africa · Sudan’s Civil War

Sudan’s RSF accepts a 90-day humanitarian truce but rejects a U.S.-brokered withdrawal — leaving the war’s core dispute unresolved

+

The paramilitary Rapid Support Forces said they would accept an unconditional three-month humanitarian truce — allowing UN-supervised aid deliveries into Darfur, Kordofan, Ed Damazin and the Nuba Mountains — in response to a U.S. peace plan first presented in June by Washington’s senior adviser Massad Boulos. That plan pairs a 90-day truce with a political process, a comprehensive ceasefire and reconstruction. But the RSF rejected the plan’s proposed gradual, partial withdrawal from positions it holds, particularly in North Darfur and North Kordofan — the very concession the fighting is about.

The gap leaves the diplomacy stalled at the same fault line as before. Sudan’s army (the SAF) has conditioned any broad acceptance on a full RSF withdrawal from the cities it occupies, so a partial truce that freezes the RSF’s territorial gains is close to the opposite of what the government will sign. The humanitarian upside is real — a supervised pause could open aid corridors to famine-stricken areas after three years of war and mass displacement — but without movement on withdrawal, the truce reads less as a breakthrough than as each side trying to look like the party saying yes to Washington.

Elections · Courts

A federal appeals court refuses to revive Trump’s mail-voting order, leaving it blocked across 23 states through the midterms

+

A three-judge panel of the First Circuit Court of Appeals denied, 2–1, the Justice Department’s request to stay a Boston district court’s June injunction against key parts of Trump’s March executive order on mail voting (EO 14399). The ruling — issued July 25 and reported by some outlets Saturday, July 26 — leaves blocked, in the 23 states plus the District of Columbia that sued, the order’s directives that DHS and the Social Security Administration compile a nationwide list of verified citizen voters and that the U.S. Postal Service adopt new mail-ballot handling rules, for any election held on or before November 3, 2026. Judges Gustavo Gelpí and Julie Rikelman (both Biden appointees) formed the majority; Judge Joshua Dunlap (a Trump appointee) concurred in part and dissented in part, arguing the states lacked standing to block DHS from merely providing citizenship data.

The panel rejected DOJ’s core standing argument — that unimplemented rules had caused no harm — reasoning that the order’s “rapidly approaching deadlines” left the plaintiff states “no practical choice but to respond… now.” The district judge’s underlying opinion had held that “the Constitution does not grant the President any specific powers over elections.” As Lawfare’s analysis of the order lays out, Article I, Section 4 assigns the “times, places and manner” of federal elections to the states, with a secondary role for Congress — which has not authorized what the order attempts. DOJ has signaled it may seek emergency relief at the Supreme Court.

The Left-Leaning Take

Voting-rights advocates (the ACLU, Democracy Docket) cast the decision as a needed check on an unconstitutional presidential power grab over elections — and on a fraud narrative they say the evidence does not support — aimed at suppressing mail ballots before a high-stakes midterm. The emphasis: the president has no general authority over how states run their elections, and the courts are enforcing that line where Congress has not acted. (In-window signed columns from Vox or Slate on this specific ruling were not located — the advocacy framing is provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The immediate right-side reaction came mostly from movement outlets (RedState), which framed the panel as “activist” Democratic-appointed judges shielding “mail-in ballot chaos” and cheered a likely Supreme Court appeal, where they see a favorable conservative majority. A dedicated National Review, WSJ editorial-board or Reason piece on this ruling did not surface in-window — the mainstream-right legal view is provisional; a states’-rights federalist could as easily read the ruling as vindicating state control of elections.

Middle East · The West Bank

A deadly clash near Nablus sets off the West Bank’s worst flare-up in months — mass settler reprisals, a sweeping Israeli raid and an opposition “powder keg” warning

+

The occupied West Bank saw its worst violence in months after a July 24 clash near the village of Tal (Tell), southwest of Nablus, in which roughly 20–30 Israeli settlers attacked the village. Accounts of the toll diverge along familiar lines: the Palestinian Health Ministry says Israeli soldiers shot and killed four Palestinians, while the Israeli military reported two Israelis dead, including a settler serving in a local defense squad. The episode carries a governance and an election angle. Opposition Democrats leader Yair Golan, launching his party’s election campaign at a Tel Aviv rally on Saturday, accused Netanyahu and Defense Minister Israel Katz of turning the West Bank into “a powder keg,” saying the blood spilled there is “on the PM’s hands.” Human Rights Watch, in a July 24 report, warned that the surge in settler violence “threatens mass atrocities.”

Climate · Extreme Weather

Wildfires drive more than a quarter-million people from their homes across France and Spain as a heatwave grips Europe

+

Wind-driven wildfires tore across southwestern France and central and eastern Spain through July 25 amid a punishing heatwave and drought, forcing well over a quarter-million people to evacuate. Reported evacuation totals varied as the story developed — CNN and CBS cited “more than 250,000,” ABC News put it above 265,000, and France 24 reported more than 300,000. In France, the largest blaze, in the Gironde region near Bordeaux, had burned roughly 32,700 hectares — about three times the area of Paris — and destroyed around 140 homes, with evacuations across Gironde, Landes, Var and Tarn and President Macron deploying about 1,000 soldiers to support firefighters. In Spain, fires in the Madrid region and Ávila scorched nearly 45,000 hectares, and a 78-year-old man with reduced mobility died near Manises, northwest of Valencia — reported as the outbreak’s first death.

Spain declared a state of emergency and France sought EU assistance, with reinforcement crews arriving from Greece, Italy and Portugal; Spain alone deployed thousands of personnel and dozens of aircraft. Officials warned that shifting winds and high temperatures overnight into July 26 continued to hamper containment. Scientists have repeatedly tied the intensity of Europe’s 2026 fire season to human-caused climate change, which compounds the heat and drought that prime the landscape to burn.

Sources: CNN, France 24, Al Jazeera, CBS News, CNBC
Space · Spaceflight

Starship deploys its first operational Starlink satellites and sticks its softest splashdown yet on Flight 13

+

SpaceX flew the 13th integrated test of its Super Heavy–Starship vehicle on Friday, July 25, from Starbase in Texas, clearing two headline milestones. The upper stage deployed the first batch of next-generation Starlink V3 satellites (reported at 20) from its payload bay — the first time Starship has released functioning operational payloads, a key step toward the far higher-bandwidth V3 constellation. The flight also served as verification toward a future orbital mission, including tests needed for an eventual in-space engine relight. Some earlier accounts characterized the Super Heavy booster’s Gulf splashdown as “hard” — it did not reignite all of its landing-burn engines — while later reporting emphasized the overall softness and success of the sea landings. Big development hurdles still loom before Starship can be considered operational: reaching orbit, catching the booster back at the launch tower, and reusing the new V3-era hardware.
