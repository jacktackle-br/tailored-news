Venezuela Oil · ‘Oil Grab’

Democrats say Trump’s Venezuela oil deal proves the Maduro raid was an ‘oil grab’ all along

+

Two days after President Trump announced what he called “the biggest oil deal in world history” — a framework giving a U.S.-controlled joint venture majority access to more than 65 billion barrels of Venezuelan reserves at “no cost” to taxpayers — the political fight in Washington moved to the January 2026 military raid that captured then-president Nicolás Maduro and brought him to New York on federal drug-trafficking charges. Congressional Democrats argued the two events are linked. Sen. Tim Kaine (D-Va.) called it “corruption at epic scale,” saying Trump “was always after Venezuela’s oil”; Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.) said “this isn’t a win” but “proof Trump put our service members at risk to get Venezuelan oil for his billionaire buddies.”

The administration has released no text of the agreement, and much remains unverified: how U.S. “control” would be exercised, when any reserves could actually be drilled, and whether the arrangement — granted by interim president Delcy Rodríguez — can survive legal challenge in Caracas, where critics across the political spectrum call it constitutionally void. Analysts note real output would take years regardless. (Deal terms are per Trump’s announcement and reporting; the White House has offered few public specifics.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads the sequence as the quiet part said out loud: a president who insisted the Maduro operation was about drugs and democracy has now claimed the country’s oil, vindicating those who called it a resource war dressed up as counter-narcotics. They argue American troops were risked to enrich Trump allies, and that a secret, text-free “deal” announced by social-media post is exactly how self-dealing gets laundered as statecraft. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The directional administration-and-allies framing casts it as a win on every axis: a narco-dictator jailed, hostile influence rolled back, and a strategic resource secured for U.S. buyers without a taxpayer outlay. But there is audible libertarian and restraint-right unease — that an undisclosed, open-ended concession invites cronyism, that “control” of another nation’s oil is neither cheap nor durable, and that regime-change-for-resources is precisely the entanglement the coalition was elected to avoid. Iran War · Polling

Six months in, support for the Iran war sinks to 31% as Trump’s overall approval hits a record-low 33%

+

An NBC News review of recent public polling finds Americans still sour on the U.S.–Israel war on Iran as it passes the six-month mark. In a Reuters/Ipsos poll that closed in late August, just 33% approve of Trump’s job performance — matching the lowest reading in that survey across either of his terms — while support for U.S. military action against Iran fell to 31%, down from 37% in March and 34% earlier in the month. A late-July Quinnipiac poll found 66% disapprove of Trump’s handling of Iran specifically, against 28% who approve.

Notably, the erosion reaches into Trump’s own base: Republican support for the war slipped to about 69% from 77% in March, and 83% of Americans now expect the conflict to drag on “for an extended period.” The numbers land with roughly ten weeks to the midterms and are read across outlets as a structural drag on Republicans defending competitive Senate seats. (Figures are per the cited pollsters and carry standard sampling error.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The left frames the polling as the public catching up to reality: the “no more forever wars” candidate delivered an open-ended one with no exit, depleted arsenals and a contested Strait of Hormuz pushing prices, and voters — including Republicans — are recoiling. They argue the numbers are less a blip than a verdict, and that no convention spectacle can rebrand a war a supermajority never wanted. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The right splits. Restraint-minded and MAGA-skeptical writers treat the collapse in Republican support as proof the war is a self-inflicted wound eating the presidency. Hawkish interventionists counter that polls always sag mid-conflict, that finishing the job against Tehran is worth short-term unpopularity, and that flinching now would be the real strategic and electoral error. (The anti-Trump right’s critique is intra-right disagreement, not the hawkish mainstream position; directional.)

Nepal · Tibet

Nepal’s glacier-flood toll climbs past 900 dead and 4,000 missing, with hundreds of foreign tourists unaccounted for

+

Casualties from the Aug. 26 glacier collapse and flash floods along Nepal’s Trishuli River kept rising through the weekend. Nepal’s disaster authority (NDRRMA) reported at least 903 dead, more than 1,470 injured and about 4,247 missing on the Nepali side, with a further 16 dead in Tibet’s Gyirong County. Satellite imagery indicates a large section of glacier near Langtang Lirung broke away around 5,200 meters and plunged some 1,200 meters onto the valley floor, gathering rock and debris and bursting downstream through the Himalayas; some bodies were carried far into India.

The disaster’s international footprint is stark: as of Aug. 31 roughly 592 foreign nationals are listed among the missing, including some 288 Indians, 90 Americans, 53 Ukrainians, 51 Malaysians, 35 Australians and 33 Britons. Chinese authorities issued fresh flood warnings on Aug. 30 after detecting increased upstream flow, and attention has turned to an unstable barrier lake that could unleash a second surge. Scientists tie the event to Himalayan glacier melt and warn such glacial-lake outburst floods will grow more frequent. (Figures per Nepali and Chinese authorities and still being revised.)

Sources: CNN, Al Jazeera, NPR

Iran · Strait of Hormuz

Tiny Larak Island becomes a Hormuz ‘toll gate’ as Iran is reported to charge tankers $2m for safe passage

+

With the war past six months, reporting this week describes Iran turning Larak Island, at the eastern mouth of the Strait of Hormuz, into a de-facto checkpoint controlling who transits the world’s most important oil chokepoint. Shipowners are said to submit cargo manifests, ownership details and destinations in advance; approved vessels are then escorted through Iranian waters under IRGC supervision. Multiple outlets report Tehran charging some tankers roughly $2 million per passage — paid in cash, cryptocurrency or barter — with tracking data cited as showing dozens of transits through the “Larak corridor” since the spring.

The mechanism converts a battlefield stalemate into leverage and revenue: before the war, roughly a fifth of the world’s seaborne oil and LNG moved through Hormuz, and a controlled, pay-to-pass regime keeps a price premium and closure risk alive. India, Pakistan, Iraq, Malaysia and China are reported to be negotiating de-facto safe-transit terms directly with Tehran. It also sits awkwardly beside Sunday’s U.S. strike on Iranian launchers near Larak, underscoring how contested the waterway remains. (Toll figures are per shipping-intelligence and press reporting, not officially confirmed by Tehran.)

Space · NASA

NASA’s Roman Space Telescope launches successfully, beginning a million-mile journey to probe dark energy

+

The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope lifted off at 7:26 a.m. EDT on Sunday, Aug. 30, aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center, and separated from the rocket about 31 minutes later. NASA confirmed the observatory deployed its solar panels and lower instrument sun shade roughly an hour and a half after launch, clearing the first critical milestones. Roman is now on a three-month, roughly million-mile cruise to its operating orbit near the Sun–Earth L2 point; Trump phoned into the post-launch press conference.

Roman pairs a Hubble-class primary mirror with a field of view about 100 times larger, enabling sweeping, high-resolution surveys. Its core missions are to map cosmic structure and expansion to probe dark energy and dark matter, to run a statistical census of exoplanets — including via gravitational microlensing — and to host a broad guest-observer program. After a commissioning phase, NASA targets Roman’s first public images for early 2027. (Deployment status per NASA; full commissioning is ongoing.)

Energy · Grid

California passes 21,000 MW of grid batteries — and used more solar than gas across the first half of 2026

+

The California Energy Commission says the state has surpassed 21,000 megawatts of battery storage connected to its grid — 21,112 MW, the nation’s largest fleet and a more-than-2,500% increase in about seven and a half years. The storage buildout is reshaping the daily generation mix: utility-scale solar out-generated natural gas on 82% of days in the first five months of 2026, up from 21% across 2024–2025, and for the first time Californians used more solar than gas over the first half of the year. State figures show solar use up about 22% and gas down roughly 51% versus the same months in 2024.

The milestone is a real-world test of the “solar-plus-storage” model, with batteries absorbing midday solar and discharging into the evening peak that gas plants once owned. It lands against a national bottleneck: hundreds of gigawatts of storage nationwide wait years for grid interconnection, and federal policy has swung toward gas and away from offshore wind. (Figures per the California Energy Commission and EIA grid data.)

Science · Cryosphere

Hundreds of hidden ‘iceberg quakes’ found at Antarctica’s Thwaites ‘Doomsday’ Glacier

+

A new analysis in Geophysical Research Letters reports a catalog of 362 largely uncatalogued seismic events at West Antarctica’s Thwaites and Pine Island glaciers between 2010 and 2023, including 245 near the marine edge of Thwaites — the vast, unstable glacier nicknamed the “Doomsday Glacier” for its potential to raise global sea level. Many appear to be glacial earthquakes generated when huge icebergs calve, capsize and collide with the ice front. Because such quakes emit almost no high-frequency energy, standard monitoring had largely missed them; the team recovered them using regional surface waves.

The timing is telling: activity peaked in 2018–2020, the same interval when Thwaites’ ice tongue accelerated toward the ocean, hinting that changing ocean conditions may be helping destabilize the glacier. The work gives scientists a new, sensitive way to watch the ice front in near-real time — a potential early-warning signal for a system whose collapse would carry outsized consequences for coastlines worldwide. (Observational study of 2010–2023 data; causal links remain under study.)

Sources: Scientific American, The Conversation, Geophysical Research Letters, ScienceDaily
