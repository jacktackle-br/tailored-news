The Budget · Shutdown, Day One

The government is shut: funding lapsed at midnight, and the White House says it is moving from furloughs to mass firings

+

Federal funding lapsed at 12:01 a.m. on Oct. 1 after the Senate failed to pass a stopgap, opening the first government shutdown of President Trump’s second term. The impasse is unchanged from the overnight deadline: Republicans, who hold the Senate 53-47, want a “clean” short-term bill funding agencies at current levels into late November; Democrats are withholding the votes needed to reach 60 unless the measure also extends the enhanced Affordable Care Act premium subsidies that expire at year’s end. On day one most front-line functions continue: the TSA (roughly 61,000 of 64,000 staff deemed essential) is still screening passengers and FAA controllers are still working — all without pay for now — while hundreds of thousands of other federal employees are furloughed.

What makes this shutdown different is the administration’s posture. Budget director Russell Vought’s OMB had directed agencies, in a Sept. 24 memo, to prepare not just furloughs but reductions in force — permanent layoffs — in programs whose discretionary funding lapses and that are “not consistent with the president’s priorities.” The White House signalled the RIFs are imminent, with Trump calling the lapse an “unprecedented opportunity” to pare back functions he disfavors; federal unions have signalled legal challenges, arguing a funding gap is not lawful grounds for permanent terminations. The standoff lands five weeks before the Nov. 3 midterms. (Fast-moving; the scale and timing of any actual layoffs were still unfolding as of this writing.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left says the party controlling the White House and both chambers owns this shutdown — and owns letting health-care subsidies lapse while shielding tax cuts tilted to the wealthy. Converting a budget impasse into permanent mass firings, they argue, is lawless coercion: weaponizing workers’ livelihoods to break the minority rather than negotiate. Hold the line on the ACA. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right says Democrats manufactured a shutdown to jam an expensive Obamacare bailout onto an unrelated funding bill — pass the clean stopgap, then argue health policy on its own track (the WSJ editorial line). Libertarians (Reason) note a bloated government could stand real trimming. Anti-Trump conservatives at The Bulwark warn that normalizing shutdown-triggered mass firings sets a precedent the right will regret — an intra-right dissent, not the party line. (Directional.)

Justice Dept. · Courts

Comey moves to toss his indictment, saying the grand jury never voted the charges it was handed

+

Lawyers for former FBI director James Comey have asked a federal judge in the Eastern District of Virginia to dismiss the two-count indictment against him, alleging “flagrant” misconduct in the grand-jury process. Comey was charged Sept. 25 with making a false statement to Congress and obstruction, tied to 2020 Senate testimony — days after Trump publicly pressed Attorney General Pam Bondi to act against his perceived adversaries. The case is being prosecuted by Lindsey Halligan, a former Trump personal attorney installed as interim U.S. attorney; the defense says the grand jury rejected one proposed count and never saw or voted on the final two-count version Halligan signed.

The motion also challenges Halligan’s authority to serve, questioning whether her interim appointment was lawful — a threshold issue that, if the judge agrees, could sink the indictment regardless of its merits. Legal analysts note the dual vulnerability: a procedural defect in how the charges were returned, layered on a dispute over whether the prosecutor who returned them is validly in office. The Justice Department defends both the appointment and the indictment. (An active motion, not a ruling; the prosecution and defense dispute the grand-jury account.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a political prosecution unraveling on contact with the law: an inexperienced loyalist installed to deliver an indictment the president demanded, cutting corners so badly the grand jury was bypassed. It reads the case as retribution dressed as justice, and the grand-jury revelation as proof the process was a formality. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right is split. Some argue Comey should answer for alleged false statements and that procedural fights do not equal innocence. But conservatives at The Dispatch and Reason warn that a botched, apparently retaliatory indictment discredits the rule-of-law case against Comey and hands him a martyr’s exit — prosecute on evidence through career attorneys, or not at all. (Directional.)

Gaza · Middle East

Trump puts a clock on Hamas: ‘three or four days’ to accept the 20-point plan, as eight Arab and Muslim states line up behind it

+

President Trump said Hamas has “three or four days” to accept the 20-point framework he unveiled with Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, warning there is “not much” room to negotiate and that if Hamas refuses he will let Israel “do what they have to do.” The plan calls for an immediate ceasefire; release within 72 hours of the 20 living hostages and the remains of the dead in exchange for hundreds of Palestinian prisoners; no governing role for Hamas; the group’s disarmament; a phased Israeli withdrawal; and an interim administration of Palestinian technocrats overseen by an international “Board of Peace.” Hamas says it is studying the proposal “responsibly,” and noted it was not consulted before the announcement.

The most significant shift is diplomatic: the foreign ministries of Qatar, Egypt, Jordan, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Turkey, Indonesia and Pakistan welcomed the plan and called Trump’s effort sincere, a rare unified Arab-Muslim endorsement that narrows Hamas’s room to say no. The sticking point remains disarmament and sequencing — Hamas resists surrendering weapons or all governance absent a full Israeli withdrawal, while Israel ties withdrawal to disarmament. A “yes” that dodges the weapons question could still stall the deal. (A framework, not a signed agreement; Hamas’s formal answer is expected within days.)

Sources: NBC News, GNA, Gulf News, AAP
Venezuela · The Caribbean

The campaign edges toward land: as the Senate takes a Venezuela briefing, Trump signals strikes may move ashore

+

The U.S. military’s Caribbean campaign against alleged drug-running vessels — at least six strikes since early September, killing roughly 27 people by the administration’s own account — is edging toward Venezuelan soil. Trump has said he is “certainly looking at land” now that the sea is “under control,” and reported a strike on a dockside “implementation area” used to load boats tied to the Tren de Aragua gang. The Senate Foreign Relations Committee took a closed briefing on Venezuela from State Department officials, as lawmakers press for the legal basis and evidence behind the lethal strikes.

The core disputes are unresolved. The administration has not published proof the destroyed boats carried U.S.-bound narcotics; Caracas says many of the dead were fishermen, and legal scholars and members of Congress call the strikes extrajudicial killings absent clear authorization. The U.S. has deployed roughly eight warships and some 4,500 personnel to the region, while President Nicolás Maduro has moved troops to the coast and casts the buildup as a pretext for regime change. Land strikes, if ordered, would cross a threshold no recent U.S. counter-narcotics operation has. (Casualty counts are disputed; land action is signalled, not confirmed.)

Physics · Particle Physics

The LHC closes another hiding place: no sign of microscopic ‘quantum black holes’ up to about 12 TeV

+

Physicists analyzing data from the Large Hadron Collider report no evidence that the machine has been producing microscopic quantum black holes — hypothetical objects predicted by certain theories of extra spatial dimensions, in which gravity would become strong enough to form tiny, instantly evaporating black holes at collider energies. The new analysis, led by a UC Santa Barbara group, finds no such signatures and pushes the exclusion limit up to roughly 12 TeV, sharply narrowing where the exotic physics could still hide.

A null result, but a consequential one: the search tests whether our familiar three dimensions of space are all there are, or whether gravity “leaks” into hidden dimensions that would lower the energy at which quantum-gravity effects appear. Ruling the effect out to higher energies tightens constraints on those models and helps focus where the next generation of experiments should look. (A constraint on theory rather than a detection; limits depend on the specific models examined.)

Sources: Phys.org, ScienceDaily
Technology · Semiconductors

OpenAI goes vertical: a custom ‘XPU’ built with Broadcom heads toward mass production, loosening Nvidia’s grip

+

OpenAI is preparing to mass-produce its own AI accelerator in 2026, a custom chip — internally dubbed an “XPU” — co-developed with Broadcom under a reported $10 billion agreement and fabricated by TSMC. The company plans to deploy the silicon internally to train and run its models rather than sell it, a move aimed at cutting costs and reducing dependence on Nvidia, whose GPUs still dominate AI computing. It puts OpenAI alongside Google, Amazon and Meta, all of which have built in-house chips for their AI workloads.

The vertical push reflects how acute the compute bottleneck has become: controlling the hardware roadmap lets a model developer tune chips to its own software and hedge against GPU scarcity and pricing. It also deepens the industry’s reliance on a thin supply chain — Broadcom for design, TSMC for fabrication — even as it chips at Nvidia’s near-monopoly. Whether custom silicon meaningfully narrows OpenAI’s costs will depend on volume and how the XPU performs against Nvidia’s latest parts. Energy · Grid & Storage

US battery build-out hits a new record: 20.2 GWh added in a single quarter, with 2026 on track for a 20% jump

+

U.S. battery installations set a record in the second quarter of 2026, with 20.2 GWh of new storage coming online, putting the country on track for about 71 GWh for the full year — roughly a 20% increase over 2025. The surge is driven by cheaper cells and an urgent need to firm up the fast-growing supply of solar and wind. Through the first half of the year, utility-scale solar led new projects by count (207 plants) with batteries next (95), and the two technologies together have dominated capacity additions and the interconnection queue.

Storage is increasingly being procured like dispatchable capacity rather than a niche add-on, reshaping how grid operators plan for peak demand — a shift sharpened by surging electricity load from AI data centers. The trend is global, too: EU solar capacity has passed 300 GW, with solar supplying a record share of the bloc’s power. The bottleneck now is less about deploying panels and cells than about building the transmission and interconnection to absorb them. (Figures are from industry trackers and grid data; annual totals are projections.)

Neuroscience · Human Development

Two minds in one: the human brain may have been built by fusing two ancient nervous systems, Stanford researchers find

+

A Stanford-led study reports that the human brain develops from two distinct cellular systems, evidence that evolution may have fused together two ancient nervous systems with very different jobs. Tracing how neural cells arise and organize during development, the researchers find signatures of two separately originating lineages woven into the single organ we carry — a finding that reframes long-standing questions about how complex brains were assembled over evolutionary time.

If it holds up, the dual-origin picture could help explain features of brain architecture and function that a single, unified developmental program struggles to account for, and may offer a new lens on certain neurodevelopmental differences. As with any single study of a deep developmental question, the claim will need replication and will be scrutinized against competing accounts. (A new study; interpretations of developmental lineage are typically refined by follow-up work.)
