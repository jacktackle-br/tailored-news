Justice · The Death Penalty

A judge orders Tennessee to preserve every trace of Christa Pike’s failed execution, as the Army sets a December 3 firing squad for Nidal Hasan

+

In Nashville on Wednesday, Chancellor I’Ashea Myles granted a motion from Christa Pike’s lawyers ordering the state to preserve a broad range of evidence from the September 30 execution attempt that Pike survived. The order covers photographs, video, audio and surveillance footage; the drugs, syringes, IV lines and tubing in the state’s possession; and logs, notes and incident reports. Reports differ slightly on the details: CNN says it also reaches 911 calls and communications among state officials, and the Nashville Banner says it includes Signal and text messages. The judge said the Department of Correction must take an “affirmative step” to “claw back” anything already deleted. The written order is still to be drafted. The state called the order “premature” because it says it is already collecting the material, and a lawyer for the department said there are currently no plans to try again to execute Pike. Her attorneys say she remains hospitalized, can speak only in a limited way, and must consent before any new lawsuit is filed. Lead attorney Luke Ihnen called the hearing “just a first step.”

The Pike case now has a federal counterpart. The Pentagon said this week that President Trump approved Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s recommendation that Nidal Hasan, who killed 13 people at Fort Hood in 2009, be executed by firing squad. Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle has set the execution for December 3 at Fort Hood. It would be the military’s first execution since 1961 and its first by firing squad since World War II. NPR reports that some death-penalty experts suspect the timing is linked to the Tennessee failure. The Pentagon has said nothing beyond its announcement. A challenge to Hasan’s detention in civilian federal court could still delay the execution.

The Left-Leaning Take

Slate has run several pieces this month (exact days unverified). One blames the Supreme Court’s conservative majority for refusing to hear Pike’s challenge to the protocol, and argues that executing her three decades after the crime served no legitimate purpose. In another, Dahlia Lithwick and law professor Corinna Barrett Lain argue that lethal injection has been “sanitized” to hide its violence. A third asks whether lethal injection is any more humane than the firing squad the Pentagon has now chosen for Hasan.

The Right-Leaning Take

No in-window piece from National Review, The Dispatch, Reason or the WSJ editorial page turned up on either case. Republican reaction to Hasan has come from officials: Sen. Ted Cruz called him a radical Islamist terrorist and praised the decision. Conservatives have long criticized the Obama-era Pentagon for classifying the 2009 massacre as “workplace violence,” and they present the execution as overdue justice. (Reaction from officials; no opinion-outlet pieces found.)

The War in Ukraine · Update

Ukraine’s death toll from the birthday barrage climbs to 28 after a missile flattens a Pryluky apartment block

+

The toll from Russia’s overnight attack kept rising through Wednesday. By evening, Ukrainian officials (as reported by Reuters) put the national toll at 28, up from the dozen reported this morning. Most of the deaths were in Pryluky, in the northern Chernihiv region, where a missile hit a five-story apartment block. Reuters cited local officials putting the Pryluky toll at at least 18, including four children. Regional governor Viacheslav Chaus later said 20 had died there, including five children. Officials also reported deaths in Kyiv and other cities. Zelensky said Russia fired about 70 missiles, many of them ballistic, along with dozens of Shahed drones. Ukraine’s air force counted 130 drones and 48 cruise missiles. He said infrastructure in Kyiv and 12 regions was hit, with energy the main target. He called it a birthday “gift” to Putin and “one of the most vile strikes.”

Analysis: Zelensky had warned a day earlier that intelligence showed a massive strike was being prepared, and the Post notes this was an attack “Kyiv saw coming.” Seeing it coming did not stop the losses. The strike fits a pattern seen through early autumn. Russia has been hitting the energy grid along with Kyiv’s bridges, an effort the Post has described as trying to make the capital unlivable by winter, while Western attention is split by the Iran war. Casualty figures are still preliminary and differ between outlets.

Diplomacy · The Atlantic Alliance

From the Pnyx, Rubio tells Europe the West must choose “the unapologetic pursuit of national power”

+

On Wednesday, Secretary of State Marco Rubio spoke at the Pnyx in Athens, the hill below the Acropolis where ancient Athenians held their assemblies. He told European allies the West is at an “inflection point.” “We choose the unapologetic pursuit of national power,” he said, tying that choice to reindustrialization, rebuilding militaries and securing supply chains. He said Western nations had spent years outsourcing “more of our sovereignty” to global bureaucracies, and that “the word nationalism became a dirty word.” He said the West now faces a choice between keeping its place “at the head of the world” or “atrophy, and servitude, and decline.” Prime Minister Kyriakos Mitsotakis then hosted him and called U.S.–Greek ties the strongest they have ever been. The trip is a tour of NATO allies that began in Iceland and continues Thursday in Lisbon.

Analysis: The speech carries the “America First” worldview into a classical setting, and it is aimed at two audiences. For European governments struggling with a costly U.S. war with Iran and the stalemate in Ukraine, it is a demand that they rearm and stop relying on multilateral institutions. Reuters noted that Rubio is a possible 2028 contender, and the speech also works as a statement of his own foreign-policy ideas, close to the tone of his Munich address in February. It is not yet clear whether Mitsotakis or other allies accept his argument about sovereignty.

Sources: Reuters (via US News), Oct 7, CBS News, AP (via PBS), RFE/RL, Times of Israel

The White House · The Iran War at Home

“Let ’em take out Los Angeles”: Trump’s riff on Iran and gas prices draws bipartisan fire, and a White House cleanup

+

At a Monday-night rally in Grand Island, Nebraska, for Sen. Pete Ricketts, President Trump argued that higher gas prices from the Iran war are worth paying. Then he added: “They could take out a city. Let ’em take out Los Angeles. Let ’em take out San Diego. This is a very small price to pay, and it’s going to be over with very soon.” The crowd laughed and cheered, AP reported. Gas prices have risen about 50 cents a gallon since the war began in late February. California leaders responded on Tuesday. Gov. White House communications director Steven Cheung said Trump “was clearly stating U.S. cities would have been threatened if a nuclear Iran hadn’t been stopped.” The White House rapid-response account called critics “buffoons.” PolitiFact and FactCheck.org both read the full passage and concluded that the “small price” referred to gas prices, and that Trump did not mention nuclear weapons. AP wrote that the White House’s insistence he was misunderstood “underscored the ambiguity of his meandering speech,” in which a case that the war protects Americans “came out sounding like an invitation to attack two U.S. cities.”

Chemistry · The Nobel

Mirror molecules: the chemistry Nobel goes to Kagan and Soai for showing how life’s “handedness” can build itself

+

On Wednesday the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Henri B. Kagan of France (Université Paris-Sud, Orsay) and Kenso Soai of Japan (Tokyo University of Science) “for the discovery of non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis.” Many molecules, amino acids among them, come in two mirror-image forms, yet living things use only one. How that “homochirality” could arise on its own has puzzled chemists for more than a century. Kagan is 95.

In 1986 Kagan discovered a “non-linear effect”: a catalyst only slightly enriched in one mirror form could produce a far larger excess of one form in the product than anyone expected. Soai went further. In 1995 he reported an autocatalytic reaction whose product speeds up its own formation, amplifying a tiny initial imbalance until essentially only one form remains. Coverage notes that no reaction outside living things had been shown to do this before, which is why the Soai reaction became central to origin-of-life debates. The committee said the work has been “revolutionary” for chemists making pharmaceuticals, flavors, scents and new materials.

Climate · Diplomacy

With oil near $100, the EU makes energy security its main argument for COP31

+

A draft EU Council text that will set the bloc’s position for next month’s COP31 summit in Antalya, Turkey, makes energy security the core argument for moving faster away from fossil fuels. Bloomberg reported the draft on Tuesday and Euronews on Wednesday. It calls a “just, orderly and equitable” transition toward renewables and efficiency central to “energy security, affordability, and availability for all.” Brent crude is near $100 a barrel with Hormuz shut.

The draft does not propose new headline targets. It asks COP31 to agree a “mitigation package” for 2030: tripling renewables, doubling efficiency gains, faster electrification, and cuts to methane and other non-CO2 gases. It also wants the Mitigation Work Programme extended to at least 2040, and it says there is “no scope” for new climate-finance pledges. The push follows a UN assessment last month that warming will likely overshoot 1.5°C. It comes with the U.S. out of the Paris Agreement and EU emissions up 1% in 2025.

Space · Launch

South Korea’s Nuri rocket puts up a record 15 satellites, and a private company runs the launch for the first time

+

South Korea’s homegrown Nuri rocket flew for the fifth time at 12:25 p.m. local time Wednesday (Tuesday night in the U.S.) from the Naro Space Center in Goheung. It carried a record 15 satellites. All five NEONSAT microsatellites, the start of the country’s first domestically launched constellation, separated normally, the Korea AeroSpace Administration said. Nine of ten CubeSats deployed; one failed to eject.

This was also the first launch run by a private company. Hanwha Aerospace, which builds Nuri, held full operational command, staffing 20 of the 22 launch-control consoles while government agencies supervised. When complete at 10 satellites, NEONSAT is meant to image the Korean Peninsula more than three times a day, for security and disaster response.
