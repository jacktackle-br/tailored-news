National Security · War Powers

As the Iran war widens, Washington’s fight over who authorized it — and how the dead are being counted — sharpens

+

Thursday’s renewed U.S. strikes on Iran and the war’s spread into Egyptian waters (see Foreign Affairs for the military and diplomatic picture) pushed the domestic fight over the campaign’s legality and cost back to the surface. The war, ongoing since February 2026 and now in its fifth month, has never been authorized by Congress; the administration asserts Article II authority. On July 23 the House passed a war-powers resolution rebuking the campaign, with four Republicans — Reps. Tom Barrett (Mich.), Brian Fitzpatrick (Pa.), Thomas Massie (Ky.) and Warren Davidson (Ohio) — joining Democrats; a Senate companion measure fell short. Neither would bind the president. Trump, briefed Wednesday, called the latest tanker incident “a little more of the same” and has said he is weighing a “massive attack… bigger than ever before.”

A parallel controversy is over the numbers. Reporting says the Pentagon has reclassified some regional service-member deaths as “overseas operations” rather than war fatalities, a move critics dispute; U.S. military deaths since February have been reported at roughly 17–18, with 400-plus wounded, and total deaths reported anywhere from about 1,700 Iranians to 3,400-plus overall — figures that diverge sharply by outlet and should be treated as contested. Public support is low: a Reuters/Ipsos poll found only about one in three Americans back the war, and a Fox News poll put disapproval of Trump’s handling at a record 66%.

The Left-Leaning Take

The left frames this as an aimless, escalating war Trump started and cannot exit — and one whose true cost the administration is hiding. Slate describes a conflict that keeps “spiraling,” with Americans killed and Iranians dying in large numbers while peace prospects “remain dim.” The Boston Globe opinion page argues the Pentagon is “cooking the books on the Iran war dead” by reclassifying casualties, and the Philadelphia Inquirer editorial board calls the strategy a failure and urges a diplomatic off-ramp. The through-line: no clear goals, mounting deaths, and an administration massaging the count.

The Right-Leaning Take

The right is visibly split. The hawkish critique comes from the Wall Street Journal editorial board, reportedly furious that officials leaked that the U.S. is “running out of ammunition” — projecting weakness and inviting Iran to “outlast a president who no longer wants the fight” (a characterization Trump disputes). The restraint wing pushes the opposite way: Reason and Reps. Massie and Davidson press the constitutional war-powers objection, insisting Congress, not the president, must authorize war. So the intra-right fight is hawks who fault Trump for dithering against constitutionalist non-interventionists who want the war reined in.

Justice Department · Confirmations

Trump threatens to withdraw Blanche’s AG nomination until his GOP holdouts leave office as they demand the IRS fund be killed in writing

+

The Senate Judiciary Committee has postponed its vote to advance Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche’s nomination to be attorney general; Chairman Chuck Grassley canceled the markup after negotiations failed to win over two Republicans. Sens. John Cornyn (R-Texas) and Thom Tillis (R-N.C.) are withholding support, demanding a written guarantee that the Justice Department will not revive the “anti-weaponization fund” — a roughly $1.78 billion pool created in the settlement of Trump’s $10 billion lawsuit against the IRS over the leak of his tax data, which critics warn could pay Trump allies. Cornyn also wants written confirmation that an IRS pledge not to audit Trump, his family and affiliated businesses has been narrowed. Blanche has told Congress the fund is “not going forward, period,” but Cornyn says the settlement remains on paper and that DOJ “simply refused to cooperate.”

Trump escalated the standoff Thursday, July 30, saying he might withdraw Blanche’s nomination until after Tillis and Cornyn leave office — both are effectively lame ducks, Cornyn having lost his primary to Ken Paxton and Tillis retiring, which several outlets note frees them to defy the president. Blanche, Trump’s former personal defense lawyer, met with both senators Thursday afternoon; the outcome was unclear. Earlier lawsuits in D.C. and Virginia federal courts had the fund paused, with a judge seeking assurances it is permanently dead.

The Left-Leaning Take

Left commentary treats the fund as a corrupt “slush fund” and casts the two senators as unlikely, cut-rate heroes. Slate calls the IRS settlement “extremely shady,” a mechanism for Trump “to funnel taxpayer cash to his friends” — possibly including Jan. 6 defendants — and notes pointedly that Cornyn and Tillis will defy Trump only now that he has ended their careers and they have “no careers to lose.” MSNBC-style analysis argues Blanche has kept “hedging” on the fund in his written answers rather than kill it outright.

The Right-Leaning Take

The right is divided pro-Trump vs. institutionalist. MAGA-aligned outlets frame Cornyn and Tillis as holding the nominee “hostage” and defying the president. Against them, Bloomberg Opinion argued the confirmation battle with the two “is worth fighting,” and National Review’s Bench Memos engaged the legal question of whether the settlement can be resurrected (full text inaccessible — partial). On the anti-Trump right (label: intra-right disagreement, not the mainstream-right position), The Bulwark praised the two senators for showing “spine” against an indefensible payout scheme.

Trade · Tariffs

Trump grants Mexico a 90-day tariff reprieve as the pharma-tariff deadline arrives with no immediate 100% hit

+

On Thursday, July 30, President Trump announced a 90-day extension of the tariff deadline for Mexico, a day before an August 1 deadline to impose 30% duties, citing a “very successful” call with President Claudia Sheinbaum. Existing levies stay in place during talks: 25% on Mexican goods tied to fentanyl, 25% on Mexican-made cars, and 50% on aluminum, copper and steel. Separately, Friday, July 31 was Trump’s deadline for large drugmakers to reach onshoring deals to avoid a threatened 100% tariff on imported patented drugs.

In practice the first round of 100% pharma tariffs did not land Friday: 17 companies reached most-favored-nation/onshoring deals cutting the rate (reported at roughly 15–20%, with EU branded pharma capped near 15%), and the broader tariffs are set to take effect September 29, 2026. For context, the Tax Foundation estimates Trump’s 2026 tariffs amount to an average tax increase of about $900 per U.S. household this year, and FactCheck.org’s July update noted job growth slowed and manufacturing employment kept declining despite the tariffs.

Middle East · The Iran War

“It’s our turn”: the U.S. unleashes a “heavy wave” of strikes on Iran as the war jumps to Egypt’s coast and oil heads for its biggest monthly jump since March

+

After a brief late-July lull, the roughly five-month U.S.–Iran war flared violently on July 29–30. U.S. Central Command said it carried out a two-hour “heavy wave” of strikes from midnight, hitting dozens of Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps targets — command centers, missile and drone facilities, and coastal surveillance sites — with explosions reported across southern Iran including Bandar Abbas and the islands of Kish and Qeshm. Iranian state media said a U.S. strike on Qeshm Island killed a couple and their two-year-old child and killed three IRGC members in Zanjan; Reuters said it could not verify the claims. The IRGC said it retaliated against the Azraq air base in Jordan and bases in Kuwait and Bahrain and claimed to have destroyed U.S. aircraft; CENTCOM rejected that, saying all incoming fire was intercepted or failed to reach its targets and no aircraft were damaged. Trump said it was “our turn to hit them” and later suggested the Iranian shot came from a different faction than the officials Washington had been talking to.

The conflict visibly widened geographically. An American-owned gas vessel (the FSRU Energos Winter) and the LNG tanker GasLog Salem caught fire after a drone strike at Egypt’s Mediterranean port of Damietta near the Suez Canal, killing at least one worker; Egypt confirmed the strike, warned of “escalation,” and said no group had claimed it (Iran denied involvement, and attribution remains unconfirmed). Saudi Arabia separately reported drones launched from Iraq at critical infrastructure. Markets reflected the risk: Brent settled near $89, with oil on track for its biggest monthly gain (about 21%) since March and analysts openly discussing a $200 scenario if the Strait of Hormuz is choked. Casualty totals are contested across outlets, and the campaign is one Congress never authorized.

Europe · Russia’s War on Ukraine

A Russian cruise missile detonates inside Poland during a mass barrage on Ukraine — but Warsaw holds off on Article 4

+

During one of Russia’s largest overnight assaults on Ukraine in weeks (night of July 29–30), a Russian missile crossed into NATO territory and came down in a field near Tarnawa-Kolonia in Poland’s Lublin province, roughly 100 km from the border. Residents reported an explosion around 4 a.m. that left a crater about 10 meters wide with no casualties. Polish PM Donald Tusk said recovered fragments point to a Russian Kh-101 cruise missile but that “there is no reason to think the target was Poland,” noting Ukrainian pilots had tried to intercept missiles nearing the border. NATO scrambled jets, an attack helicopter and other aircraft and activated ground defenses, saying it would “take all necessary measures to defend NATO territory.” The wider barrage killed at least eight to nine civilians across Ukraine (reports vary), including children, and injured more than 50, with damage in Kyiv and Lviv; July 31 was declared a day of mourning in Kryvyi Rih.

Unlike the September 2025 drone incursion — when Poland invoked NATO’s Article 4 to trigger formal consultations — Warsaw this time convened an emergency coordination group and consulted allies but pointedly held off on Article 4 pending the investigation, a deliberate move to avoid an automatic escalatory ladder while the “accident vs. deliberate” question is unresolved. The practical effect is still that a Russian weapon detonated on NATO soil for at least the second time in under a year, steadily eroding the firebreak between the Ukraine war and a direct NATO–Russia confrontation.

Middle East · Gaza Ceasefire

Cairo talks show “rare progress” toward a Hamas-disarmament roadmap — even as Israeli strikes keep killing

+

Negotiations in Cairo on implementing the second phase of the U.S.-brokered Gaza ceasefire (in effect since October 10, 2025) saw what sources called “rare progress” on July 30. A Hamas delegation that arrived Tuesday met Egyptian, Qatari and Turkish mediators; a Hamas official and a diplomat close to the talks called them “positive.” The reported focus is a roadmap that would decommission all heavy and light weapons in Gaza, alongside a surge in humanitarian aid, governance by a civilian Palestinian administration, an Israeli withdrawal and an international stabilization force. Israel is pressing for Hamas to cede power entirely and fully disarm; Hamas reportedly brought a new proposal on its “military infrastructure.”

The optimism is heavily qualified. The truce has halted full-scale war but not near-daily Israeli strikes: on July 30, strikes killed at least four to six Palestinians, including children, across the Strip (the toll was revised upward through the day), and reporting cites more than 1,200 Palestinians killed since the ceasefire took hold. Disarmament has been the central obstacle to phase two from the start, so movement on a decommissioning roadmap — if it survives contact with Israel’s conditions — is the most substantive in months.

Technology · Semiconductors

TSMC starts building an “EMIB-like” chip-packaging technology — and the AI-packaging race sends Intel up 12%

+

On Thursday, July 30, reports surfaced that TSMC is developing a new advanced packaging method — a silicon-bridge approach resembling Intel’s Embedded Multi-die Interconnect Bridge (EMIB) — in partnership with Taiwan-based substrate maker Kinsus Interconnect Technology. It is a response to TSMC’s flagship CoWoS-L packaging being sold out through 2026 and well into 2027, with lead times reportedly stretching toward 78 weeks. The silicon-bridge design is meant to support larger multi-die AI chips at lower cost while adding badly needed capacity and supplier diversification. The project is early-stage, with no commercial launch timeline.

The signal matters because advanced packaging — how many chiplets you can stitch into one giant AI processor — has become as much a bottleneck as the transistors themselves. Intel’s EMIB has drawn interest from Google (TPUs), Amazon (Trainium 3) and reportedly Nvidia (evaluating it for a future multi-GPU part); TSMC building its own EMIB-style bridge reads as a tacit concession that Intel’s approach is a potent contender. The report moved markets: Intel shares jumped roughly 12–13% and TSMC gained about 7% on July 30.

Technology · AI & Industrial Policy

Europe opens the tender for up to seven “AI gigafactories” — a €30 billion bet to close the compute gap

+

On July 30 the European Commission formally launched its call for proposals to publicly co-finance up to seven “AI gigafactories” — large-scale AI compute-and-datacenter hubs meant to close Europe’s computing gap with the U.S. and China. The initiative is backed by up to €10 billion in EU and national funding and is expected to unlock at least €20 billion in private investment, for roughly €30 billion total. The call is split into two lots: an initial lot funding up to four projects (up to €100M each in phase one, up to €400M in phase two) and a larger lot (up to €200M initially, up to €800M later).

The application deadline is November 12, 2026; the Commission says 76 potential consortia have expressed preliminary interest. Selected projects would be chosen in early 2027 and begin operating within roughly 18 months. The move is the compute-infrastructure companion to Europe’s broader push to build sovereign AI capacity rather than rent it from American and Chinese hyperscalers.

Sources: Euronews, EUnews, European Commission
Energy · Electrification

Tesla builds its 10 millionth vehicle — a milestone shadowed by a stalling growth curve

+

On July 30, Tesla’s 10 millionth EV — a Diamond Black Model Y — rolled off the line at the Fremont factory, making Tesla the first pure-play EV maker to reach eight figures of cumulative production. The climb from the 2006 Roadster prototype took 20 years, but the jump from 1 million (2020) to 10 million took just about six, spanning Fremont plus Gigafactories in Shanghai, Berlin and Texas — a compression that captures how fast EV manufacturing scaled last decade.

The milestone lands amid a growth plateau. Tesla delivered about 830,000 vehicles in the first half of 2026 (Q2 above 480,000, a quarterly record), but U.S. sales fell roughly 13% year-over-year in Q2, pushing the company toward newer markets such as Japan and Australia. Coverage framed the number as a genuine manufacturing achievement set against clearly slowing EV demand growth — the story of an industry maturing out of its hypergrowth phase.

Sources: Electrek, TechCrunch, InsideEVs
Science · Astronomy

The first ALMA SETI search opens a barely-explored radio band — and finds we’ve effectively sampled 21× more stars than anyone counted

+

University of Manchester doctoral researcher Louisa Mason led the first SETI survey using the ALMA array in Chile, mining archival “Band 3” data in two narrow windows around 90.642 and 93.151 GHz — millimeter/submillimeter frequencies Mason says “remain almost completely unexplored for SETI.” No alien signals turned up, but the work shows existing astronomy archives can be repurposed for technosignature searches rather than requiring dedicated observing time. A companion modeling paper found that, counting incidental background stars (“stellar bycatch”), past surveys may have effectively covered more than 6 million stars — versus the roughly 288,000 previously tallied.

The two papers appear in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society; results were presented at the RAS National Astronomy Meeting (NAM 2026) in Birmingham. (Dateline note: the work was presented and covered around July 26 rather than strictly in the past 24 hours, but it remained the freshest and most substantive pure-science item in the window.)
