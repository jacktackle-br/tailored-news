Trump · Ireland

Trump lands in Ireland for talks with its leaders — and a stop at his own golf resort

+

President Trump touched down in Dublin on Saturday, Sept. 12, greeted at the airport by Tánaiste and Finance Minister Simon Harris before flying by helicopter to Phoenix Park to meet Ireland’s president, Catherine Connolly, at her official residence, Áras an Uachtaráin, and Taoiseach Micheál Martin. He was due to address U.S. and Irish business leaders at the American ambassador’s residence, then travel on to his oceanfront golf club in Doonbeg to watch the Irish Open. Connolly, elected to Ireland’s ceremonial presidency in 2025, was among those who protested Trump’s 2019 visit to the country.

Asked about helping ease traffic through the Strait of Hormuz amid fresh Saudi jitters over the Iran war, Trump said “everything will work out just fine.” Ireland is an outsized host of U.S. pharmaceutical and tech operations, so trade and tariffs shadowed the visit even as the public events stayed light. (A working visit built around private events and a golf stop; substantive readouts from the leaders’ meetings were limited in coverage seen.)

A libertarian note (Reason) would keep an eye on conflict-of-interest questions while welcoming warmer trade ties. (Directional.)

+

In a series of Friday-night social-media posts, Trump called on the Smithsonian to erect a 30-foot “Washington Colossus” statue of George Washington outside the National Museum of American History, replacing “Infinity,” the abstract rotating stainless-steel sculpture at the entrance. (These are presidential requests posted to social media, not directives with legal force.)

Immigration · Courts

CASA and four Marylanders sue over ‘warrantless’ ICE arrests, alleging racial profiling

+

Four Maryland residents and the immigrant-rights group CASA filed a 56-page federal lawsuit in U.S. District Court in Baltimore challenging a wave of warrantless immigration arrests, alleging that ICE agents are “indiscriminately stopping and arresting” people they perceive to be Black and brown immigrants. The suit says nearly 7,000 street arrests have been carried out in Maryland communities since Jan. 20, 2025. Under federal law, agents must establish probable cause that a person is unlawfully present and likely to flee before a warrant can be obtained in order to make a warrantless civil arrest.

The Maryland case echoes a related D.C. lawsuit in which a court issued a preliminary injunction in December 2025 ordering the administration to stop making warrantless immigration arrests without individualized probable-cause determinations. (The complaint’s figures and characterizations are the plaintiffs’ allegations; the government had not responded in filings seen this cycle.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left casts this as a Fourth-Amendment and due-process emergency: mass, suspicionless sweeps that profile by appearance and inevitably ensnare citizens and lawful residents — exactly the pattern a federal judge already curbed in D.C. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The directional right frames the arrests as lawful enforcement of immigration statutes aimed at removable non-citizens, and sees activist groups and sympathetic courts trying to hamstring interior enforcement through litigation. (Directional.)

Iran War · Gulf

Saudi Arabia shuts its East–West pipeline after a drone strike as Houthis lock down the Red Sea coast

+

Saudi Arabia temporarily shut down its 745-mile East–West pipeline — the conduit it uses to move crude to the Red Sea and bypass the congested Strait of Hormuz — after the line came under a drone attack that Riyadh and Baghdad said originated in Iraq, where Iran-backed militias operate. With Hormuz tanker traffic reported down to roughly seven transits a day amid the U.S.–Iran war, Iranian-aligned forces now sit on both maritime approaches to the Arabian Peninsula: Houthi fighters, having taken the port of Mokha, have pushed along Yemen’s Red Sea coastline toward the Bab el-Mandeb strait.

Analysts framed the pipeline closure as the strategic squeeze Tehran has sought since reimposing pressure on the Gulf in July — leaving Saudi export routes exposed at both ends. It lands days after Trump declined a Saudi request for direct U.S. strikes on the Houthis, favoring intelligence and targeting support for now. (Operational details rest largely on Saudi and Iraqi statements and wire reports; treat transit counts and attribution as provisional.)

Sources: ABC News, Spokesman-Review/AP, South China Morning Post, GlobalSecurity

Tech · AI Chips

Chip startup Positron raises $875M to challenge Nvidia on inference with commodity memory

+

AI-chip startup Positron announced $875 million in new funding at a roughly $5 billion post-money valuation — a $375 million Series C plus a Series C-1 of up to $500 million — co-led by NEA, Atreides Management, Valor Equity Partners, Andra Capital, Dylan Patel’s SemiAnalysis Capital and Netscape/Silicon Graphics founder Jim Clark. The valuation is nearly five times the $1 billion Positron carried after a $230 million Series B in February.

The money funds tape-out of “Asimov,” a chip built around cheap, consumer-grade LPDDR5X memory in place of the scarce, costly high-bandwidth memory (HBM) that dominates today’s AI accelerators — a bet that commodity memory can ease supply and power bottlenecks for AI inference and loosen Nvidia’s grip on that market. Tape-out is targeted for the end of 2026, with production slated for the second half of 2027. (Valuation and roadmap figures are the company’s own disclosures.)

Energy · Electric Vehicles

U.S. EV sales slip after the federal tax credit lapses, even as Europe sets records

+

Fresh U.S. Energy Information Administration data show battery-electric vehicles fell to about 6% of new U.S. vehicle sales in the first half of 2026, down from roughly 7% a year earlier — a dip the EIA ties to the expiration of federal EV purchase and lease tax credits on Sept. 30, 2025. Hybrid sales, which don’t depend on charging infrastructure, rose over the same stretch, suggesting buyers are shifting toward electrified-but-not-plug-in options as incentives vanish.

The U.S. trend runs against the global grain: EVs set new records across Europe in August (Norway reached roughly 98.7% of new-car deliveries), and, per the IEA, cheaper lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) batteries now make up around 90% of storage deployments — with much new U.S. LFP output flowing to grid storage rather than cars. (U.S. share figures are EIA estimates; the European record is a monthly print.)

Science · Cardiology

A world-first study finds the human heart can regrow muscle after a heart attack

+

Researchers from the University of Sydney, the Baird Institute and Royal Prince Alfred Hospital reported the first direct evidence in humans that heart-muscle cells (cardiomyocytes) can re-enter cell division and regenerate after a heart attack — a process previously observed only in mice. The finding overturns the long-held view that damaged heart tissue is wholly incapable of regrowth.

The caveat is important: the natural response appears far too weak to replace all the cells lost in an infarct, so a damaged heart cannot simply heal itself. But identifying that the machinery exists at all opens a path to therapies that could amplify the heart’s own repair — a potential new front against heart failure. (Widely covered around Sept. 9; the effect is modest and not yet a treatment.)

Sources: ScienceDaily, University of Sydney, Medical Xpress, SciTechDaily
Science · Cosmology

Webb spots a dense clutch of early galaxies that may have blown a ‘bubble’ in the cosmic fog

+

Using the James Webb Space Telescope and its JADES survey, astronomers reported a candidate overdensity of galaxies in the very early universe that appears to have carved out a bubble of ionized gas — a local clearing in the neutral-hydrogen fog that once made the cosmos opaque. It offers a snapshot of reionization, the era roughly within the first 500 million years when light from the first galaxies burned through that fog and could finally travel freely.

The result supports the picture that clustered early galaxies, acting together, drove reionization from the inside out. The team says it will re-observe the region with JWST and ALMA — targeting hydrogen-alpha and doubly-ionized oxygen — to map the structure in three dimensions and directly test its role. (A candidate overdensity from a single survey; the bubble interpretation awaits the follow-up observations.)

Sources: Universe Today, arXiv / ApJ (preprint)
