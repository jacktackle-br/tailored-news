The White House · The Ad Money

No refund: Trump won’t commit to repaying the taxpayer money already spent on ads praising him, and Schumer goes to the FEC

+

A White House official said on Tuesday that President Trump has not committed to reimbursing the government for the campaign-style TV spots that have already aired with the tag “Paid for by the U.S. Government.” Asked directly whether he would pay the money back, Trump told reporters “We’ll decide,” calling the ads “really good” and “promotion for the country, done by many presidents before.” The spots were drawn from up to $20 million the Department of Homeland Security set aside; reported estimates of what has been spent so far run from about $10 million to roughly $12 million.

The hedge follows Trump’s Monday reversal: after criticism from both parties, including Senate Majority Leader John Thune, he said the pro-Trump super PAC MAGA Inc. would pay for the ads from now on. The White House later said the PAC would cover future “public service announcements,” not those already broadcast. Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer called the spending a “gross waste and abuse of taxpayer dollars,” demanded a full investigation, and said he would report Trump to the Federal Election Commission for illegal coordination with the super PAC. Critics, including the watchdog Public Citizen, argue the original ads broke laws against using appropriated money for propaganda.

The Left-Leaning Take

Slate and Mother Jones, writing as the ads spread in late September and early October, call them campaign commercials paid for with public money weeks before the midterms. Mother Jones pointed out that one spot copies a Trump campaign ad frame for frame, with only the logo changed. To this camp, moving future costs to MAGA Inc. does not fix anything: the money already spent stays spent, and a president coordinating with his own super PAC is a new problem of its own. (No in-window pieces from Vox or Guardian opinion turned up.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Right-of-center commentary has mostly been critical too. In The Dispatch (Sept. 30, updated Oct. 2), Jonah Goldberg called the ads a symptom of a “deformed constitutional order” that celebrates the president as a strongman. Republican leaders like Thune said they liked the message but objected to taxpayers paying for it. The Bulwark (anti-Trump right, not the party line) has gone much further and called for impeachment over the ads. The administration’s defenders, Vice President Vance among them, say the ads are nonpartisan PSAs, since Trump is not on the ballot. (No in-window National Review or WSJ editorial found.)

The Supreme Court · Opening Day

Big Oil vs. Boulder: a short-handed Court opens its term split over who pays for climate change

+

The Court opened its October 2026 term on Monday with nearly two hours of argument in Suncor Energy v. County Commissioners of Boulder County. The question is whether Boulder’s state-court suit against Suncor and ExxonMobil, which seeks to make them pay local costs of climate-driven floods, fires and drought, can go forward, or whether federal law preempts it. Only eight justices heard the case because Justice Samuel Alito is recused. The Trump administration sided with the companies. Their lawyer, Kannon Shanmugam, called the case “an unprecedented effort to use state law to regulate global conduct.” Boulder’s counsel, Kevin Russell, answered that states have long provided tort remedies for injuries inside their borders even when the conduct happened elsewhere.

Reporters disagree on how the argument went. CNN and CBS described a fractured bench with no clear majority, and CBS said most justices sounded more doubtful about the companies’ position. Justice Barrett questioned a central part of the oil companies’ argument. Colorado Newsline and other outlets read the Republican-appointed justices as skeptical of Boulder, pointing to Justice Kavanaugh’s remark about a “wall of precedent” treating interstate pollution as a federal matter. Alito’s recusal matters: a 4–4 tie would leave in place the Colorado Supreme Court ruling that let the suit proceed. A decision is expected by the end of the term. The Court also heard Johnson v. United States Congress, on disability benefits for incarcerated veterans, on Monday. An ERISA case is set for Wednesday.

The Left-Leaning Take

Left-leaning legal coverage, from Vox’s explainer to MSNBC’s legal blog, says the result is “difficult to predict,” with several justices’ votes in play. It also notes that Alito’s absence makes the companies’ path harder. To this camp the case is about accountability, not regulation: Boulder’s theory is that the companies deceived the public about a product they knew was dangerous, and a ruling for them would shut dozens of similar suits out of state courts. (Directional; no Slate or Mother Jones piece found in window.)

The Right-Leaning Take

National Review’s Bench Memos read the argument as a Court “poised to shut down Boulder’s bid to regulate global emissions.” Its view is that a single county using state tort law to set worldwide energy policy is exactly what the federal structure and the Clean Air Act rule out, and it cites Kavanaugh’s “wall of precedent.” Outlets further right call the suit “climate lawfare.” Libertarians tend to agree on federalism and add a separation-of-powers point: courts are the wrong place to set carbon policy. (Directional.)

The Midterms · Four Weeks Out

Trump’s approval stuck at a record-low 32% as Hispanic voters swing hard toward Democrats

+

A Reuters/Ipsos poll completed Monday puts President Trump’s approval at 32%, unchanged from September’s reading, which was the lowest of his political career. The sharpest movement is among Hispanic Americans. Only 24% approve of his performance, the lowest share in either of his terms. Among Hispanic registered voters, 52% plan to vote Democratic in the House midterms and 26% Republican, the widest Democratic edge Reuters/Ipsos has recorded since Trump took office. The online survey of 4,506 adults has a margin of error of about 2 points. Respondents pointed to rising costs that followed the war with Iran.

The map is moving the same way. The AP reported late last week that Senate Republicans’ main super PAC has shifted money out of North Carolina and into Kansas, where Sen. Roger Marshall faces Democrat Adam Hamilton. It has reserved nearly $8.2 million in ads for Marshall, and a second former Republican governor of Kansas has endorsed Hamilton. Early voting is underway in several states ahead of the November 3 election. Republican Rep. María Elvira Salazar of Florida recently ran an ad telling Trump that the Hispanics who elected him “feel betrayed today.”

The Left-Leaning Take

The left sees the 2024 Latino realignment unwinding. Mother Jones (September) reported from a Miami district now seen as a test of how far Trump has fallen with Latino voters. The argument in that camp is that deportation overreach and war-driven inflation have cost the GOP the very voters who expanded its coalition. The Iran War · The Red Sea Front

Saudi-backed forces say they have driven the Houthis back from Bab el-Mandeb as Tehran calls talks with Washington “meaningless”

+

Forces of Yemen’s internationally recognized government, backed by what a Saudi-led coalition spokesman said were 100 fighter jets, advanced quickly along the Red Sea coast on Monday. They say they retook the port of Mocha, the Dhubab district and key positions on the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, which the Iran-backed Houthis had seized in a September offensive. Reuters, citing military sources, said the push drove the Houthis out of most of what they took last month. The Houthis deny losing ground, and the government’s claims have not been independently confirmed. Saudi Arabia reported three people wounded in Houthi attacks on its southern airports.

The fighting is a second front in the US-Israeli war with Iran, now more than seven months old. Iran’s closure of the Strait of Hormuz has already strained world oil supply, and Houthi control of Bab el-Mandeb would have given Tehran another chokepoint. Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian said Monday that negotiating with the U.S. is “meaningless,” arguing Washington has “attacked us three times after talks.” Indirect talks have stalled since Trump rejected Tehran’s latest ceasefire proposal in late September. Separately, the U.S. has moved its B-1B bombers out of Britain’s RAF Fairford after an alleged Iran-linked plot against the base. Secretary of State Marco Rubio declined to tie the two directly. Germany · Espionage

Germany’s former spy chief is arrested on suspicion of treason

+

German federal prosecutors arrested August Hanning, 80, who led the foreign intelligence service, the BND, from 1998 to 2005. They say he is “strongly suspected” of treasonous espionage, spying out state secrets and attempted treason. A former aide, identified only as Manfred D., was arrested the same day in Steinfurt and charged with aiding and abetting. According to prosecutors, the aide gave Hanning about 2,000 documents over the years, many classified for internal BND use only. Hanning allegedly used them in private consulting work and, at least once, used the material to draft an analysis for an officer of a foreign intelligence service.

Prosecutors have not said which country the two are suspected of working for. That gap, and Hanning’s stature as a former head of German intelligence, make this an unusually serious espionage case. It comes as Berlin deepens its support for Ukraine. Chancellor Friedrich Merz was in Kyiv over the weekend pledging another €1 billion in military aid.

Spain · Snap Election

Sánchez gambles on a snap election for November 29 after parliament sinks his housing plan

+

Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez called an early general election for November 29 in a televised address on Monday. On Friday, the lower house had voted down two emergency decrees meant to protect renters, despite last-minute negotiations. Housing has become Spain’s most explosive issue. Protests spread after an 87-year-old woman was evicted when she could no longer afford a steep rent increase, and violence broke out in Barcelona as tens of thousands marched. Sánchez said he needs a stronger mandate to “overcome the resistance of the elites.” His cabinet has also approved a package of 21 urgent housing measures.

The vote comes more than a year before it was due, and Sánchez starts behind. His Socialists trail in nearly every poll. A defeat would remove one of the last left-led governments among Europe’s major economies.

Energy · The Oil Shock

“Scarily thin”: Aramco’s chief warns the world’s oil cushion is nearly gone, and could take two years to rebuild

+

Saudi Aramco CEO Amin Nasser told the Energy Intelligence Forum in London on Monday that the world’s “supply resilience cushion is scarily thin.” By his count, global oil inventories have fallen from about 10 billion barrels when the US-Iran war began to under 6 billion. Only about 10% of that is practically available, he said, which is why governments are “struggling with 100 million barrels.” He also warned that refilling stocks could take up to two years even after the Strait of Hormuz fully reopens.

The “100 million barrels” is the emergency release the G7 agreed last week. It is coordinated through the IEA and front-loaded with diesel to ease a refined-fuel shortage that has pushed diesel prices to record highs. It follows a record 400-million-barrel IEA release in March, about two-thirds of which has been drawn so far. The IEA’s governing board is due to set the details at its October 14–15 meeting. Nasser’s warning suggests these drawdowns are using up a buffer the world may not be able to rebuild quickly.

Artificial Intelligence · Open Models & Agents

Mistral bets on a trillion-parameter open model as Meta and others write the rules for AI agents that shop for you

+

French AI lab Mistral released a new flagship on Tuesday, Mistral Large 4, nicknamed “le Chonk.” It is a 1-trillion-parameter open model that Mistral calls the most capable open system built outside China, with particular strengths in cyber, coding, manufacturing, finance and multimodal work. The release strengthens Europe’s main open-weights challenger against Chinese labs. One of those, DeepSeek, is reportedly considering doubling its current funding round to as much as $15 billion.

Also Tuesday, Meta, Walmart, Stripe and Bret Taylor’s startup Sierra published a “personal agent protocol.” It is an open standard for how AI agents identify themselves and transact with businesses, aimed at what its backers call the “chaos” of bots placing orders and making bookings. Meta’s own agent app, Muse, has held the top spot in Apple’s App Store since early September. CNBC reports that long-running agents are increasing demand for ordinary CPUs, which has pushed AMD shares to new highs.

The Grid · Gas, Data Centers & Cars

AI’s appetite pushes planned US gas plants up 44% in eight months, while Tesla turns its best-sellers into home backup batteries

+

New analysis of filings with the federal Energy Information Administration puts planned grid-connected gas capacity at 60.4 GW through 2030. That is up 44% from the 41.8 GW expected in December 2025, and nearly triple the pipeline in early 2023. Texas accounts for about a third of it. Data centers are the main driver: gas projects planned on-site for data centers jumped from 97 GW at the end of 2025 to 189 GW in the first half of 2026. Climate analysts warn that plants built now could lock in decades of fossil-fuel use.

Electrification moved forward on a smaller scale too. On Tuesday, Tesla switched on Powershare Home Backup for the Model 3 and Model Y, so its two volume cars can power a house during an outage through a Powerwall 3. Several rival automakers already offered this. Electrek’s first drive of the Kia EV3 ($29,890, up to 321 miles of range) called it the best-packaged affordable EV now on sale in the US. Both come as US EV demand is under pressure after Congress repealed federal tax credits.

Paleontology · Egypt

A crocodile hidden in the Sphinx’s stone: scientists name an ancient species that also lost its nose

+

Paleontologists have identified a new extinct crocodilian, Herugavialis cairensis. It was a long-snouted, fish-eating gavial that hunted a warm, shallow sea over what is now northern Egypt about 45 million years ago. Its fossils come from the same limestone formation that ancient Egyptians carved into the Great Sphinx. The bones were dug up in the 1920s and filed under the living false gharial until CT scans showed features that set them apart. The study appears in Royal Society Open Science.

The name has a story. Like the Sphinx, the fossil skull is missing the tip of its nose. The team, co-led by Philip Mannion of University College London, named the genus after Heru (Horus), the god possibly behind the monument. The find adds an Eocene branch to the gavial family tree and shows that old museum drawers can still hold new species.

Sources: CNN, Oct 6, Science News, Phys.org
