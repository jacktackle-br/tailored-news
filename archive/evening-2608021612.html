National Security · US–Iran War

Trump says U.S.–Iran talks will resume Monday after he called off a “massive attack” — Tehran denies asking for the pause as pump prices hit $4.09

+

Following up on his Saturday-evening announcement, Trump said Sunday (Aug. 2) that negotiations with Iran would resume Monday after he called off what he described as a “massive attack,” and that Israel had agreed to join a U.S. commitment to try to complete an agreement ending the roughly five-month war. Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman had urged Trump to refrain from new strikes, stressing the need for dialogue to de-escalate, per the official Saudi news agency. Iran again gave no indication it had requested any pause: its semi-official Fars agency dismissed Trump’s move as a “repeated retreat from threats.” No new strikes were reported by either side into Sunday evening.

The framework Trump has floated would pair an “immediate, complete and total” reopening of the Strait of Hormuz and an end to Iran’s nuclear threat with a lifting of the U.S. naval blockade and a resumption of Iranian oil exports, inside a roughly two-week window to finalize terms. The economic backdrop is the new pressure point since this morning: the national average for gasoline stood at about $4.09 a gallon Sunday — roughly 34% above pre-war levels — a drag analysts increasingly tie to Trump’s sinking approval. On legality, the war was launched in February 2026 without congressional authorization, and the Senate’s most recent war-powers resolution failed roughly 49–50 on July 30 (per Lawfare’s tracking).

The Left-Leaning Take

Another “talks resume Monday” announcement is exactly the improvisation critics warned about: a constitutionally illegitimate war Trump entangled the country in on his own authority, now managed by on-again, off-again strike threats and a vague, terms-to-be-determined pause that resembles the earlier framework that collapsed. Meanwhile ordinary families pay for it at a $4-plus pump — the clearest sign the strategy is a president searching for an exit he has no plan to reach. (Synthesized from Guardian and Vox opinion.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The hawkish mainstream right stays skeptical from the other side: reopening Hormuz and lifting the blockade in exchange for a pledge rewards Iranian coercion of the waterway, an arrangement the Wall Street Journal editorial board earlier branded a “strategic retreat” and National Review called lopsided toward Tehran. Intra-right dissent: the populist non-interventionist wing and libertarians (Reason) instead welcome any off-ramp and note the war was never authorized — a minority strain, not the hawkish mainstream position.

Sources: Washington Post, Bloomberg, CNN, NBC News

The war between the U.S. and Iran, and the gas-price inflation it has driven, are cited as major contributors to the slide. This is new since this morning’s brief, which focused on the mechanics of the Senate map rather than the president’s own numbers.

Europe · Russia’s War on Ukraine

Russia pounds Kyiv with ballistic missiles and drones overnight as its summer offensive stalls at Pokrovsk and July casualties hit a 2026 high

+

Russia launched roughly 35 missiles — most of them ballistic — and nearly 190 drones at Ukraine overnight into Aug. 1, with Kyiv and the surrounding region the primary targets, according to the Institute for the Study of War (ISW) and Ukrainian officials. ISW assessed that Moscow is exploiting Ukraine’s shortage of Patriot interceptors, and that Russia fired a record number of missiles in July — more than it produces in a month — leaning on S-400 and Zircon systems to offset dwindling ballistic stocks. Over the past 24 hours Ukraine reported 193 combat clashes, the heaviest concentrated in the Pokrovsk sector.

The intensity of the bombardment masks a stalling ground campaign. Two and a half months into Russia’s spring–summer offensive, its rate of advance remains low and it has made no operationally significant gains, while suffering its highest casualty rate of 2026 in July — paying a steep price for minimal territory around Pokrovsk, the offensive’s intended centerpiece. The pattern points to an attritional war in which long-range strikes, not maneuver, are Moscow’s main lever — and in which Ukraine’s interceptor supply is the pressure point to watch.

Sources: Kyiv Post (ISW), Ukrinform

National Security · US–Iran War

Trump says he called off a fresh wave of strikes on Iran, claiming mediators reached the “parameters” of a deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz — Tehran signals no such concession

+

In a Saturday-evening (Aug. 1) social-media post, President Trump said he had agreed to cancel a planned attack on Iran “subject to being able to rapidly make a DEAL,” asserting that Iran and “other Middle Eastern countries” had asked him to hold off. He described an emerging framework that would include the “Immediate, Complete, and Total OPENING OF THE HORMUZ STRAIT, and an end to Iran’s nuclear threat.” Reporting indicated the proposal would also halt Iran-backed militia attacks in Iraq against Gulf states and Jordan and, in exchange, lift the U.S. naval blockade and again allow Iranian oil exports. No final deal has been reached, mediation is described as ongoing, and U.S. and Israeli forces had been preparing what would have been one of the harshest bombing campaigns of the war — aimed at Iranian energy infrastructure, possibly this weekend. No strikes were reported from either side overnight into Sunday.

The move is the latest turn in a roughly five-month war that began Feb. 28, 2026, with joint U.S.–Israeli strikes launched without congressional authorization; a spring ceasefire collapsed on July 8, after which Washington reimposed a naval blockade. Iranian state media gave no indication Tehran had requested a pause or shifted on the waterway, and the semi-official Fars agency mocked Trump. The State Department issued a fresh advisory urging Americans across the Middle East to consider departing now; Kuwait said it activated air defenses and downed Iranian drones early Saturday; and Saudi leadership publicly urged de-escalation. On the war’s legality, the Senate has repeatedly tried and failed to force a withdrawal — the most recent war-powers resolution failed roughly 49–50 on July 30 — and Trump has suggested the War Powers Act itself is unconstitutional (per Lawfare’s tracking of the administration’s war-powers reporting).

The Left-Leaning Take

The whole enterprise is a constitutionally illegitimate, open-ended war Trump entangled the country in on his own authority, and this “deal” is another vague, unverifiable arrangement much like the earlier memorandum that collapsed back into fighting. A two-week, terms-to-be-determined pause changes nothing about a war Congress has repeatedly tried and failed to end; the on-again, off-again strike threats reveal a president improvising toward an exit he has no strategy to reach. (Synthesized from Guardian and Vox opinion.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The hawkish mainstream right is skeptical from the opposite direction: the Wall Street Journal editorial board branded the earlier Iran arrangement a “strategic retreat” and an “economic bailout” of Tehran, and National Review has called the terms “lopsided in Tehran’s favor” — framing another off-ramp as rewarding Iranian coercion of the Strait. Intra-right dissent: the populist non-interventionist wing and libertarians (Reason) instead cheer any de-escalation and stress the war was never authorized — a distinct minority strain, not the hawkish mainstream position.

Energy · Batteries

Australia’s home-battery rebate nears 500,000 installs — adding more storage in a year than the entire US home fleet

+

Australia’s federal Cheaper Home Batteries Program is on track to pass half a million installations in August, according to the latest tally reported by RenewEconomy. Roughly 487,000 subsidized systems had been installed as of the reporting date — adding about 13 gigawatt-hours of behind-the-meter storage — with installers still adding around 2,000 systems a day, even after the rebate was wound back in May.

For scale, the 13 GWh installed in Australia over twelve months exceeds the roughly 9 GWh of the entire US residential battery fleet. The $7.2 billion program launched July 1, 2025, and is projected to put batteries in more than 2 million Australian homes by 2030, delivering on the order of 40 GWh of additional storage. The rapid uptake is increasingly relevant to grid operation, providing distributed capacity to soak up midday solar and discharge into the evening peak.

Astronomy · Space

A private robot sent to save NASA’s Swift telescope is spinning out of control, pushing the rescue to late August

+

The commercial mission to rescue NASA’s Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory hit serious trouble in late July. Katalyst Space Technologies’ LINK servicing spacecraft — a roughly $30 million craft built by the Arizona startup to grapple the aging observatory and boost it to a higher orbit — went into a spin over the weekend after two of its three attitude-control reaction wheels became inoperable during commissioning. Engineers used a series of thruster burns to cut the spin rate from about 9 degrees per second to under 4, and said they were “not giving up,” per a July 31 NASA blog update and reporting in Scientific American.

The rendezvous with Swift, previously imminent, has now slipped to the end of August. The stakes are concrete: the roughly $500 million Swift observatory has no propulsion of its own and faced about a 90% chance of falling out of orbit later this year; a successful boost could add years of life to the gamma-ray-burst and transient-astronomy workhorse. The attempt is being watched as an early test of commercial in-space servicing of an uncooperative science satellite never designed for docking.

Sources: Scientific American, NASA (Swift blog), The Register
Archaeology · Science

LiDAR uncovers 396 hidden earthworks in the Amazon, evidence of a “lost” civilization of up to 3 million people

+

A study published in Nature (reported July 31) used airborne LiDAR to reveal 396 previously unknown geoglyphs — large geometric earthworks defined by deep ditches and raised banks — beneath the forest canopy of Brazil’s Acre state. Researchers flew more than 4,400 kilometers and surveyed roughly 4,500 square kilometers, producing the clearest picture yet of the “Aquiry” culture, which carved ceremonial and settlement earthworks into southwestern Amazonia between about 600 BCE and 850 CE.

Extrapolating from earthwork density and the labor needed to build and maintain them, the team estimated a peak population of roughly 1.25 to 3 million people around CE 100–300, and projected that the wider region may hold 24,000 to 30,000 such structures in total. The findings add to a growing body of evidence overturning the old view of the pre-Columbian Amazon as untouched wilderness, pointing instead to large, organized societies that engineered the landscape at scale.

Sources: Scientific American, Archaeology Magazine, Live Science
