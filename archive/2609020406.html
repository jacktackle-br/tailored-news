Iran War · Regional Escalation

The U.S.–Iran war spills across the Gulf as Tehran fires on Arab states and Brent oil pushes toward $95

+

The war widened overnight into Wednesday, Sept. 2. After U.S. Central Command struck Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps targets inside Iran — a response to attacks on commercial shipping and on U.S. forces in the region — Iran launched retaliatory drones and missiles reported to have targeted U.S.-linked sites across several Gulf and neighboring states, with incoming fire directed at Kuwait, Jordan and Bahrain. The exchange followed Monday’s strikes on two crude tankers roughly 17 nautical miles off Khasab, Oman, the first reported attacks on commercial vessels since CENTCOM declared the strait’s lanes cleared of mines. Brent crude climbed above $95 a barrel intraday before easing, with U.S. WTI near $90, as traders priced in a fresh war-risk premium.

President Trump vowed to respond “very hard,” is reported to be weighing a strike on Iran’s Kharg Island oil-export hub, and again posted an AI-generated video captioned to suggest Kharg had been hit — though a U.S. official confirmed no such strike has occurred, marking the claim a threat, not an event. Vice President JD Vance has warned Tehran to stop firing on commercial shipping “or there are going to be some consequences.” The IEA has characterized the months-long disruption to Hormuz traffic — the chokepoint for roughly a fifth of the world’s seaborne oil — as among the largest supply shocks in the history of the oil market. (Some regional-strike claims rest on preliminary and disputed reporting; the Kharg claim remains unverified.)

Economy · Labor Market

A stalling job market becomes the midterms’ central fight as Trump’s immigration crackdown shrinks the workforce

+

Ten weeks before the midterms, the economy has moved to the center of the political fight, and the data are mixed at best. Monthly job growth that averaged more than 230,000 in 2022–24 has slowed to roughly 30,000 over the past year and a half; the labor force has contracted by more than a million people since January; and the unemployment rate for U.S.-born workers has risen to about 4.7% from 4.4% a year ago — the opposite of what the administration predicted its immigration crackdown would deliver for native-born workers. Goldman Sachs estimates net immigration has collapsed by roughly 80%, and the CBO projects the reduced inflows will mean about 5.3 million fewer U.S. residents a decade from now while adding roughly half a trillion dollars to the deficit by 2035.

Administration officials frame the slowdown as a deliberate, healthy adjustment. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has argued the U.S. simply doesn’t need to add as many jobs as before, given a shrinking labor supply. But rising gasoline prices — pushed up by the Iran war — and soft hiring have handed Democrats a kitchen-table message, and new polling shows Republican candidates trailing in North Carolina and even in GOP-leaning Ohio, Iowa and Texas, with some Senate Republicans privately describing the president as a growing liability. (Figures per Goldman Sachs and CBO estimates and administration data; monthly totals are revised.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees the crackdown backfiring on its own terms: native-born unemployment is up, hiring has stalled, key sectors face labor shortages, and the promised windfall for American workers never arrived — only a smaller economy and a bigger deficit. Bessent’s “we don’t need as many jobs,” they argue, is an admission that the plan was never really about jobs. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The directional right counters that a tighter labor market is the point: fewer workers competing for jobs should, in time, lift wages for citizens, and slower headline growth reflects a one-off supply reset, not a downturn. Supply-side skeptics on the right are warier — warning that shrinking the workforce and widening the deficit is a strange way to run a “roaring economy,” and that gas prices and soft hiring are a real midterm vulnerability. (Directional; no in-window signed editorial verified.)

Congress · Shutdown Averted

Trump signs the stopgap into law, funding the government to Dec. 11 and shoving the real spending fight past the midterms

+

The shutdown threat is off the table. After the House passed the Senate-cleared continuing resolution 370–48 on Tuesday, Sept. 1, President Trump signed it into law, funding the government through Dec. 11 and averting a partial shutdown before the Oct. 1 fiscal deadline. The opposition was bipartisan and small — 19 Republicans and 29 Democrats voted no — and the deal carried odd riders, including a short-term delay of a congressionally enacted ban on intoxicating hemp products that the White House and industry wanted time to renegotiate.

The lopsided margin confirmed the cross-party truce both leaderships wanted ten weeks out from the midterms: neither side chose to stage a shutdown on voters’ screens with control of both chambers on the ballot. But the peace is procedural, not substantive — the full-year appropriations disputes are simply deferred into a post-election, lame-duck December, where the fights over spending levels, riders and the hemp ban will all resurface. (Vote tally per congressional reporting; signing per CBS News.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left takes the relief and keeps the wariness: a clean-ish extension beats a manufactured crisis, but punting to a lame-duck December hands the majority a quiet window and preserves leverage for a year-end squeeze on programs voters depend on. They note the same figures who courted shutdowns in past standoffs suddenly prize calm precisely because the pre-election polling is poor. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The institutional right calls it responsible governance and smart politics — keep the lights on, deny Democrats a shutdown to run against, and fight the spending war from a stronger post-election footing. The 19 Republican no votes are the tell: hardline fiscal conservatives argue another status-quo CR locks in bloated baselines and forfeits the majority’s one moment of real leverage. (Directional; no in-window signed editorial verified.)

Space · Astrophysics

NASA’s Nancy Grace Roman telescope is off — launched nine months early to map dark energy and hunt distant worlds

+

NASA’s Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope lifted off Aug. 30 at 7:26 a.m. EDT aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center, roughly nine months ahead of schedule, and is now on a million-mile cruise to the second Sun–Earth Lagrange point (L2). Roman is a wide-field flagship: its camera has a field of view about 100 times larger than Hubble’s, letting it survey vast swaths of sky at comparable sharpness and generate enormous panoramas of the cosmos.

Its headline goals are the universe’s deepest mysteries. To probe dark energy, Roman will chart how gravity subtly bends light across cosmic distances — weak gravitational lensing — building a three-dimensional map of dark matter along the way, and will use exploding stars and the clustering of galaxies as complementary rulers of cosmic expansion. It will also discover thousands of exoplanets, many via microlensing toward the star-packed galactic bulge. NASA expects Roman’s first images in early 2027. (Mission now in commissioning; science surveys begin after checkout.)

Sources: NASA, CNN, NASA Science (Roman blog)

Energy · Global Shift

For the first time, solar was the world’s single biggest source of energy-demand growth, the IEA’s 2026 review finds

+

The International Energy Agency’s Global Energy Review 2026 marks a milestone: in 2025, solar power met more than a quarter of the world’s additional energy needs — the largest single share of global energy-demand growth from any source, ahead of fossil fuels. Renewable capacity additions rose about 16% to some 800 gigawatts, a 23rd consecutive record year, with solar PV accounting for more than three-quarters of the new build and wind for roughly a fifth. India led the majors, boosting additions nearly 60% on the back of almost 50 GW of new solar.

The picture is uneven. Offshore wind stumbled — EU installations fell to just 1 GW, down from 1.7 GW a year earlier, with only France and Germany adding capacity — a reminder that the transition is being led by cheap solar and batteries rather than by costlier, policy-sensitive technologies. The IEA calculates that the cumulative deployment of solar, wind, nuclear, EVs and heat pumps since 2019 now displaces more than 35 exajoules of fossil-fuel demand a year, equal to around 7% of global fossil use. (Figures per IEA analysis of 2025 data.)

Sources: IEA, Energy Digital
Physics · Superconductivity

A kagome metal turns out to hide two superconducting states at once — and strain can switch one on without the other

+

A study published Aug. 28 in Physical Review Letters reports that the ground state of the kagome metal CsV₃Sb₅ is not one superconducting phase but two nearly identical (“degenerate”) states with different gap symmetries. Using nuclear spin-lattice relaxation measurements while stretching the crystal, the team observed a striking double superconducting transition under tensile strain, with the upper transition showing a fully gapped (nodeless) state — direct evidence that two distinct pairing states coexist rather than merging into one.

Crucially, strain sharply raised the superconducting transition temperature while leaving the material’s charge-density-wave order untouched, showing that superconductivity here can be tuned independently of the surrounding charge order. Kagome metals — built from a lattice of corner-sharing triangles — have become a testbed for exotic, intertwined electronic phases, and the result sharpens a long-running puzzle about how unconventional superconductivity emerges from such materials. (Peer-reviewed condensed-matter result; interpretation of the pairing states remains under active study.)

Sources: Physical Review Letters, ALS / Berkeley Lab, Phys.org
