National Security · US–Iran War

Trump says he called off “the biggest attack since World War II” on Iran and that talks begin Monday — Tehran denies any deal, says it is negotiating with Oman, not Washington

+

Late Saturday (Aug. 1) into Sunday, President Trump said he was halting a planned major strike on Iran, asserting that “the perimeters” of a deal had been reached and that he acted at the request of Gulf allies — Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Qatar — as well as Iran itself. He described the framework as including the “Immediate, Complete, and Total OPENING OF THE HORMUZ STRAIT” and an end to Iran’s nuclear threat, with Israel joining the pause, and told reporters aboard Air Force One that negotiations would “begin Monday afternoon” — today, Aug. 3. Trump called the canceled operation what would have been “the biggest attack since World War II,” and made the pause explicitly conditional on “being able to rapidly make a DEAL.” No strikes were reported from either side over the weekend.

Tehran flatly contradicted the account. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Iran is negotiating with Oman — not the United States — over temporary safe passage through the Strait of Hormuz, that those talks are in their “final stages,” and that the strait “will never return to its pre-war status” while U.S. “aggression” continues; the semi-official Mehr agency called Trump’s telling “a lie.” Iran also floated a new toll-and-governance regime for the waterway, which the U.S. Secretary of State has said would make a deal “unfeasible.” The Washington Post noted this fits a pattern: Trump has repeatedly announced halts to Iran strikes that then proceeded. The war began Feb. 28, 2026 without congressional authorization; the most recent Senate war-powers resolution failed roughly 49–50 on July 30 (per Lawfare’s tracking), and the practical test today is whether the “Monday afternoon” talks actually materialize.

The Left-Leaning Take

Trump manufactured this crisis and is now claiming victory for gesturing back toward a status quo — a free Strait of Hormuz — that existed before he and Netanyahu launched an unauthorized war. Iran’s on-the-record denials expose the “deal” as a face-saving narrative, and the on-again, off-again strike threats read as improvisation, not strategy. (Synthesized from Slate and MSNBC opinion.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The hawkish mainstream right is scathing from the opposite side: the Wall Street Journal editorial board (“Trump’s Latest Iran ‘DEAL’,” Aug. 2) mocked that he “has already ended the Iran war eight times” and asked whether the ninth will be the charm, urging that Iran be judged by what it does, not what either side promises. Intra-right dissent: anti-Trump conservatives at The Bulwark (David Frum and Jonathan V. Last, “Iran Is Going to Make Trump Eat It”) argue he has boxed himself in and Tehran holds the leverage — a minority strain, not the hawkish-mainstream line.

Jonah Goldberg (The Dispatch)

In the G-File (“The Iran Peace Deal Is What Trumpism Looks Like”), Goldberg argues the “deal” is less strategy than self-interest — “in war and in peace, Trump’s loyalty is to Trump” — and that he cannot honestly claim to have met his three stated aims of ending the war, reopening Hormuz and blocking an Iranian bomb. Justice Department · Confirmation Fight

Blanche cuts a Sunday-night deal to rescue his AG nomination, scrapping Trump’s $1.8 billion “anti-weaponization” fund — committee vote reset for Tuesday

+

Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche broke the confirmation standoff Sunday night (Aug. 2), announcing a deal with the two Republican holdouts — Sens. John Cornyn (Texas) and Thom Tillis (N.C.) — and issuing an order rescinding the roughly $1.8 billion “anti-weaponization” fund at the center of their objections. The fund grew out of the Justice Department’s settlement of Trump’s lawsuit against the IRS; critics warned its payouts could reach Jan. 6 participants and that a related May 19 release order, which Blanche alone signed, conferred sweeping benefits on Trump, his family and his businesses. With the fund gone, the Senate Judiciary Committee — which postponed a vote on July 29 — has rescheduled its vote for 9 a.m. Tuesday (Aug. 4).

The reversal caps weeks of brinkmanship. Cornyn and Tillis had said in late July they were not ready to back Blanche, throwing the nomination into jeopardy just before the August recess; National Review reported Trump was threatening to withdraw the name and wait for a “more pliant” Senate in January. Blanche — Trump’s former personal criminal-defense lawyer, confirmed as deputy attorney general in March 2025 and acting attorney general since April 2, 2026 — still faces opposition that foregrounds a federal judge’s referral of government attorneys in the IRS case, including Blanche, to state bars for possible discipline.

The Left-Leaning Take

Scrapping the fund does not fix the deeper problem: Blanche is still, in office, acting as Trump’s lawyer. His written answers and his lone signature on the self-dealing IRS release show he lacks the independence the job requires, and Cornyn and Tillis extracted a single concession while waving through a fundamentally compromised nominee who deserves rejection. (Synthesized from Washington Post and MSNBC opinion.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The MAGA-aligned right (Blaze Media: “Confirm Todd Blanche and reject the right’s culture of surrender”) wants him confirmed and casts the holdouts as squishes; National Review framed the fight as Trump “playing a high-stakes game of chicken.” Intra-right dissent: the Washington Times opinion page ran “Why the Senate should reject Todd Blanche,” a notable break treating the IRS-settlement conduct as disqualifying — a minority view, not the mainstream-right position.

Sources: NBC News, Just the News, National Review, Washington Post (opinion)
2026 Midterms · Senate Map

Democrats have put an unusually wide red-state Senate map in play — but CNN warns a stubborn “approval gap” may keep it from being enough

+

A CNN analysis published Monday (Aug. 3) laid out a 2026 Senate battleground tilted sharply toward Republican turf: of the nine seats seen as most competitive — Alaska, Georgia, Iowa, Maine, Michigan, New Hampshire, North Carolina, Ohio and Texas — Trump carried all but Maine and New Hampshire in 2024. Democrats have nonetheless made several red states genuinely competitive: a Fox News poll shows Texas state Rep. James Talarico at 51% to Attorney General Ken Paxton’s 48%, North Carolina pits Republican Michael Whatley against former Gov. Roy Cooper, and Kentucky has Rep. Andy Barr against Charles Booker. The party is also deploying a novel tactic — nudging no-hope nominees to drop out so independents can face Republicans one-on-one in deep-red states.

The backdrop is Trump’s slumping standing: polling aggregators put his approval in the high-30s and net around –20 (a second-term low as of July 31), with independents near 34% — below the 36% that preceded the 2018 Democratic wave — though Gallup’s early-August reading was higher at 40%. CNN’s caveat is the catch: Trump’s approval has fallen below the level that preceded midterm waves in 1994, 2006, 2010 and 2018, yet Democrats are struggling to convert disapproval into support for their own candidates — an “approval gap” that could blunt any wave.

The Left-Leaning Take

A genuinely expanded map plus historically bad Trump numbers create a real chance to flip the chamber, and the drop-out-for-independents gambit is a creative answer to a brutal map (Schumer touts a “clear and strong path”). The more sober liberal read concedes the conversion problem — disapproval isn’t yet translating into Democratic votes, and red-state gravity may cap the gains. (Synthesized from liberal-leaning election analysis and Brookings.)

The Right-Leaning Take

“In play” polling in red states routinely overstates Democratic strength; fundamentals and 2024 margins tend to reassert themselves by November, and the “approval gap” is itself the evidence that Democrats are overrated on this map. Expect the emphasis to fall on candidate quality and Trump-state partisanship as the ceiling on any Democratic upside. (Synthesized from RealClearPolling analysis and conservative midterm previews.)

Sources: CNN, NBC News, RealClearPolling, Brookings

Europe · Russia’s War on Ukraine

Ukraine mounts a deep overnight drone barrage on Russian oil and air-base targets as Russia claims 635 drones downed — and its own strikes kill civilians inside Ukraine

+

Ukraine launched one of its larger long-range drone operations of the summer overnight into Sunday (Aug. 2). Kyiv’s military said its forces struck the Saratov oil refinery and the Engels strategic-bomber air base — setting fires at both — and hit an oil depot in the western Kaluga region; regional governors reported the drones also killed several people and set ablaze a warehouse of Wildberries, Russia’s largest online retailer. Russia’s Defense Ministry claimed it downed 635 Ukrainian drones overnight, an unusually high figure that itself signals the scale of the assault. Outlets diverge on the toll inside Russia — AP and France24 report “at least five” killed while the Moscow Times and others say eight — a discrepancy to treat as a live count.

On the Ukrainian side, Russian attacks over the preceding day killed at least six people and injured more than 80, regional authorities said. Ukraine’s Air Force reported Russia launched 133 drones overnight, of which 109 were downed, with 24 striking 19 locations. The tit-for-tat deep-strike escalation continues even as diplomatic attention sits elsewhere: Ukraine is now routinely reaching refineries and bases hundreds of kilometers inside Russia to degrade its refining capacity and rear-area logistics, while Russia’s spring–summer offensive around Pokrovsk remains largely stalled.

Sources: France24 (AFP), The Moscow Times, Reuters via US News, Kyiv Independent
Middle East · Gaza

Israeli strikes escalate across Gaza despite Trump’s “Board of Peace” roadmap, as an Israeli minister insists there is “no deal to halt attacks”

+

Israeli strikes across Gaza intensified over the weekend and into Monday even as the July 30–31 “Board of Peace” disarmament roadmap is supposedly being finalized. Al Jazeera reported at least 19 Palestinians killed Sunday (Aug. 2) as attacks escalated; NBC News put the day’s toll at 13 and other outlets reported nine — a material cross-source discrepancy reflecting rolling, contested counts. Among the named dead in an air raid on Deir el-Balah were Kamal Abu Muailiq, 68, and his wife Huda, 59. Crucially, an Israeli minister stated there was “no deal to halt attacks,” underscoring that Israel has not publicly accepted the latest framework. Gaza health authorities say that since the October 2025 ceasefire, at least 1,222 Palestinians have been killed and 4,053 injured.

The sequencing dispute has hardened rather than resolved. Under the roadmap, a first phase would hand civilian governance and security to a transitional committee supervised by the Board; a second phase requires Hamas and other factions to disarm completely, with the detailed plan to be drafted within 14 days and verified internationally. But Israel has not publicly agreed; Hamas says it will implement nothing unless Israeli forces meet withdrawal obligations, while the Israel Defense Forces say they will not pull back from the “Yellow Line” until Hamas fully disarms. The continued strikes and the explicit “no deal to halt attacks” line cast fresh doubt on the plan’s two-week timetable.

Sources: Al Jazeera, NBC News, AP via Star-Advertiser, Times of Israel

Artificial Intelligence

OpenAI’s unreleased “Astra” model posts solutions to 10 long-open math problems, each with a machine-checkable Lean proof — and mathematicians are split

+

On Aug. 1 (widely covered Aug. 2), OpenAI published ten solutions to previously open problems in mathematics and theoretical computer science, produced by an internal, not-yet-released model it calls Astra and bills as its “next major model.” The headline result is the first explicit construction of a non-sofic group, resolving a question open since Mikhail Gromov introduced soficity in 1999. Other claimed results include a counterexample to Connes’s rigidity conjecture, improved upper bounds for high-dimensional sphere packing, an n⁴/log n formula lower bound for the permanent, and a superexponential lower bound for multicolor Ramsey numbers (resolving Erdős problem 183). Each result shipped with a Lean 4 formalization — machine-checkable certificates posted to OpenAI’s GitHub with a reported “sorry” count of zero (no unproven steps), at a compute cost of roughly $2,000 per result.

The reaction is itself part of the story. Fields Medalist Timothy Gowers said he would recommend one of the proofs to the top journal Annals of Mathematics “without hesitation,” and mathematician Daniel Litt called it “a big deal.” Skeptics including Gary Marcus caution that math and coding give models unusually clean verification signals, so success here should not be read as general reasoning; observers also note the results cluster in AI-friendly areas and that OpenAI did not repeat the broad external-peer-review claim it made for its May 2026 Erdős unit-distance result. The June 2 “Leiden Declaration,” backed by the International Mathematical Union with signatories including Terence Tao, is being cited as a warning against judging results by company announcements alone.

Sources: SiliconANGLE, The Decoder, Tech Times
Climate · Energy

China’s solar surge nears a historic milestone as coal slips below half of electricity generation for the first time on record

+

China’s solar build-out is approaching a historic threshold even as Beijing signals it will rein in the pace of new additions, according to a report dated Aug. 2 (SCMP, also carried by The Star). Solar generation surged more than 40% year-on-year in the first half of 2026 and met roughly one-third of peak electricity demand during midday summer heatwaves. Most strikingly, coal-fired power fell below 50% of total electricity generation for the first time on record in the first half of the year — a symbolic marker for the world’s largest emitter.

The momentum builds on a record 2025, when China added 315 GW of solar and 119 GW of wind in a single year — more new renewable capacity than any nation in history — pushing wind-plus-solar to about 22% of its mix and driving the first absolute annual drop in coal output since 2015 (per Ember). Battery storage is scaling even faster, shifting more of each day’s solar past sunset. The new wrinkle is policy: amid grid-absorption and curtailment concerns, Beijing is now moving to slow the sheer scale of additions.

Sources: The Star / SCMP, SCMP, Ember
Space · Planetary Science

NASA’s Curiosity rover finds a vast “sea of polygons” on Mars — a honeycomb field that may record ancient water

+

NASA/JPL announced (release circulating Aug. 1, picked up broadly Aug. 2) that the Curiosity rover has imaged an enormous field of honeycomb-shaped polygonal textures in a region called “Valle Grande” on the slopes of Mount Sharp, capturing a 360-degree view over June 19–20, 2026. Curiosity has seen polygons before but never this many concentrated in one place, with individual shapes ranging from roughly 2 to 4 inches (5–10 cm) across.

Polygonal patterning can form through several processes — surface drying like mud cracks, repeated temperature cycling, compaction after burial, or shrinkage from water loss or mineral changes — several of which point to the past presence of water. Scientists are now analyzing the shapes and the chemistry of the terrain to determine which process built this landscape, with implications for reconstructing the history of water and habitability in Gale Crater.

Sources: NASA/JPL, NASA
Batteries · Electrification

Massachusetts opens its residential-battery virtual power plant to EVs, paying drivers to send power back to the grid

+

Utilities Eversource and National Grid are adding residential vehicle-to-grid (V2G) charging to their ConnectedSolutions virtual power plant in Massachusetts (announced around July 23, with follow-on coverage in the days after). The program paid more than $5.4 million to residential battery owners in the state in 2025; the expansion now lets EV owners feed power back during peak demand. Eligible vehicles include the Ford F-150 Lightning, Nissan LEAF, Kia EV9, Polestar 3 and Volvo EX90, with performance incentives up to $275 per average kW over the summer — roughly $1,375–$2,750 for many participants — and support from EnergyHub and The Mobility House.

The scale of the untapped resource is the point. Massachusetts already has more than 150,000 EVs on the road — at about 75 kWh each, roughly 12 GWh of potential capacity, versus just under 1.6 GWh of stationary storage deployed statewide as of June 2026. ConnectedSolutions overall spans five states, orchestrating more than 280,000 distributed energy resources and over 800 MW of flexible capacity. (Program launched in late July, at the edge of the daily window — included as the strongest current electrification development.)

Tennis · WTA DC Open

Rain suspends the DC Open final with Pegula ahead of Eala 6–4, 1–2 — the Filipina’s historic title bid to be decided Monday

+

The Mubadala DC Open women’s final was not completed Sunday (Aug. 2): rain and lightning suspended play after about 55 minutes and pushed the finish to Monday, Aug. 3 (resuming around noon ET). When play stopped, American top seed Jessica Pegula led 21-year-old Filipina Alexandra Eala 6–4, 1–2 in the second set, with Pegula to serve on the resumption. Eala — chasing her first career WTA title — reached her maiden WTA 500 final by upsetting No. 3 seed Naomi Osaka 6–4, 6–2 in the semis; Pegula came through past Diana Shnaider 7–5, 6–4 and is seeking a second DC title (after 2019) and her 12th WTA crown overall.

As of this morning’s brief the champion is still to be decided, so no final result is available; a win would make Eala the first Filipina to capture a WTA singles title. Any “final” score circulating before the noon-ET restart should be treated as unconfirmed.
