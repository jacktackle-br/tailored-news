History · Archaeology

Poison on the shore of a vanished lake: stone points from 125,000-year-old Arabia carry traces of arrow-poison plants, possibly the oldest poisoned weapons ever found

+

A study published this week in Science (Clarkson et al.) describes ‘Uruq Aljelibah, a site on the shore of the ancient Mundafan lake in what is now the Rub’ al-Khali desert of southern Saudi Arabia. During a wetter phase about 125,000 years ago, the area was a green lakeshore visited by large animals. The team collected more than 5,500 stone tools and flakes. Several points show impact damage consistent with hunting big game. Chemical residues on some of them point to Acokanthera schimperi, the arrow-poison tree still used by hunters in East Africa, along with other potentially toxic plant compounds. If the reading holds, people were poisoning weapons tens of thousands of years earlier than the best-established evidence for the practice.

Both the authors and outside experts note the caveats. The 125,000-year date comes from the lake and shoreline deposits, not from the artifacts. Because the tools were collected from the surface rather than excavated from dated layers, archaeologist Jeffrey Rose notes that the date is a maximum rather than a precise age. No human remains were found, so linking the points to Homo sapiens moving out of Africa is an inference. The residue evidence is also described as suggestive rather than conclusive. Even so, the find matters for the history of technology. Poison makes a projectile lethal without great force, which implies knowledge of plants and their chemistry and planning over several steps. If confirmed, it would show that kind of knowledge on what was then a crossing point between Africa and Eurasia.

Religion · Social Science

The church stayed home: a new analysis finds turnout in America’s white evangelical heartland fell to two-thirds of 2024 levels, and what remained swung away from the GOP

+

A Washington Post analysis published Nov. 14 examines 343 counties it identifies as white “evangelical hubs,” almost all in the Southeast from Texas to Virginia. In this month’s midterms, turnout in those counties was about 66 percent of its 2024 level, compared with about 78 percent in neighboring counties in the same states, and the vote in those areas shifted noticeably toward Democrats. Dante Chinni of the American Communities Project told the Post that if those counties had turned out at their neighbors’ rate, they would have produced hundreds of thousands more Republican votes. NBC exit polling points the same way: voters identifying as white evangelical or born-again made up 17 percent of the electorate, down from 23 percent in 2024, even though about four in five of them still backed Republicans.

The figures matter because white evangelicals have been the steadiest bloc in American politics for two generations, the only religious group in which a majority of registered voters preferred GOP House candidates in Pew’s October survey. The new data suggest that the change came through abstention rather than conversion, and that difference matters for interpretation. Before the analysis appeared, sociologist of religion Ryan Burge told RNS that this was mostly an inflation election and that the turnout data were still unclear. An RNS column called the result a victory for “Golden Rule Christianity.” Separate Pew–Knight data released this month found American religiosity broadly “stable” after a long decline. Together they raise the question of what political faith looks like when believers stop showing up. (The Post’s full methodology could not be retrieved; figures are from its summary and secondary reports.)

Philosophy · Metaphysics

Why is there anything at all? A philosopher offers what he calls a genuinely new answer to the oldest question in metaphysics, and warns it is neither simple nor easy

+

In an Aeon essay published Nov. 12, Andrew T. Brenner, an associate professor of religion and philosophy at Hong Kong Baptist University and author of Why Is There Something Rather Than Nothing? (Cambridge, 2026), returns to Leibniz’s question. The standfirst says he offers a new answer that is “neither simple nor easy.” The usual candidates, which his book surveys, include a necessary being such as God; the claim that an empty world is incoherent; “axiarchism,” the view that things exist because it is good that they do; laws of nature that make something inevitable; probabilistic arguments that nothingness is just one outcome among infinitely many; and the idea that every possibility is realized.

Brenner’s own line, as previewed in a Cambridge blog post earlier this year, challenges an assumption almost everyone in the debate shares: that “nothing” is the simplest default, which existence then has to beat. He suggests that it may in fact be simpler for there to be something, and that this simplicity could itself help explain why there is something. In a field where most proposals rename the mystery, a claim that turns the burden of proof around is worth reading closely.
