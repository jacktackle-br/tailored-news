Immigration & the Law · New Tonight

DOJ activates the never-used “alien terrorist” deportation court — and the judge sends its first case back, demanding a real legal theory

+

The Justice Department has invoked the Alien Terrorist Removal Court for the first time since Congress created it in 1996 — a secretive five-judge tribunal built for extraordinary deportations that would otherwise expose classified evidence, and which allows the rapid removal of noncitizens deemed “alien terrorists” along with their immediate family members. DOJ filed a sealed application, its cover sheet marked classified, on July 15 to remove an unnamed respondent. At a July 16 hearing at which no sworn testimony was taken, the court’s chief judge, Joan Ericksen — a George W. Bush appointee — found the government’s submission insufficient and ordered it to lay out a fuller explanation of its legal theory and supporting facts, reportedly by July 22.

Legal analysts read the order as an early signal that the long-dormant court will not be a rubber stamp for the administration’s deportation drive. Because the proceedings turn on classified material, the respondent may never see the full case; civil-liberties lawyers have flagged how far the process departs from ordinary immigration hearings. The government has not publicly identified the target or the alleged conduct.

The Left-Leaning Take

The liberal alarm is that the administration has dusted off a Star-Chamber-style tribunal to deport people on secret evidence they cannot see or rebut — and, by statute, to reach their relatives too. On this view, activating a court that lay unused for three decades is less about one dangerous individual than about building a fast, opaque removal machine that sidesteps the due-process protections of the regular immigration system. That the judge balked is offered as proof of the danger, not a reason to relax. (No dedicated in-window Mother Jones / Vox / Slate / Guardian column surfaced — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream-right position is that this is a lawful tool Congress itself created for precisely the hardest national-security removals, and that the judge’s demand for a stronger filing shows the safeguards work as designed. But there is a civil-libertarian dissent of the sort Reason presses: a secret-evidence court that can sweep in family members is exactly the security-state overreach conservatives condemn when a future administration wields it. Label that an intra-right caution, not the White House line. (No signed in-window National Review / Dispatch / WSJ editorial was retrievable — provisional.)

Trade · Developing Since This Morning

As his 10% global tariff nears its Friday expiry, Trump’s team readies a sweeping Section 301 replacement — while Carney brands the new Canada levies a treaty violation

+

U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer signaled Tuesday that the administration will “soon” roll out new tariffs under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, tied to forced-labor findings — duties the office has already proposed on roughly 60 economies (in the 10–12.5% range) covering by Greer’s own estimate about 99% of U.S. trade. The move would rebuild, on sturdier legal ground, the tariff wall the Supreme Court dismantled when it struck down the 2025 “Liberation Day” regime; the flat 10% levy now in place rests on Section 122 of the 1974 act, a stopgap adopted hours after that ruling and set to lapse this week (July 24). Greer said he could not specify timing.

The pivot lands a day after Trump imposed an additional 50% tariff on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods. Prime Minister Mark Carney sharpened his response Tuesday, calling the levies a “direct violation” of the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement, but said that after speaking with Trump the two had agreed to “intensify” trade negotiations — while warning he would keep “all options” open if the tariffs proceed on August 19.

The Left-Leaning Take

The liberal frame is tariff-by-improvisation: an executive that lost its signature trade program in court is now rebuilding the same wall through whatever statute might survive the next lawsuit, keeping businesses and allies in permanent uncertainty and pushing costs onto U.S. consumers already squeezed by the Gulf war. Repackaging blanket protectionism as a forced-labor remedy, on this read, is a legal fig leaf over a policy the courts already rejected once. (No dedicated in-window Mother Jones / Vox / Slate / Guardian column surfaced — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Populist-right voices welcome a tougher, more litigation-proof tariff architecture aimed at forced labor and Canadian “discrimination.” But the free-market and libertarian objection — the one Reason and Cato-aligned writers make — is that laundering sweeping protectionism through Section 301 is cleverer law but the same economic error, and that tariff power belongs to Congress, not a trade representative rewriting the map by proclamation. Treat this as an intra-right free-trade dissent. (No signed in-window National Review / Dispatch / WSJ editorial was retrievable — provisional.)

Press & the Law · New This Week

DOJ sought the phone records of New York Times reporters — and of two spouses and one reporter’s mother — to unmask the sources behind its Air Force One coverage

+

A court motion unsealed Monday, July 20, revealed that the Justice Department sought the phone and text-message records of several New York Times journalists — and of some of their relatives, including two reporters’ spouses and one reporter’s mother — in an effort to identify the confidential sources behind the paper’s reporting on President Trump’s Qatari-gifted replacement Air Force One jet. DOJ served subpoenas to third-party phone providers beginning July 10, the same day three Times reporters were hand-delivered grand-jury subpoenas to testify — one day after the Times published a report detailing shortcomings on the aircraft, including that it lacked defensive countermeasures found on the current presidential fleet. The records demands covered a timeframe wider than the articles themselves.

The Times said the government had acted in “bad faith,” bypassing its own longstanding protocols by failing to give the journalists advance notice and by demanding the material “without first conducting any serious investigation.” Under DOJ’s news-media guidelines, prosecutors are ordinarily required to negotiate with or notify outlets before seizing reporters’ records. The Freedom of the Press Foundation called the effort “gross,” with one advocate saying that in reaching for journalists’ relatives, “even the Mafia says families are off limits.” The department has not publicly cited the statute underpinning the leak inquiry.

The Left-Leaning Take

The liberal read is that an administration that styles itself the champion of free speech is criminalizing journalism: dragging in reporters’ spouses and a reporter’s mother to hunt leakers who embarrassed the president over a foreign government’s gift is textbook intimidation, meant to chill national-security reporting and deter sources far beyond this one story. Science · Paleontology

Bone-stress models suggest some young sauropods could rear up on their hind legs — until sheer size made the pose impossible

+

A new study finds that two elephant-sized South American sauropods, living around 66 million years ago, were unusually well built to rise onto their hind legs and stay there for relatively long stretches — especially when young. Researchers built digital reconstructions of femurs from seven sauropods spanning a range of evolutionary branches, body sizes and skeletal features, then borrowed an engineering technique to estimate how much stress gravity and body weight placed on the thigh bone during a bipedal stance. The two species’ robust femurs handled those forces markedly better than the bones of their larger relatives.

As the animals grew, however, their ballooning weight made the upright posture far harder to sustain — a size ceiling on the behavior. The authors suggest rearing may have helped the dinosaurs reach leaves high in trees, appear more intimidating to predators, or compete for mates, and note the approach could be applied to other extinct giants to test which were physically capable of standing tall.

Sources: ScienceDaily, SciTechDaily

Energy · The Grid

California’s batteries hit a record 12.99-gigawatt discharge as solar tops 70% of the grid, reshaping the summer peak

+

California’s clean-power buildout set back-to-back records in early July. On the evening of July 9, the state’s battery fleet discharged a record 12.99 gigawatts — more than New York City draws on a hot summer day — with batteries covering about 36% of demand across the CAISO grid at peak. A day later, on July 10, solar generation reached 23 GW and briefly supplied 72% of regional electricity demand, the third time since June 1 that California had broken its own solar record.

The pattern shows solar and storage increasingly working as a pair: developers have installed roughly 16 GW of battery capacity in CAISO that charges on cheap midday sun and then competes with gas plants after sundown, flattening the evening ramp that has long defined the state’s grid. Federal data underscored the shift, with the EIA reporting that solar generation surpassed natural gas in CAISO over the first five months of 2026. The milestones are a concrete marker of how fast batteries are moving from grid curiosity to a load-bearing part of the system.
