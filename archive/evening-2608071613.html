Justice Department · Confirmation Endgame

Cassidy’s emotional “yes” clears the path for Blanche as attorney general — even as Murkowski joins Collins in defecting, sending it to a Saturday floor vote

+

The whip count that was open this morning broke tonight. In an emotional floor address Friday afternoon, Sen. Bill Cassidy (R-La.) — the last uncommitted Republican and a critical holdout — announced he will vote to confirm acting Attorney General Todd Blanche, appearing to clear the way for confirmation. Cassidy said Blanche is “not perfect” but that he expects him to run the Justice Department more effectively than a plausible alternative. Earlier Friday, Sen. Lisa Murkowski (R-Alaska) said she would oppose the nomination, joining Sen. Susan Collins (R-Maine), who declared her “no” earlier in the week — leaving two GOP defections that the narrow majority can absorb.

The math now points to confirmation. Majority Leader John Thune has the Senate racing to clear business before the August recess; a cloture vote was teed up for Friday and a final confirmation vote is expected as late as Saturday, Aug. 8. The nomination had been salvaged after Blanche formally rescinded the roughly $1.8 billion DOJ “anti-weaponization fund” on Aug. 2 to placate GOP holdouts, though legal analysts continue to flag that the walk-back may lack durable legal effect and that audit-immunity protections tied to Trump’s IRS settlement remain the unresolved substance. As of this evening the cloture and final tallies had not been recorded.

The Left-Leaning Take

Confirming Trump’s former personal lawyer institutionalizes a weaponized DOJ, the left argues: a Boston Globe editorial calls him “unfit,” citing subpoenas against journalists, and Slate maintains the fund’s rescission is “worse than useless” theater because Blanche cannot unilaterally undo a settlement without Trump’s sign-off. Cassidy’s hand-wringing “yes,” in this read, is exactly the kind of reluctant acquiescence that has let the guardrails fall one senator at a time.

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right treats the fund’s withdrawal as resolving the one legitimate objection: National Review’s Bench Memos frames much of the remaining opposition as a political hit, and House Judiciary Republicans are openly whipping to “Confirm Todd Blanche.” The Dispatch is more ambivalent, casting the fight as a proxy war over DOJ independence. Intra-right dissent (anti-Trump right, Bulwark): don’t trust the rescission — it greases confirmation while the audit-immunity deal survives — labeled anti-Trump right, not the mainstream position.

Sources: Washington Post, NBC News, NPR, CNN (live), Boston Globe (opinion), Slate
Immigration · The 14th Amendment

Five weeks after the Supreme Court struck down his first attempt, Trump signs two narrower executive orders targeting “birth tourism” and the children of “alien enemies”

+

In an Oval Office signing Thursday evening (Aug. 6), President Trump issued two new executive orders seeking to limit birthright citizenship — titled “Continuing to Protect the Meaning and Value of American Citizenship” and “Ending Birth Tourism” — a deliberately narrower approach roughly five weeks after the Supreme Court, on June 30, held that the 14th Amendment guarantees birthright citizenship and struck down his broader 2025 order. The orders would exclude from automatic citizenship children born to members of designated foreign terrorist groups, those deemed “alien enemies,” and children of certain foreign-government employees such as diplomats, while directing agencies to curb commercial “birth tourism.” Trump called the June ruling “very unfortunate” and said his administration was “making adjustments.”

The orders had not been implemented as of publication, and fresh legal challenges are widely expected before either could be enforced (SCOTUSblog, NPR, AP). Legal commentators note the drafting is engineered to slot into the narrow categories the Court left open — children of foreign diplomats and hostile occupiers have long sat outside the “subject to the jurisdiction” clause — setting up a second round of litigation over how far those exceptions stretch.

The Left-Leaning Take

This is an end-run around a Supreme Court that just told the administration no, the left argues: dressing an unconstitutional aim in narrower language doesn’t make it lawful, and “alien enemies” framing invites exactly the kind of open-ended executive discretion the 14th Amendment was written to foreclose. The practical target, critics say, is immigrant families — with the “birth tourism” label doing rhetorical work to normalize stripping citizenship at birth. (Directional synthesis; Vox/Slate/Guardian opinion desks were not cleanly crawlable in-window.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Even sympathetic legal voices are cautious. Reason’s Volokh Conspiracy (“Trump Calls Roberts’s Bluff”) reads the order as a targeted test of the categories the Court expressly did not resolve — children of alien enemies and diplomats — rather than a frontal defiance, while cautioning the “alien enemy” theory is legally aggressive. The restrictionist right cheers the birth-tourism crackdown as a long-overdue closing of a loophole; the libertarian read warns against expanding executive power over who counts as a citizen.

Sources: NPR, ABC News, TIME, SCOTUSblog, Reason (Volokh)

Middle East & South Asia · It’s Signed

Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan sign the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement — an attack on one is now an attack on all three

+

What this morning’s brief tracked as an imminent signing is now done. On Friday, Aug. 7, Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan and Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif signed the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement, a trilateral collective-defence pact under which an armed attack on any one of the three is to be treated as an attack on all three. Turkish reporting indicates the framework covers joint exercises and training, technology transfers and intelligence sharing; analysts flag that it creates a formal channel for Turkey’s Baykar to deepen drone co-production inside Saudi Arabia, advancing Riyadh’s push to build rather than merely buy weapons and pare its decades-long dependence on U.S. arms. The pact extends to NATO member Turkey the mutual-defence commitment Saudi Arabia and Pakistan concluded bilaterally in September 2025.

The most-watched question — whether nuclear-armed Pakistan’s arsenal now implicitly shelters the Gulf — got a partial answer. Analysts note that if Islamabad’s umbrella did extend to Riyadh, it would be the first time a Gulf state enjoyed a nuclear deterrent not supplied by Washington; but Pakistani Defence Minister Khawaja Asif told Reuters that nuclear weapons were “not on the radar” of the agreement. Signed in Islam’s holiest city for maximum symbolic weight and against the backdrop of the U.S.–Israel war on Iran, the pact knits Sunni-majority powers into a bloc spanning the Middle East and South Asia — a hedge, as Gulf states read it, against wavering American security guarantees.

Sources: Al Jazeera, CBS News, Breaking Defense, Reuters (via US News), Reuters (nuclear analysis)

Iran War · Pentagon & Munitions

Trump denies U.S. missile stockpiles are depleted and vows to hunt “leakers” — after reports the Iran war burned through key munitions and a Camp David clash with Hegseth

+

Multiple Tier-1 outlets reported this week that the roughly six-week U.S. bombing campaign against Iran has sharply drawn down American precision-munition and air-defense stocks. Per Washington Post and NPR reporting (Aug. 4–5), the U.S. has expended more than half its pre-war inventory of four of the seven weapons the Pentagon designates “key munitions”; reporting put the Patriot interceptor inventory at an estimated 700–850, down from roughly 2,400 before the war, and (per CNN) THAAD depletion near 80%. Offensive stocks of ATACMS and Precision Strike Missiles are described as nearly exhausted, with replenishment estimated to take years — into the 2030s for some systems. WaPo reported that Trump’s frustration “boiled over” at Camp David, where he pressed Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth on why he had apparently been misled about the shortfalls.

On Aug. 6, Trump publicly rejected the reporting, posting that U.S. stockpiles at the “medium and upper medium grade” have “never been higher or better” and that the country has a “virtually unlimited supply.” He said “leakers” were “being hunted down” and that “long-term jail sentences will be sought,” while defending Hegseth. The dispute carries a partisan overlay: Hegseth accused Sen. Mark Kelly (D-Ariz.) of having “blabbed” about a classified briefing, while Kelly and other Democrats counter that munitions expended in an openly acknowledged war are not classified. The Pentagon has announced expanded THAAD and PAC-3 interceptor production deals with Lockheed Martin and Northrop Grumman. Flag the factual conflict: Trump’s “never higher” claim is directly contradicted by the Tier-1 sourcing, and no straight-news desk corroborates the president’s characterization.

The Left-Leaning Take

The stockpile crisis is the predictable cost of a strategically incoherent bombing campaign the administration boasted was “not a fair fight”: Trump prizes the appearance of strength over the real thing, and firing off years of irreplaceable interceptors has left the U.S. weaker toward the Indo-Pacific and its allies — the opposite of what it advertises. (Directional synthesis from Slate’s Iran-war coverage; Mother Jones/Vox/Guardian opinion were not crawlable in-window.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The hawkish right treats the depletion as real and alarming, not a hoax: The Dispatch (Mackenzie Eaglen) argues precision munitions underpin deterrence and the shortage demands an urgent industrial-base surge, while Reason warns the U.S. burned exquisite, slow-to-replace weapons intercepting cheap drones — and, in the libertarian read, that the depletion is itself evidence the war was a mistake. Intra-right dissent (anti-Trump right, Bulwark): retired Gen. Mark Hertling argues the bombing pause is best explained by interceptor scarcity, not victory — labeled as anti-Trump right, not the mainstream-right position.

Sources: Washington Post, Bloomberg, NPR, CNN, The Dispatch, The Bulwark
Iran War · Strait of Hormuz

Iran says a Hormuz shipping deal with Oman is “agreed in principle” — but the framework would bar U.S. and Israeli ships and stop short of the full reopening Trump has demanded

+

On Aug. 6, Iranian officials said a framework with Oman to manage traffic through the Strait of Hormuz — the passage for roughly a fifth to a quarter of the world’s seaborne oil — had been reached “in principle” (Bloomberg, WaPo). Inbound vessels would use a northern corridor near the Iranian coast and outbound vessels a southern corridor along Oman’s, an arrangement critics say hands Tehran and Muscat joint control of the chokepoint, which has been effectively closed since fighting between the U.S. and Iran resumed. Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi cautioned the deal would not automatically reopen the strait and that Washington must meet further conditions first; Bloomberg reported the terms would bar U.S.- and Israeli-linked ships and could require compensation from “hostile” states before free transit resumes.

The framework is tied to reviving the collapsed U.S.–Iran ceasefire. Trump has insisted publicly on the “Immediate, Complete, and Total OPENING” of the strait and said Aug. 6 the war would end “pretty soon.” A CNN analysis called the emerging deal “taking shape — but not one Trump wants,” because even a return to the prewar status quo would read regionally as a concession Iran granted rather than a U.S. victory. Flag: this is an Iran–Oman framework with U.S. conditions still outstanding, not a signed U.S.–Iran deal, and Iran maintains its only direct talks are with Oman. Trump has teased an imminent agreement repeatedly, so the “close” framing carries a track record of slippage.

The Left-Leaning Take

Slate has argued Hormuz is “the only thing that really matters” to Trump in this war, and that his maximalist threats keep collapsing into face-saving climbdowns; from that vantage a framework leaving Tehran with a veto over the strait is a strategic defeat dressed up as a win, with American consumers still paying at the pump. (Directional synthesis from Slate’s Hormuz coverage.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Reason (“Trump backs down from yet another empty threat to Iran”) casts the arc as bluster followed by retreat that mostly restores the prewar status quo at great cost, and The Dispatch’s fact-check disputes the administration’s framing of who reopened the strait and on what terms. Hawkish outlets warn that any arrangement codifying Iranian/IRGC control of transit is a durable strategic loss, however it is packaged.

Justice Department · Attorney General

Bill Cassidy (R-La.) said he had not decided, wanting assurance Blanche won’t simply act as Trump’s lawyer. With a narrow GOP majority the whip count is tight but leadership expects confirmation. Legal analysts add that the rescission may lack durable legal effect and that residual audit-immunity protections for the Trump family are the real remaining story.

+

A key caveat flagged by Reuters and Euronews is that there was no immediate clarity on each state’s specific commitments — leaving open how far the mutual-defense language extends in practice, and whether Pakistan’s nuclear umbrella is implicitly on offer.

Europe · Russia’s War on Ukraine

A deadly ballistic barrage on Kyiv exposes Ukraine’s interceptor gap — as European allies and Washington both decline to hand over more Patriots

+

A Russian overnight assault on Kyiv and the surrounding region (into Wednesday, Aug. 5) killed at least 17 people and wounded 44, President Volodymyr Zelensky said. Russia fired roughly 24 ballistic missiles, several identified as hypersonic Zircons or supersonic Oniks, and about 115 drones (many jet-powered); Ukraine intercepted none of the ballistic missiles — the one threat only the U.S.-made Patriot can reliably stop. Targets were largely civilian: warehouses, a brewing company, building-materials depots, logistics facilities and a railway station. “Ballistic interceptors are what could have saved the lives of those killed today,” Zelensky wrote.

The follow-on driving Friday’s coverage is a Washington Post report that several European governments — Ukraine’s principal military backers — have refused to give up Patriot interceptors, fearing depletion of their own stockpiles; a former senior Ukrainian official warned “there are no interceptors right now” and forecast a “worst-case scenario” this winter. Trump has separately confirmed the U.S. will not transfer its own Patriot interceptors, citing American needs — a striking echo of the domestic stockpile fight in the Politics section. The through-line is a hardening supply crisis: escalating Russian ballistic salvos plus a drying interceptor pipeline from both Washington and European capitals point to a materially more dangerous autumn and winter for Ukrainian cities.

Sources: Washington Post, CNN, NPR, Novaya Gazeta Europe

Energy · Grid & Storage

DOE closes a $489M loan to add 220 MW of battery storage to Puerto Rico’s grid — with the original solar component stripped out

+

The U.S. Department of Energy’s Office of Energy Dominance Financing (the current administration’s rebranding of the Loan Programs Office) closed a $489.4 million loan (Aug. 5–6) to Amanecer Puerto Rico LLC, a subsidiary of Pattern Energy, funding 220 MW of battery energy storage in Arecibo and Santa Isabel using U.S.-manufactured batteries and capable of backing up more than 100,000 customers during shortages. DOE projects the systems will cut electricity costs by an estimated $312.5 million over 25 years (an agency-stated projection).

The notable change is what got cut: the deal descends from an earlier conditional commitment to Pattern Energy that included both solar PV and storage; the finalized loan is storage-only, with the solar component dropped — a shift several outlets tied to the administration’s move away from the Biden-era commitment. Puerto Rico’s grid has struggled with chronic outages since Hurricane Maria, making fast-dispatch storage a hedge against the island’s recurring blackouts.

Science · Paleobiology & Evolution

567-million-year-old fossils from Canada’s Mackenzie Mountains push back the origins of animal movement and sexual reproduction

+

A study published in Science Advances (peer-reviewed), covered Aug. 4–5, describes an unusually rich Ediacaran fossil site in a remote part of Canada’s Northwest Territories (the Mackenzie Mountains). Researchers led by teams at the American Museum of Natural History and Dartmouth catalogued more than 100 fossils, including six taxa never before recorded in North America. Dated to about 567 million years, the assemblage suggests animal locomotion and sexual reproduction began roughly 5–10 million years earlier than the previously documented “White Sea” assemblage known mainly from Europe, Asia and Australia.

Named organisms include Funisia, a tube-shaped colonial creature offering the oldest known fossil evidence of sexual reproduction; Dickinsonia, a flat-bodied mobile grazer; and Kimberella, a muscular-footed animal widely interpreted as an early mollusk relative — together sketching a fuller picture of the “Ediacaran biota” that preceded the Cambrian explosion.

Sources: ScienceDaily, SciTechDaily, Earth.com, The Debrief
Space · Cosmology

A JWST survey of ~1,400 early galaxies finds a “leaky” minority did most of the work of reionizing the universe

+

New JWST/NIRSpec analysis circulating Aug. 5–6 compiled spectra from 1,428 galaxies at redshift 5–10 (roughly 500 million to 1 billion years after the Big Bang) and measured how many ionizing photons each produces and how many escape into the intergalactic medium. The galaxies split into two populations: about 20% are “strong leakers,” yet that minority accounts for roughly 87% of the ionizing photons reaching the intergalactic medium — implying cosmic reionization was driven by a small, highly efficient subset of galaxies rather than spread evenly across the early galaxy population.

Caveat: the result is a preprint, posted to arXiv on July 24, 2026, and not yet peer-reviewed; the Aug. 5–6 write-ups (phys.org, Astrobites) cover that preprint. If it holds, it sharpens a central question in early-universe cosmology — which sources cleared the primordial fog — by pointing to a handful of prolific emitters rather than the galaxy population at large.

Sources: Phys.org, Astrobites
