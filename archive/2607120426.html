The Iran War · Escalation

U.S. strikes about 140 Iranian targets after Tehran attacks a Hormuz-bound ship and closes the strait; Trump calls the June ceasefire “over”

+

The U.S.–Iran ceasefire signed in mid-June has fully collapsed. President Trump declared the truce “over” on July 8 at the NATO summit in Turkey, and over July 11–12 U.S. Central Command carried out successive waves of strikes — the latest round reported at roughly 140 targets (an earlier wave was put near 90), hitting missile and drone sites, naval assets, ammunition storage, communications and coastal-surveillance sites, with explosions reported at Bushehr, Bandar Abbas, Sirik and Assalouyeh. Washington framed the operation as a response to IRGC attacks on commercial shipping: the Cyprus-flagged container vessel GFS Galaxy suffered a serious engine-room fire, its crew abandoned ship, and one crew member is missing. Iran declared the Strait of Hormuz closed “until further notice” and retaliated with missiles and drones against U.S. military sites in the Gulf — interceptions and alerts were reported over Qatar (three people, including a child, wounded by falling shrapnel in Doha), Bahrain (home to the U.S. Fifth Fleet), the UAE and Kuwait.

Shipping through Hormuz has ground to a near-halt, with the UN maritime agency saying roughly 6,000 seafarers are stranded in the area. Outlets diverged on oil prices — some put Brent near $76 and roughly flat, others near $79 (up from about $72 earlier in the week) with WTI around $75; treat the exact figures as unsettled. On the domestic-law side, both chambers of Congress had in late June passed a War Powers resolution directing the removal of U.S. forces (House 215–208 on June 3; Senate 50–48, with Republicans Cassidy, Collins, Paul and Murkowski crossing over), widely described as symbolic and without force of law. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer said Trump had delivered “maximum confusion, maximum chaos, maximum cost.”

No dedicated in-window opinion column from Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or the Guardian on this exact escalation surfaced (the research crawler was blocked from those domains), so this is standing analytical framing rather than a cited column: the anti-war and constitutional left casts the strikes as an open-ended, unauthorized Middle East war — the very outcome Congress voted to halt — and warns of mission creep toward regime change with no congressional authorization and mounting civilian and economic costs, echoing Schumer’s “maximum chaos.” No column is invented here.

The Right-Leaning Take

The hawkish mainstream faults Trump less for fighting than for weakness: National Review (“Iran Gets Back on Trump’s Bad Side”) argues Tehran reignited the war, and the WSJ editorial board is reported to have rebuked Trump that his restrained June retaliation failed to deter and let Iran “win the battle for Hormuz.” A distinct libertarian/restraint strand runs the other way — Reason has argued for months that Trump “never made the case for war with Iran to the public” — an intra-right split, not the hawkish consensus.

In “How Trump Left Himself With No Good Solutions on Iran,” Goldberg argues Trump has boxed himself in over the Strait of Hormuz, leaving only bad options after building a ceasefire on unrealistic expectations.

U.S. Senate · Sudden Loss

Sen. Lindsey Graham, four-term South Carolina Republican and leading foreign-policy hawk, dies at 71 after a “brief and sudden illness”

+

Sen. Lindsey Graham (R-S.C.) died Saturday night, July 11, at age 71, his office announced July 12, attributing it to “a brief and sudden illness” without further detail; per CNN, responders were dispatched to a Washington address around 8:30 p.m. for a report of chest pains. Graham had recently returned from Ukraine, was scheduled to appear on NBC’s “Meet the Press” on Sunday, and on Friday had announced agreement with the administration to advance a Russia sanctions package. First elected to the Senate in 2002 after House service, he won reelection three times (most recently 2020), chaired the Budget Committee, and sat on Judiciary, Appropriations, and Environment and Public Works. He was among the Senate’s most prominent foreign-policy hawks — a leading advocate for Israel and Ukraine aid and an aggressive Iran posture — and his arc from 2016 Trump critic to close ally became part of his political identity.

Trump called him “one of the greatest people and Senators I have ever known” on Truth Social; Gov. Henry McMaster called him “irreplaceable”; Sen. Tim Scott mourned “a statesman.” Reports agree Republican Gov. McMaster will name an interim replacement, but framing varies on term length: the seat was already up in the 2026 cycle, so the permanent successor is set to be chosen at the November 3, 2026 election, though one account phrased the appointee as serving “until January.”

The critical-left assessment emphasizes a legacy of hawkish interventionism — regime-change advocacy across Iran, Iraq, Syria and Libya — and what detractors call political opportunism, citing his transformation from calling Trump unfit in 2016 to becoming one of his most loyal defenders.

The Right-Leaning Take

No in-window National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason column on Graham’s death was found — the news broke only hours before this brief — so this is standing framing plus on-record eulogies, none invented. The right is mourning Graham as a patriot and steadfast defender of a muscular American foreign policy, strong national defense and support for Israel and Ukraine, reflected in tributes from Trump, McMaster and Scott. A libertarian/restraint minority on the right has long criticized his interventionism, but no such in-window column surfaced.

Press Freedom · DOJ

Justice Department subpoenas four New York Times reporters over Air Force One security stories, ordering grand-jury testimony in a leak probe

+

The DOJ subpoenaed four New York Times journalists — Julian E. Barnes, Eric Lipton, Tyler Pager and Eric Schmitt — with federal agents delivering the subpoenas at their homes Friday evening. Issued by U.S. Attorney Jay Clayton of the Southern District of New York (whom Trump recently nominated as director of national intelligence), the subpoenas compel testimony before a Manhattan federal grand jury Wednesday “in regard to an alleged violation of federal criminal law.” They stem from a Wednesday Times article reporting that the Secret Service urged Trump to leave the NATO summit in Turkey aboard an older Air Force One rather than the Qatari-donated Boeing 747 over security concerns, plus a follow-up that the gifted plane lacked defensive countermeasures, including advanced antimissile capabilities.

Times deputy general counsel David McCraw said agents appearing at reporters’ doorsteps “should shock the conscience of any American who believes in the Constitution and the press freedom it protects.” The Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press and the Committee to Protect Journalists condemned the move as a break from longstanding DOJ practice of seeking reporter testimony only as a last resort. As Lawfare’s coverage details, AG Pam Bondi’s April 2025 memo rescinded Biden-era protections and loosened media-subpoena guidelines; earlier in 2026 the DOJ issued and then withdrew subpoenas to Washington Post and Wall Street Journal reporters, and the FBI raided the home of Post reporter Hannah Natanson in January. As of this window the Times subpoenas remain in force and have not been rescinded.

The Left-Leaning Take

No dedicated in-window column from Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or the Guardian specifically on the Times subpoenas was retrievable, so this is standing framing, not an invented column: the left reads the move as an authoritarian escalation — the government treating journalists as criminal suspects to punish coverage it dislikes, with an intended chilling effect on sources — and a fresh argument for enacting the PRESS Act to bar federal compulsion of journalists’ testimony and source material.

The Right-Leaning Take

The right is split. The pro-Trump right frames it as a legitimate grand-jury investigation into a genuine leak of sensitive national-security information about presidential-aircraft defenses, consistent with Bondi’s rescission of Biden-era limits. But an institutionalist/libertarian strand runs against the administration: Reason rejects criminalizing journalism (“it’s not journalists’ job to protect government secrets”) and condemned the January raid on a Post reporter, while National Review’s own reporting has been critical of the DOJ’s press posture — an intra-right disagreement, not a unified line.

Sources: PBS NewsHour, CBS News, CNN, CPJ, Lawfare

Ukraine · Russian Barrage

Russian overnight barrage kills at least six across Ukraine; glide bombs hit a crowded Sumy bus stop as Kyiv pleads for air-defense resupply

+

Russia struck Ukraine overnight into Saturday, July 11, with what the air force counted as six ballistic missiles, six cruise missiles and 121 drones, killing at least six people and wounding dozens. The deadliest single strike hit the northern city of Sumy, where two glide bombs struck a crowded area including a bus stop — four killed and 17 wounded, with images showing a gutted bus. A missile strike on the southern port of Odesa killed two more, a drone wounded seven at a civilian enterprise in Kharkiv, and 11 were wounded in Kyiv. President Zelensky pressed for faster Western weapons deliveries as Ukraine faces a shortage of interceptors — a gap analysts tie partly to the Middle East war straining global Patriot supply. Ukrainian forces, meanwhile, continued long-range strikes on Russian oil tankers and vessels in the Sea of Azov.

The nationwide death toll was reported inconsistently across the window: Reuters and Al Jazeera cited six killed on July 11, an earlier Reuters dispatch dated July 10 cited eight, and the Kyiv Independent reported “at least 10” killed and 80-plus injured. The variance reflects rolling updates and different counting windows rather than a direct contradiction.

Sources: Al Jazeera, Reuters (via US News), Kyiv Independent, ISW (via Kyiv Post)
Venezuela · Quake Aftermath

Venezuela’s twin-earthquake death toll climbs past 4,100 with tens of thousands displaced as aftershocks hamper the search near Caracas

+

Nearly three weeks after the June 24 twin earthquakes (magnitudes 7.2 and 7.5) struck in and around Caracas and coastal La Guaira, the confirmed death toll continued its steep climb through the July 11–12 window, passing roughly 4,100–4,300 by the weekend (up from about 3,535 on July 7), with some 16,700 injured and about 17,800 left without housing. More than 12,800 people were sheltering across roughly 80 sites in Caracas and La Guaira. Rescue and recovery work continued amid ongoing aftershocks — over 860 recorded, including a 4.6-magnitude event off the Caribbean coast.

Exact figures differ by the hour and source: the Guardian reported the toll “tops 4,300” on July 11 while ABC News reported it topping 4,100, and a figure of 4,333 also circulated. The USGS has cautioned the final toll could ultimately land anywhere from 10,000 to 100,000 given the scale of destruction, and estimates of the number still unaccounted for (one figure cited around 50,000) are highly uncertain and should be treated as provisional.

Sources: ABC News, Al Jazeera, NBC News

Tech · Robotics

Surgeons pilot humanoid robots to remove gallbladders from live pigs, a world first published in Nature

+

A University of California San Diego team led by engineer Michael Yip used two teleoperated humanoid robots to perform complete minimally invasive gallbladder removals on live pigs — in what the researchers describe as the first surgery on living animals by humanoid robots. The work was published in Nature and reported widely on July 9–10. The robots were built on off-the-shelf Unitree G1 platforms (about five feet tall, ~60 lbs) fitted with custom adapters for laparoscopic tools; the machines were not autonomous, as human surgeons drove every movement from a nearby console.

Two operations succeeded: one pairing a humanoid with a human-surgeon assistant, the other run entirely by two humanoids working together. The researchers framed the appeal as cost — a G1 with dexterous hands runs in the tens of thousands of dollars, versus roughly half a million to several million for dedicated systems like Intuitive Surgical’s da Vinci — potentially extending robot-assisted, remotely operated surgery to smaller or under-resourced hospitals. The results are preclinical and not yet cleared for human patients.

Sources: UC San Diego Today, Popular Science, Forbes
Energy · The Grid

Clean energy carries the U.S. grid through an early-July heat wave, with Texas hitting a record 83 GW and solar covering over 30%

+

A punishing heat wave in early July pushed U.S. power demand toward records, and newly added solar, wind and battery storage were central to keeping the lights on, according to grid coverage from Canary Media. Texas set a record for peak demand in any July — about 83 gigawatts on the evening of July 6 — with solar supplying more than 30% of that load and helping the grid operator avoid conservation appeals. The PJM system serving the mid-Atlantic saw demand hit near-record levels, with solar output running consistently high through the peak days.

Regional detail underscored the electrification story: in New England, recently commissioned offshore wind and the New England Clean Energy Connect line (carrying Canadian hydropower) cut oil burn versus a comparable 2025 heat wave, while wind was a major contributor in the central Southwest Power Pool. More than 16 GW of new battery capacity added since last summer helped shift supply into the peak evening hours.

Sources: Canary Media, Utility Dive, Marketplace
Climate · Extreme Heat

Europe hits its third heat wave in six weeks as a heat dome reloads over Iberia and France; Barcelona logs its hottest day in 112 years

+

As of July 11, Western Europe was enduring its third major heat wave in roughly six weeks, with a heat dome parked over the Iberian Peninsula and France; forecasters projected highs in the low-to-mid 40s°C in Spain and Portugal and up to about 45°C (113°F) in central and southwestern France. Barcelona recorded its hottest temperature in 112 years around July 8–9, and destructive wildfires were burning from Portugal through Spain into southern France. The EU’s Copernicus service, whose data (reported July 9) showed June 2026 broke heat and ocean-temperature records, characterized the rapid succession of waves as a hallmark of a warming climate.

On attribution, a World Weather Attribution rapid study released June 26 concluded the late-June European heat would have been far less intense — roughly 6.3°F (about 3.5°C) cooler — without human-caused climate change, and that heat at this level is now tens to hundreds of times more likely than in earlier decades and was effectively unheard of 50 years ago. (WWA studies use peer-reviewed methods but are not themselves peer-reviewed.)

Sources: Euronews, Carbon Brief, World Weather Attribution
Space · Astronomy

ESA’s Euclid telescope finds 31 ancient quasars, including the two most distant ever seen, from when the universe was ~670 million years old

+

The European Space Agency’s Euclid mission reported, around July 6, the discovery of 31 of the oldest known quasars — extremely luminous, actively feeding supermassive black holes — including the two highest-redshift quasars ever detected, at redshifts of about 7.77 and 7.69. That places them in the universe’s first ~670 million years (roughly 5% of its current age), surpassing the prior record redshift of 7.64 set in 2021. The finding was published in Astronomy & Astrophysics.

The result matters because such massive black holes existing so early sharpens a standing puzzle: how black holes grew to enormous masses so soon after the Big Bang. Euclid’s wide-field survey also revealed a larger-than-expected hidden population of early quasars, expanding the census of the first generation of supermassive black holes and their host galaxies. (This broke about six days ago, at the edge of the window, but is the strongest genuine discovery in the period.)

Sources: ESA, NASA Science, Phys.org
