Iran War · The Bill Comes Due

The Pentagon puts the Iran war at $33.4 billion — and Tehran throws Trump’s talk of talks back in his face

+

The government released its first detailed cost tally of the war on Tuesday, Sept. 15. The Pentagon put the military’s spending at well over $33.4 billion — about $22.3 billion in expended munitions, $3.7 billion in lost equipment and roughly $7.4 billion in incremental day-to-day operating costs — and that figure excludes hundreds of millions more spent by other agencies. Officials said Iranian strikes have damaged or destroyed “hundreds of buildings and structures” at US bases across the Middle East and “dozens” of US aircraft, and flagged emerging munitions shortfalls. The nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office estimated the war could run roughly $2 billion more per month for as long as it continues; it began Feb. 28, 2026.

On the diplomacy, President Trump spent Sept. 14–15 signaling he was open to the “concept” of talks to end the war. A senior Iranian official rejected what he called Trump’s “mixed signals,” insisting “the stakes have changed” and ruling out any dialogue “until Iran’s conditions are met” — hardening the rebuff Tehran issued this morning through security-council secretary Ali Larijani. (Cost figures are the Defense Department’s own tally and remain provisional; agency-by-agency totals are incomplete.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads the tally as the receipt on a war of choice: $33.4 billion and climbing at some $2 billion a month, dozens of US aircraft lost, munitions running short — all with no congressional authorization and a majority of Americans opposed. Trump’s “openness” to talks, in this frame, is hollow public relations while the bombing and the blockade continue and Tehran, predictably, refuses to negotiate under fire. (Directional; opinion desks not individually reachable this cycle.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The hawkish directional right (National Review, WSJ editorial) casts the cost as the price of restoring deterrence and freedom of navigation, and pins the stalemate on Tehran for closing Hormuz and spurning Trump’s overture. The realist and libertarian right (Reason, The American Conservative) reads the same numbers the opposite way — a $2-billion-a-month money pit with no exit, vindicating warnings that an unauthorized war would become exactly this. A live intra-right split. (Directional.)

Sources: CNBC, CBS News, The Hill
Justice Dept. · ‘Grand Conspiracy’ Fallout

A day after quitting, diGenova walks back his ‘no evidence’ bombshell — as the year-old probe still has zero indictments

+

The turmoil around the DOJ’s “grand conspiracy” probe deepened after Joe diGenova’s abrupt resignation. His widely quoted line — “if you want indictments where there’s no evidence, you have an ethical problem” — drew immediate attention as a signal the case was a bust. He has since told the Associated Press the remark was “taken out of context,” insisting: “There’s plenty of evidence in all of these cases to prove the theories of prosecution. It just takes time to get there, and some people want to get there a little faster than others.” The Justice Department thanked him for his “many years of service,” noting his five months on the probe; reporting attributed his exit to frustration from Trump and DOJ leadership over the pace of the investigation.

The underlying record is unchanged: after roughly a year and dozens of subpoenas, the investigation — run out of the Southern District of Florida (Fort Pierce, Judge Aileen Cannon’s district) — has produced no criminal prosecutions. It now passes to US Attorney Jason Reding Quiñones. Former CIA director John Brennan remains subpoenaed to testify Oct. 15; earlier subpoenas issued last spring were withdrawn in favor of voluntary interviews, and an earlier attempt to prosecute former FBI director James Comey was dismissed after a court found the assigned prosecutor unlawfully appointed. (Case posture is provisional; quotes from wire and legal-press reporting.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left says the overnight walk-back proves the point: a prosecutor said the quiet part — no evidence — then recanted a day after quitting under White House and DOJ pressure to move faster. A year, dozens of subpoenas and zero indictments, the frame runs, is not a case; it is a political vendetta in search of a crime, now being pushed to produce charges on a schedule set by the president’s grievances. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The MAGA-aligned right treats the delay as bureaucratic foot-dragging — Brennan and Comey must finally answer for Russiagate, and diGenova’s “plenty of evidence, it just takes time” is the honest read. But the anti-Trump right (The Bulwark — a dissent, not the mainstream view) and much of the institutionalist right (The Dispatch, likely WSJ editorial caution) counter that a probe which cannot indict in a year, and whose own lead quit citing pressure over pace, is precisely the abuse of prosecutorial power conservatives should refuse to cheer. (Directional.)

Sources: NPR / AP, ABC News, CBS News, CNN

Baltic Sea · NATO–Russia Friction

A Russian frigate fires flares at a Danish navy helicopter — hours after NATO jets down a drone over Lithuania

+

A Russian frigate fired two flares toward a Danish Fennec military helicopter on Monday as the aircraft conducted a routine photographic run of the warship in international waters off Gedser, in the Baltic. Danish officials said no warning or radio contact preceded the firing, which came within “a matter of meters” of the helicopter. Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen called it “completely unacceptable” and said it could have endangered lives; Copenhagen summoned the Russian side, and the government condemned the act as “reckless.” Moscow accused Denmark of “dangerous maneuvers” near its ship and said it was investigating.

The incident landed inside the same 24 hours in which NATO fighter jets shot down a drone over Lithuania that had crossed from Belarus — the first such interception over Lithuanian soil. NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte said strikes near alliance territory would only drive NATO to increase its support for Ukraine, and described the bloc as “committed and ready”; Lithuanian President Gitanas Nauseėda called such readiness “vital.” Taken together — a naval provocation in the west, an air incursion in the east — the two episodes underline how far Russia’s gray-zone pressure now reaches along NATO’s northern and eastern frontier, and how quickly a miscalculation could escalate. (Details rest partly on Danish and Lithuanian government accounts; Moscow’s version differs.)

Sources: Defense News, RTÉ, US News / Reuters, NPR

Strait of Hormuz · Maritime War

Supertankers move both ways through Hormuz again — a fragile recovery even as the naval war grinds on

+

Maritime-intelligence firm TankerTrackers.com, in an update posted Tuesday, Sept. 15 (from satellite imagery through Sept. 14), said very large crude carriers are again transiting the Strait of Hormuz in both directions and during daylight — one of the most operationally significant signals since the war began Feb. 28, read as commercial operators judging the risk “sufficiently managed” to resume normal runs. Ship-to-ship transfers of crude, LNG and LPG in the Gulf of Oman have also picked up. But overall Gulf oil and gas flows remain far below normal — a “fragile recovery,” not a reopening.

The security backdrop is still hot. Iran’s Gulf Strait Authority on Sept. 14 issued an updated list of 77 vessels it accuses of violating Iranian protocols in the strait, and a commercial ship was struck by an unidentified projectile while transiting over the weekend. Washington’s standing policy of striking Iranian tankers in retaliation for fire near its warships — roughly 10 hit in the past week, including five destroyed on Sept. 9 — and Tehran’s restrictions and the US naval blockade all remain in force. The clashing US–Iran accounts of the burning tanker El Gaia are still unresolved. (Traffic figures are the tracking firm’s; attribution of the weekend strike is not independently confirmed.)

Sources: Gulf News, Kurdistan24, NPR, Al Jazeera

War Powers · Iran & Oil

Oil tops $100 as the Iran war reaches American pumps — and Trump calls it ‘a very small price to pay’

+

The US–Iran conflict sharply re-escalated over the Sept. 12–14 weekend. Saudi Arabia’s roughly 745-mile east–west pipeline — the route that lets Saudi crude reach the Red Sea while bypassing the Strait of Hormuz — went offline after a weekend attack, with officials reportedly holding only about a week of stored crude at the Red Sea port; Iran-backed Houthis have expanded their grip on the Bab el-Mandeb. On Sept. 14, Iran said it destroyed a US drone over Hormuz and vowed not to fully reopen the strait until Washington lifts its maritime blockade. Crude passed $100 a barrel for the first time in roughly three-and-a-half years (US WTI reported near $104), feeding an equity selloff and briefly pushing the 10-year Treasury yield above 5%.

President Trump on Sept. 14 claimed the strait “is open,” called the price spike “a very small price to pay for safety and peace,” and predicted prices would “drop precipitously” once the war is won; he has again floated staying in Iran to “keep the oil” and earlier ordered some 172 million barrels released from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. A Marist poll cited in coverage found 56% of Americans oppose US military action in Iran. Congress’s war-powers rebukes have so far been largely symbolic (a 50–48 Senate vote in June, with four Republicans in favor), and a further concurrent resolution is set to be discharged from the Senate Foreign Relations Committee around Oct. 2. (Fast-moving figures — exact oil price, pipeline specifics — are approximate as of Sept. 14 and vary by source.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left frames this as Trump’s avoidable “forever war” — a conflict he escalated now hitting Americans directly at the pump while he waves the pain away as a “small price” and muses openly about seizing another country’s oil. The emphasis: no congressional authorization, war-powers rebukes ignored, a majority opposed, and an economy rattled — with the “keep the oil” talk cast as lawless and imperial. (Directional; opinion desks not individually reachable this cycle.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The hawkish directional right (National Review, WSJ editorial) backs confronting Iran over Hormuz as a defense of freedom of navigation and deterrence, blaming Tehran and the Houthis for the oil shock and urging resolve over retreat. The realist and libertarian right (Reason; The American Conservative) breaks the other way — a costly, open-ended, unauthorized war, with “stay and keep the oil” derided as exactly the nation-building overreach the movement claims to oppose. A genuine intra-right fault line. (Directional.)

Sources: CNBC, Washington Post, Al Jazeera, The Hill
Justice Dept. · The ‘Grand Conspiracy’

The DOJ subpoenas ex-CIA chief John Brennan the same day the prosecutor running the ‘grand conspiracy’ probe abruptly quits

+

On Monday, Sept. 14, a lawyer for former CIA Director John Brennan disclosed in a court filing that prosecutors had served a grand-jury subpoena on Sept. 11 requiring Brennan to testify Oct. 15 before a federal grand jury in Fort Pierce, Florida (the Southern District associated with Judge Aileen Cannon). It is part of the so-called “grand conspiracy” investigation — a theory that officials who investigated Trump over the 2016 Russia inquiry and the 2022 Mar-a-Lago search conspired to deprive him of his civil rights. Former FBI Director James Comey, already separately indicted, has also been drawn into the matter.

The subpoena was signed by Joe diGenova, a Trump ally counseling Attorney General Todd Blanche and leading the probe — who resigned the same day (with his wife, attorney Victoria Toensing) amid reported clashes with DOJ leadership; CNN described a “tense phone call” beforehand. diGenova told the New York Post: “If you want indictments where there’s no evidence, you have an ethical problem.” The probe now falls to Jason Reding Quiñones, US Attorney for the Southern District of Florida. Brennan’s team is seeking any communications showing the White House directed the case, to support a vindictive- or selective-prosecution defense; an earlier DOJ attempt to prosecute Comey was dismissed after a court found the assigned prosecutor unlawfully appointed. (Facts from wire and legal-press reporting; case posture is provisional.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left casts this as the clearest evidence yet of a weaponized DOJ running a presidential “vengeance tour” — subpoenaing the very officials who once investigated Trump, on a civil-rights-conspiracy theory critics call legally incoherent, with the lead prosecutor quitting rather than bring “indictments where there’s no evidence.” The frame: politicized prosecution, the collapse of DOJ independence, and a chilling message to anyone who crosses the president. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The MAGA-aligned directional right frames it as overdue accountability: Brennan and Comey should finally answer for what it calls the original weaponization, Russiagate. But the anti-Trump right (The Bulwark — a dissent, not the mainstream view) and much of the institutionalist right (The Dispatch, and a likely WSJ editorial caution) read diGenova’s exit and his “no evidence” line as an admission the case is a politically driven bust — an abuse of prosecutorial power conservatives should oppose on principle. (Directional.)

NATO jets down a drone over Lithuania as Russian strikes creep up to the alliance’s border

+

It followed a weekend of Russian strikes hugging the frontier. No one was hurt, and a separate strike landed about 2 km from Polish soil. Polish PM Donald Tusk convened an emergency session on Sept. 14 and warned of “very intensified” Russian action ahead, while NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte called Moscow “increasingly reckless” and pledged to reinforce the eastern flank. (Belarus has characterized such drones as having “gone astray” — a framing at odds with Vilnius and Warsaw; border details rest partly on government accounts.)

Strait of Hormuz · US–Iran

Iran and US Central Command give clashing accounts of the burning Indian-crewed tanker El Gaia

+

The Panama-flagged oil tanker El Gaia was left ablaze off Oman near the Strait of Hormuz with 14 crew — all Indian nationals — aboard; 13 were rescued and a search continued for one missing sailor. India’s Ministry of External Affairs condemned the attack. The accounts of what happened diverge sharply: Iran’s IRGC Navy said the tanker attempted a “forbidden” route south of the strait, struck naval mines and exploded (as carried by Iranian state media). US Central Command rejected that as a “false claim” and “yet another example of their lies,” saying the El Gaia had in fact been struck by an Iranian missile the previous month and then hit again by an Iranian drone over the weekend.

It is the clearest factual dispute of the cycle — a mine strike versus two separate Iranian munitions attacks — and it sits inside the broader 2026 Hormuz crisis, in which Tehran has restricted commercial traffic through the waterway. Independent verification of the cause was not available in the last-24h reporting, so the competing claims should be treated as unresolved; the crew nationality and the 13-of-14 rescue count are consistent across outlets. (Attribution claims rest on combatant and government statements and are provisional.)

Energy · Grid Storage

India’s battery build-out jumps 84-fold: 8.2 GWh added in the first half of 2026

+

India installed 8.2 GWh of battery energy storage in the first half of 2026 — nearly 84 times the roughly 98 MWh added in the same period of 2025 — according to a newly released report from Mercom India Research, covered Sept. 14. Cumulative installed storage reached about 9.3 GWh by the end of June, meaning most of the country’s fleet was deployed in just six months as utilities lean on batteries for grid balancing, peak-demand management, and integrating solar and wind.

Standalone, grid-scale systems accounted for roughly 98% of quarterly additions and about 83% of cumulative installations. By state, Rajasthan led with a 36% share, followed by Gujarat (20%) and Maharashtra (5%). The report also flagged a pipeline-versus-execution gap: storage tenders totaled about 28 GW in the half, but only roughly 9 GW was actually auctioned — down about 18% year over year — a sign the deployment surge is running ahead of contracting. (Figures are the research firm’s.)

+

On Sunday, Sept. 13, SpaceX launched a Falcon 9 from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral at 2:49 p.m. ET — the 700th mission of the Falcon rocket family since Falcon 1’s debut in March 2006. Science · Astronomy

Interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS looks nitrogen-rich — a clue it was born in deep cold around another star

+

Astronomers led by Lea Ferellec of Northumbria University (with Cyrielle Opitom and Colin Snodgrass) report that the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS is rich in molecular nitrogen (N₂), which they read as evidence it formed in extremely cold conditions — below about −240°C — in the distant outskirts of another planetary system. The team used the WEAVE spectrograph on the 4.2-m William Herschel Telescope in the Canary Islands to observe the comet’s plasma tail on Nov. 30 and Dec. 2, 2025, after its closest approach to the Sun, detecting ions including nitrogen, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, water and CH.

3I/ATLAS is only the third confirmed interstellar object to pass through the Solar System, after 1I/‘Oumuamua and 2I/Borisov, so its chemistry offers a rare direct sample of material assembled around another star. Published in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society and picked up widely on Sept. 13–14, the nitrogen-rich, cold-origin reading rests on a single dataset and should be treated as provisional pending further observation. (Single-study finding; awaiting independent analysis.)
