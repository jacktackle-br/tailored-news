The Budget · Shutdown Countdown

Trump and the top four leaders leave the White House still far apart — a shutdown now looms when funding lapses after Sept. 30

+

Federal funding expires at the end of the fiscal year on Sept. 30, and without a stopgap the government begins shutting down at 12:01 a.m. on Oct. 1 — now roughly two days out. On Monday, President Trump met the four top congressional leaders — Senate Majority Leader John Thune, Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, House Speaker Mike Johnson and Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries — at the White House, and the session broke up with no deal. Schumer said the two sides have “very large differences” but that he believed Trump heard Democrats’ objections “for the first time.”

Republicans, who hold both chambers narrowly, want a “clean” short-term bill that keeps money flowing at current levels into late November. Democrats are withholding their votes unless the measure also extends the enhanced Affordable Care Act premium subsidies that expire at year’s end, warning that premiums will spike otherwise. Because a stopgap needs 60 votes in the Senate and Republicans hold 53, Thune has said the outcome rests on “a handful of Democrats” — about eight would have to cross over. The fight arrives five weeks before the Nov. 3 midterms, sharpening every incentive to assign blame. (Fast-moving; positions and any last-minute stopgap could shift before the deadline.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left says Democrats finally have leverage and should use it: with health-insurance costs about to jump for millions, holding the line to save the ACA subsidies is both good policy and good politics. Let Republicans, who control the White House and Congress, own a shutdown — and own defunding health care while protecting tax breaks for the wealthy. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right frames it as Democrats threatening to shut the government down to force an expensive Obamacare bailout onto an unrelated funding bill; pass the clean stopgap, they argue, and negotiate health policy separately. Libertarians (Reason) see familiar theater from both parties and note the real problem is a budget nobody will restrain. Anti-Trump conservatives at The Bulwark counter that the party controlling every lever will own the chaos no matter who blinks — an intra-right dissent, not the party line. (Directional.)

Sources: CBS News, NBC News, NBC (ACA-subsidy demand), The Hill
The Presidency · Oil & War Powers

Trump touts U.S. control of ‘65 billion barrels’ of Venezuelan oil as the biggest deal in history — as House Democrats warn companies the legal ground is shaky

+

President Trump announced that the United States has entered an agreement giving it majority control of more than 65 billion barrels of Venezuela’s proven oil reserves, calling it “the biggest oil deal in the history of the world.” He said it was negotiated by Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth with Venezuela’s interim president Delcy Rodriguez, that it came “at no cost” to U.S. taxpayers, and that it would “significantly” lower gas prices over time. The White House disclosed no structure; Reuters described long-term access for U.S. firms to a group of oil fields plus increased Venezuelan supply. The deal follows the U.S. military operation, roughly nine months ago, that captured President Nicolás Maduro; Rodriguez said Caracas keeps “ownership and sovereignty” over its resources.

The announcement deepens a running fight over executive power. Rep. Sean Casten led a dozen House Democrats in warning 21 oil and oilfield-services companies of the legal and civil risks of relying on the administration’s asserted authority, citing Congress’s war powers, obligations under the U.N. Charter, and limits under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. An earlier Senate war-powers resolution to constrain Trump’s Venezuela operations failed narrowly, broken by Vice President JD Vance; Democrats on the Oversight, Foreign Affairs and Natural Resources committees are now pressing State and DOJ for the deal’s legal basis and the role of intermediary businessman Alejandro Betancourt. (Deal terms remain undisclosed; claimed reserve figures and price effects are the administration’s.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left calls it a war for oil made explicit: depose a foreign leader, then seize his country’s reserves and route the spoils through well-connected middlemen. Setting it up by executive fiat, they argue, runs past Congress and the U.N. Charter alike — and the “no cost” framing hides who really profits. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right sells energy dominance: cheaper gas, a strategic win over China and Russia in the hemisphere, and reserves doubled without a taxpayer bill. Libertarians (Reason) are unconvinced — regime change plus a state-brokered resource grab is exactly the entangling, executive-overreach adventurism they warn against, and they want to know who collects. Anti-Trump conservatives at The Bulwark read the intermediary questions as spoils politics — an intra-right dissent, not the party line. (Directional.)

Sources: PBS NewsHour, Kuwait Times, Semafor, Rep. Casten (House)

Moldova · Europe

Moldova’s pro-EU party wins an outright majority again, beating back a record Russian influence operation

+

In Sunday’s Sept. 28 parliamentary election, President Maia Sandu’s pro-European Party of Action and Solidarity (PAS) won about 50.2% with nearly all ballots counted — enough for roughly 55 of 101 seats and a renewed outright majority. The pro-Russian Patriotic Electoral Bloc (which includes former president Igor Dodon’s Socialists) came second at about 24.2%, and the “Alternative” bloc third near 8%; turnout was around 52%. EU leaders, including European Council President António Costa, quickly congratulated Chișinău on choosing “a European future.”

The margin matters because of what it withstood. Analysts and Moldovan officials describe an unprecedented Russian interference effort — reportedly as much as €300 million across disinformation, alleged vote-buying, and cyberattacks that targeted the Central Election Commission’s systems. A second clear PAS mandate keeps Moldova’s EU-accession track intact and marks a setback for Moscow’s hybrid campaign along its western flank, at a moment of Russian drone incursions into NATO airspace and grinding war next door in Ukraine. (Seat totals are provisional pending final certification; diaspora and overseas precincts can still shift the distribution.)

Ukraine · United States

Vance says Tomahawks for Kyiv are ‘under consideration’ as Trump floats a Budapest summit with Putin

+

On Sunday, Sept. 28, Vice President JD Vance said the administration is weighing the transfer of long-range Tomahawk cruise missiles — range beyond 1,600 km — to Ukraine, adding that any final decision rests with President Trump. In an NBC interview, President Volodymyr Zelensky argued that Trump’s holding the missiles back had “deflated” Russia’s interest in diplomacy and pressed for deep-strike capability, noting pointedly that Trump “didn’t say ‘no.’” Trump has separately floated a fresh summit with Vladimir Putin in Budapest — their first since an inconclusive meeting in Alaska — while Putin has warned in a call that supplying Tomahawks would escalate the war and jeopardize any talks.

The Tomahawk question is the hinge. Weapons that can reach far inside Russian territory are exactly what Kyiv wants and what Moscow warns against, which is why the decision doubles as leverage over whether Putin engages at all. It is a distinct thread from the weekend’s deadly Russian strikes on Kharkiv and Sumy (covered in the Morning Brief below): this is about Washington’s escalation calculus and a possible return to the negotiating table. (No transfer has been approved and no summit scheduled; officials’ positions were still shifting.)

Midterms · The Blue-Wave Question

With five weeks to go, grim polling has Republicans bracing for a wave — and the White House betting the midterms on Trump himself

+

Five weeks out from the Nov. 3 midterms, a run of polling points to a difficult environment for Republicans, and reporting over the weekend describes a White House choosing to nationalize the campaign around President Trump rather than run from him. Surveys cited across outlets show Democrats leading the generic congressional ballot by roughly 54%–46% among likely voters, and find a Trump endorsement is a net negative — about 48% say it would make them less likely to back a candidate versus 21% more likely. Analysts describe Trump as among the most unpopular presidents heading into a midterm in modern polling.

The strain is showing in the map: GOP-aligned outside groups are now spending on Senate races in traditionally safe territory — names like Nebraska, Kansas, South Carolina, Mississippi, South Dakota and Arkansas have drawn fresh attention amid talk of a potential “blue wave.” Trump’s operation is wagering that maximizing base turnout — anchored by rallies such as an upcoming one in Durant, Oklahoma — can outrun the topline numbers. (Facts drawn from Tier-1/Tier-2 polling and campaign reporting; polls are snapshots, not predictions, and the environment can shift.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads the numbers as a verdict on the second term: voters who are angry about prices, aggressive immigration enforcement and what they see as authoritarian overreach are ready to deliver a check on Trump. Nationalizing the ballot around an unpopular president, they argue, is a gift — and a long-overdue chance to reclaim a chamber of Congress. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right notes that first-term-style midterms almost always punish the party in power, and that firing up the MAGA base is the only realistic path in a low-turnout election. But the right is not united: writers at The Dispatch and other center-right outlets warn that welding the party to an unpopular Trump risks the majority, urging candidates to run on the economy and local issues instead. Anti-Trump conservatives at The Bulwark go further, treating the polling as proof that Trumpism itself is the liability — an intra-right dissent, not the party line. (Directional.)

Sources: CNN, CNN (offense map), NBC News, Brookings, TIME

Climate · Extreme Weather

A rare early-season nor’easter floods the East Coast, driving the biggest Atlantic City surge since Sandy before easing into Monday

+

An unusually strong, slow-moving nor’easter battered the mid-Atlantic and Northeast through the weekend, bringing days of coastal flooding, damaging winds and heavy rain before beginning to weaken on Monday, Sept. 28. Major tidal flooding was reported from Delaware to Long Island; in Atlantic City, water rose about 3.26 feet above normally dry ground — the highest level there since Superstorm Sandy in 2012. Waves reached as high as 18 feet along the Jersey Shore and Outer Banks, driving severe beach erosion.

At least one person was killed and several injured — including children — as high winds toppled trees, and more than 80,000 customers lost power at the storm’s peak. Parts of eastern Long Island picked up close to 4 inches of rain, with some communities topping 5 inches for the event. Forecasters expect conditions to improve through Monday as the system pulls away. An intense coastal storm this early in the fall is notable; warmer seas and higher baseline sea levels raise the ceiling on surge and rainfall from such systems. Biotech · Gene Editing

One CRISPR infusion kept ‘bad’ cholesterol about half lower a full year later, an early-stage trial reports

+

A first-in-human Phase 1 trial has reported that a single infusion of an experimental CRISPR gene-editing therapy safely and durably lowered cholesterol and triglycerides in people with hard-to-treat lipid disorders. In the 15-patient study, participants who received the highest dose saw LDL (“bad”) cholesterol fall about 52.5% and triglycerides about 47.8% from baseline at 12 months, with no serious therapy-related adverse events reported over a year of follow-up.

The treatment, CTX310 (from CRISPR Therapeutics), is a one-time infusion that carries CRISPR machinery to the liver and switches off a gene called ANGPTL3, a target already linked to lower lipid levels. Results were presented at the European Society of Cardiology meeting and published in the New England Journal of Medicine, with a Cleveland Clinic–led team among the investigators. As an early, small trial it is a proof of concept, not an approved therapy — larger studies are needed to confirm safety and durability. (Findings are from a Phase 1 study with a small sample.)

Sources: ScienceDaily, Cleveland Clinic, NEJM, CNN
Energy · Grid & Storage

U.S. battery storage sets another record, and solar-plus-storage is on track to lead an 86-gigawatt build-out this year

+

The U.S. grid is on course for a record year of new capacity, with fresh industry data showing battery storage continuing to break records. Developers added about 20.2 gigawatt-hours of new battery capacity in the second quarter of 2026 — the strongest quarter on record — putting the country on a path toward roughly 71 GWh installed for the year, about a 20% jump over 2025. The build-out is dominated by solar and storage: forecasts put total utility-scale additions near 86 GW for 2026, led by roughly 43 GW of solar, with wind additions expected to more than double to about 11.8 GW. The practical payoff showed up on the grid this summer, when Texas’s ERCOT met a record peak demand of 91.1 GW in July with heavy contributions from solar, wind and batteries. The pace comes amid a shifting federal policy backdrop for clean energy. (Figures are from industry trackers and forecasts; installation totals can be revised.)

Science · Astronomy

Hubble and Webb team up to spot 27 tiny frozen worlds beyond Neptune — and find fewer small ones than expected

+

Using NASA’s Hubble and James Webb space telescopes together for the first time on these targets, two teams of astronomers have discovered 27 new trans-Neptunian objects (TNOs) — small, icy bodies orbiting the Sun far beyond Neptune. All are less than about 25 miles (40 km) across, and the smallest is roughly 6 miles (10 km) wide. The work pairs Hubble’s visible-light view with Webb’s infrared sensitivity to pin down each object’s size, color and composition.

The results, published in two companion papers in The Astronomical Journal, held a surprise: the researchers found fewer small TNOs than models predicted, and the colors of these tiny bodies track the same patterns seen in their larger cousins — a hint that they “remember” the conditions of the early solar system. The teams, led by PhD candidates at the University of Victoria and Northern Arizona University, studied both dynamically “cold” objects on near-circular orbits and “hot” ones flung onto tilted, distant paths during the giant planets’ early migration. (Interpretations are drawn from the newly published analyses.)
