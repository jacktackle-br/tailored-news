National Security · Diplomacy

The U.S.–Iran strikes pause holds into a third day as mediated talks press forward — even as Trump keeps threatening a bigger attack

+

After about 13 consecutive nights of intensifying airstrikes, the Pentagon abruptly suspended its Iran bombing campaign late Friday, July 24. No U.S. strikes were reported Saturday or Sunday, and Iran held its fire in parallel — NPR’s July 26 dispatch was headlined that both sides had paused for a second straight day. A senior Iranian official told Reuters that Tehran will hold off “as long as the U.S. maintains the pause,” even as Iran’s Foreign Ministry cautioned that past diplomacy had been “betrayed.” Mediation is running through regional intermediaries: Oman brokered the earlier Strait of Hormuz track, with messages also passing via Qatar and Pakistan, and a compromise is reportedly centered on Iran managing vessel transit through Hormuz under fewer restrictions on shipping, as both sides look to return to the interim ceasefire that collapsed this month. U.N. Ambassador Mike Waltz told Fox News that Trump is giving talks “some space” and that contacts are happening “at every level.” Chairman of the Joint Chiefs Gen. Dan Caine reportedly cautioned Trump privately that resuming large-scale operations carries real risk given strained U.S. munitions stockpiles and a largely exhausted target list.

The off-ramp is far from secured, and outlets disagree on how real the diplomacy is: at his Monday press conference, Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei said Iran “currently ha[s] no negotiations with the United States,” insisting the only formal talks are with Oman over Hormuz. Even during the pause, Trump posted AI-generated images depicting U.S. strikes on Iran, and NPR’s July 27 analysis framed him as simultaneously threatening more attacks and saying talks are pressing on. Prime Minister Netanyahu told Fox News he “fully” backs the pressure campaign but warned a renewed Iranian attack on Israel would be “a terrible mistake”; his Washington visit this week is widely cast as a possible determinant of whether Israel rejoins the strikes. Domestically, The Hill reports Republican senators warning that confidence in Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is “eroding” as the war drags on, and a poll finding nearly eight in ten Americans say the conflict has proved harder than the administration expected — an ominous read with roughly 100 days to the midterms. As Lawfare’s analysis has argued, the campaign has proceeded without the congressional authorization an international armed conflict requires. (Outlets variously describe the campaign as “13 nights,” “13 days” or “two weeks” — the framing is not uniform.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The left reads the pause as confirmation that the strikes were discretionary all along — a war of choice that Americans broadly oppose while households absorb the cost at the pump. The recurring argument, echoed in an MSNBC opinion piece contending the bombing “may be legal” but is not thereby justified given targets that serve civilians, is that legality is not the same as legitimacy, and that what is restraining Trump is internal caution and political fear, not respect for the limits Congress tried to impose. (Fresh in-window signed columns from Vox, Slate, Mother Jones or the Guardian on the pause itself were not located — provisional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The right remains genuinely split. An intra-right restraint faction — reflected in National Review’s earlier “A War Restrained by the People” and dovetailing with the reported Vance/Caine caution and with libertarian, anti-interventionist critics — warns that an open-ended campaign is eroding Trump’s own base and drifting toward the “forever war” he ran against. The pro-escalation majority counters that a pause reads as weakness and cites Tehran’s intransigence. Separately, and labeled as anti-Trump-right dissent, David Frum (Atlantic) has argued the war rests on “Trump’s whim.” A dedicated in-window WSJ, Dispatch or Reason editorial on the pause was not located — provisional.

Economy · Trade

New 10–12.5% tariffs on some 60 economies go live as gas tops $4 and Wall Street logs a second straight losing week

+

Trump let stopgap levies lapse at Friday’s (July 24) deadline and replaced them with new duties of 10% to 12.5% on imports from about 60 economies, hitting nearly all U.S. imports and stacking on top of existing border taxes, per WaPo and AP framing. Outlets disagree on the scope — WaPo and AP say “about 60 economies” while MSNBC and some wires cite “more than 80 countries” — and the Tax Foundation estimates the overall tariff regime now amounts to roughly a $700-per-household tax increase in 2026. AAA put the national average for regular gas at about $4.09 a gallon on July 23, up roughly 15 cents on the week, though gasoline and crude futures eased late in the week on optimism about the Iran pause, with Brent slipping back below $100.

Markets closed a volatile week lower: every major index lost ground, and the S&P 500 booked its second consecutive losing week — its first back-to-back losses since March — under pressure from the Iran war, the new tariffs, stubborn inflation and jitters over AI spending. The political overlay is explicit in the reporting: with roughly 100 days to the midterms, Republicans fear the tariffs will aggravate inflation-weary voters, while Democrats hammer an affordability message — Sen. Peter Welch (D-Vt.) said Trump is “on a death march for the economy and seems not to care.” The convergence of fresh import duties, gas near $4 and a soft market week hands the opposition a unified cost-of-living line and leaves Republicans defending an economy voters say they feel in their wallets.

The Left-Leaning Take

Left-of-center opinion casts the tariffs as a self-inflicted price shock and a political gift to Democrats: an MSNBC/MaddowBlog piece argued the new levies “make things worse for Republicans ahead of the midterms,” surrendering the GOP’s traditional edge on the economy three months before the vote. The framing, amplified by Democratic officials such as Welch, is that affordability — gas, groceries, import costs — is now the defining midterm liability, and that it is entirely self-authored. (A distinct in-window Vox, Slate or Mother Jones column was not located.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The sharpest right-of-center critique came from the WSJ editorial board, which called tariffs “an obsession for President Trump, much as climate was for the Biden Administration,” and questioned imposing “yet another round of border taxes three months before the November election despite their political unpopularity and economic harm.” A companion board editorial warned that “the more Mr. Trump keeps swinging recklessly, the more Americans are likely to think there’s only madness in his tariff methods.” (WSJ text is paywalled — partial; quotes via The Hill.) Middle East · Red Sea

The Houthi–Saudi front hardens: Saudi jets strike Yemen after attacks on Aramco and tankers, and Riyadh halts then resumes Red Sea oil exports as crude tops $100

+

The Iran-backed Houthis hardened the new front they opened against Saudi Arabia. After declaring a maritime blockade of the kingdom on July 25 — barring vessels from loading or discharging at Saudi ports — and striking Aramco facilities at Jizan (where a refinery was set ablaze) and Yanbu, Saudi air defenses intercepted two ballistic missiles aimed at Yanbu-area refineries on Saturday, and Saudi coalition forces carried out retaliatory strikes on Houthi targets inside Yemen, including around Hodeidah. Yanbu is critical: it is the outlet of the East–West Pipeline, which became the kingdom’s main crude-export route once Hormuz was disrupted, handling roughly 92% of Saudi seaborne crude exports in June, according to Kpler data.

Saudi Arabia temporarily halted oil shipments through the Bab al-Mandeb lane on July 26 after the tanker attacks, then said it would resume all shipments once coalition protection measures were in place. Weekly Red Sea crude loadings had already fallen from a June 29 peak of about 9.5 million barrels a day to roughly 6.1 million the week of July 13, and Brent pushed back above $100 for the first time in two months. Trump said he would hold Iran responsible for further Houthi attacks. The activity has not fully stopped even amid the U.S.–Iran pause: a Reuters item relayed by regional outlets flagged an unidentified projectile landing near a tanker in the southern Red Sea on July 27, with the vessel and crew reported safe.

Sources: CNBC, Al Jazeera, The National, Gulf News, The Hill
Europe · Russia’s War on Ukraine

Russia hits Kyiv and other cities in a fresh overnight barrage — missile debris strikes an 18-story block and a drone kills a 9-year-old in Chernihiv

+

Russia launched a new wave of strikes overnight into Sunday, July 26, following Zelensky’s July 24 warning of a “massive” attack within 48 hours. Casualty framing diverges across desks — the Washington Post and AP led with a conservative city-specific toll, while Al Jazeera and Ukrainian regional tallies cite the higher six-to-seven-killed nationwide figure, the difference being city-specific versus country-wide counts. Kyiv, for its part, continued its deep long-range campaign, including further strikes on Caspian Sea vessels it says ferry military cargo between Iran and Russia — the same thread that opened a diplomatic rift with Tehran earlier in the week.

Middle East · The West Bank

Settlers torch two West Bank mosques and rampage across the territory as Israel launches a sweeping crackdown, raiding a Nablus hospital

+

The occupied West Bank’s worst flare-up in months escalated sharply over the weekend. Following the July 24 clash near the village of Tell (Tal), southwest of Nablus — in which four Palestinians and two Israelis were killed — settlers carried out mass reprisals overnight into July 26, torching two mosques (in the Nablus and Tulkarm areas) with “revenge” graffiti and rampaging across multiple governorates including Nablus, Tulkarm, Ramallah, Hebron, Salfit, Qalqilya, Jericho and around Jerusalem, burning homes, vehicles and property.

Israel then launched a wide crackdown: the military and Shin Bet detained hundreds of Palestinians and raided a hospital in Nablus to seize two wounded suspects. The episode carries a governance and an election angle. Netanyahu vowed to act “with full force against the terrorists,” having paired earlier raids with a pledge to accelerate the legalization of settlement farm outposts — a move critics say rewards settler violence — while opposition Democrats leader Yair Golan, launching his party’s campaign, accused the government of turning the West Bank into “a powder keg.” Human Rights Watch, in a July 24 report, warned that the surge in settler violence “threatens mass atrocities.”

Technology · Artificial Intelligence

China’s Moonshot AI releases Kimi K3, the largest open-weight model yet at 2.8 trillion parameters

+

The Chinese startup Moonshot AI made the weights for Kimi K3 publicly downloadable on the evening of July 26, a day ahead of its stated July 27 target. At 2.8 trillion parameters, K3 is described across outlets as the first open-weight model to reach the roughly three-trillion-parameter class — about 2.8 times its predecessor, K2.6, and larger than Chinese rivals such as DeepSeek V4 Pro (1.6T) and Zhipu’s GLM 5 (744B). It is a sparse mixture-of-experts model, reportedly activating 16 of 896 experts per request, with a one-million-token context window and native image support. The full weights weigh in around 1.4 terabytes, a serious barrier to self-hosting.

On Artificial Analysis’s composite leaderboard, K3 reportedly scored an Elo of 1,547, trailing only one frontier system, and ranked first on Arena’s blind Frontend Code evaluation at 1,679. The release has been widely framed as fresh evidence that Chinese labs are competing at the frontier despite U.S. compute-export limits — and that the open-weight tier, where anyone can download and run the model, is increasingly led from China. As with any launch-window figures, the benchmark numbers largely originate with the developer and third-party aggregators rather than independent audits and should be read cautiously.

Sources: VentureBeat, Tom’s Hardware, MLQ, TECHi
Technology · AI & Energy

Nvidia is in talks to backstop some $250 billion in financing for a 10-gigawatt OpenAI data center in Ohio

+

The Wall Street Journal reported Sunday, July 26 — matched by Bloomberg the same day — that Nvidia is in talks to guarantee roughly $250 billion of financing to help OpenAI lease a 10-gigawatt data-center campus being developed by a SoftBank energy subsidiary in southern Ohio. The full campus could cost more than $500 billion including chips. The guarantee would cover the lease and debt financing — easing lenders’ concerns given OpenAI’s lack of an investment-grade credit rating — but not the Nvidia chips themselves, which the company is separately discussing financing to the tune of up to $350 billion. The first phase, about 800 megawatts, is reportedly targeted for completion by 2028.

The energy angle is the striking part: a single 10-gigawatt campus is a utility-scale power draw — comparable to the output of several large nuclear reactors — underscoring how the AI buildout has become a first-order electricity-demand and grid story, not merely a chips story. Skeptics, including the investor Michael Burry (per Yahoo Finance), flagged the circularity of a chipmaker financing demand for its own chips, a pattern critics say inflates the apparent scale of AI investment.

Climate · Extreme Weather

Europe’s wildfire emergency deepens: more than 300,000 now displaced across France and Spain as a third heatwave grips the continent

+

The France–Spain wildfire emergency escalated further over July 26–27. CNBC reported on July 27 that more than 300,000 people have now been displaced, with French crews battling blazes around Bordeaux (in Gironde and Landes) and Spanish firefighters working to hold gains near Madrid. As of July 27, one of Spain’s largest recorded wildfires, near Madrid, had burned more than 111,000 acres since July 20, with separate reporting citing roughly 13,000–15,000 hectares scorched in neighboring Ávila province.

France’s interior minister, Laurent Nuñez, reported tens of thousands evacuated overnight, and President Macron deployed about 1,000 soldiers plus an A400M military transport to support firefighting, vowing France “will rebuild.” Meteorologists attribute the severity to a third consecutive heatwave that has dried out vegetation across the region; temperatures are forecast to climb again this week with no rain in sight, and officials warned that shifting winds continue to hamper containment. Scientists have repeatedly tied the intensity of Europe’s 2026 fire season to human-caused climate change, which compounds the heat and drought that prime the landscape to burn.

Space · Planetary Science

China’s Tianwen-1 orbiter captures the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS as it sweeps past Mars

+

China’s Tianwen-1 Mars orbiter has captured a faint but notable view of the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS as it raced past Mars, with images revealing its tail and glowing coma. 3I/ATLAS — discovered on July 1, 2025 — is only the third confirmed interstellar object ever observed passing through our solar system, after 1I/‘Oumuamua and 2I/Borisov, making any close-range imagery scientifically valuable.

The comet has become one of the most heavily observed ever, tracked by Hubble, the James Webb Space Telescope, and the XMM-Newton and XRISM X-ray observatories — the first interstellar comet seen in X-ray light — as well as by NASA’s Parker Solar Probe in late 2025. As of July 27 it sits in the constellation Gemini, and the Tianwen-1 imagery adds a rare Mars-orbit vantage point to the multi-observatory campaign. (The imaging itself was surfaced around July 23–24, just outside a strict 24-hour window, but stands as the cycle’s most notable genuine science development.)

Sources: ScienceDaily, European Space Agency, NASA Science

Cycling · Tour de France

Pogácar seals a record-equalling fifth Tour de France as Van der Poel storms the Paris finale

+

Tadej Pogácar (UAE Team Emirates-XRG) won the 2026 Tour de France on Sunday, July 26, capturing his fifth overall title and joining Jacques Anquetil, Eddy Merckx, Bernard Hinault and Miguel Indurain on the all-time record of five Tour victories. At 27, the Slovenian is the youngest rider ever to reach five titles. He held the yellow jersey from his stage-six win in the Pyrenees onward and took five stages en route. The final general classification: Pogácar first; Remco Evenepoel (Red Bull–Bora–Hansgrohe) second at 6:26 — the best Grand Tour finish of his career; and Isaac del Toro (UAE Team Emirates-XRG) third at 9:42.

The shortened, rain-affected final stage around Montmartre and the Champs-Élysées produced a dramatic sprint: Mathieu van der Poel (Alpecin-Deceuninck) won stage 21, narrowly beating Jasper Philipsen and Mads Pedersen after repeated attacks on the cobbled Butte Montmartre climb. The other classifications went to Pedersen (Lidl-Trek) in the green points jersey, Richard Carapaz (EF Education-EasyPost) in the polka-dot King of the Mountains, and Del Toro in the white young-rider jersey on his Tour debut — a strong showing for the UAE squad that also delivered the overall winner.
