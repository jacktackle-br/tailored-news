Pentagon · Civil-Military

A former Air Force secretary calls Hegseth’s Pentagon ‘dysfunctional’ — and the ‘climate of fear’ now eclipses the weekend resignation

+

The Pentagon story that opened this morning around Army Secretary Dan Driscoll’s resignation shifted this evening from a personnel exit to an on-the-record indictment from inside the building’s recent past. Former Air Force Secretary Frank Kendall III told CNN, PBS and ABC that the Defense Department under Pete Hegseth is, “in a word … dysfunctional,” describing a “climate of fear that’s been created” as “very palpable” and driving “very good people” out. Kendall put a number on it — roughly 25 general-officer firings plus removals from promotion lists, which he called unprecedented and noted have swept up “a lot of women and minorities” — and argued Hegseth “is not getting good advice” because subordinates are “afraid to tell him things he doesn’t want to hear.”

The critique lands alongside fresh reporting that the department has quietly installed conservative media figures as in-house “influencers”: Pentagon records show retired officers-turned-commentators, among them retired Air Force Col. Rob Maness and retired Army Col. Kurt Schlichter, assigned government email addresses in the personnel-and-readiness shop of Undersecretary Anthony Tata. All of it feeds the Republican backlash already voiced by Sen. Thom Tillis (R-N.C.), who has urged Trump to replace Hegseth. Trump continues to back his secretary. (Kendall’s remarks are attributed to CNN/PBS/ABC interviews; the “influencers” personnel details rest on Pentagon records cited in secondary reporting and were not independently confirmed here.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left reads Kendall — a career defense official, not a partisan brawler — as finally attaching a name and specifics to what critics have alleged: a loyalty-driven purge that hollows out professional military leadership, disproportionately pushes out women and minority officers, and cultivates yes-men while the country fights two wars. The “influencer” hires read as propaganda replacing expertise. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The administration lane dismisses a Biden-era holdover’s verdict as predictable sour grapes and defends a secretary’s right to reshape a brass it views as bloated and politicized. But the intra-right split is loud and worth labeling as such: Tillis on the record, plus Dispatch- and Bulwark-aligned conservatives, treat the exits and the “climate of fear” as reckless wartime mismanagement, not reform — an anti-Trump-right critique, not the mainstream-right position, which has largely defended the housecleaning. (Directional; intra-right disagreement labeled.)

Courts · Mail Voting

Trump goes back to the Supreme Court a third time in six weeks — now asking it to let USPS refuse ballots already in the mail

+

The mail-ballot fight flagged this morning developed over the weekend. On Sunday, Sept. 6, the administration filed a fresh emergency application — by SCOTUSblog’s and news accounts its third bid in roughly six weeks — asking the justices to let the U.S. Postal Service’s “Final Rule” take effect while Judge Indira Talwani’s block stands. Critics read the posture as, in effect, letting USPS refuse to deliver or return ballots that are already in transit in states that have begun mailing them for the Nov. 3 midterms — because no state can meet the rule’s envelope-redesign, portal-approval and voter-upload demands in time.

As of Monday evening the Court had not ruled. The through-line from this morning is unchanged but sharper: with ballots physically moving, every day the emergency docket stays quiet is a day the midterm vote proceeds under the old rules, and the repeated filings keep the pressure on a Court that in late August had already lifted one of two injunctions against the underlying executive order. (Legal/elections story weighted to Tier 1 wire desks and SCOTUSblog; the justices’ disposition and the order’s ultimate merits remain undecided.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees serial trips to a sympathetic Supreme Court to make mail voting harder in the final weeks before a contested midterm — a third bite at the same apple after lower courts found no evidence of the fraud the rule claims to fight. Targeting ballots already in the mail is the tell. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The administration lane argues the executive branch has authority over the Postal Service and a real interest in uniform, verifiable election mail, and that one district judge shouldn’t set national ballot-delivery policy on the eve of an election. A libertarian strand (Reason-style) is warier: federalizing ballot handling by executive fiat concentrates power whoever holds it. (Directional.)

Courts · Mail Voting

With ballots already going out, the Supreme Court weighs Trump’s emergency bid to impose USPS mail-ballot rules before the midterms

+

The fight over mail voting sits at the Supreme Court on an emergency posture as the first states begin sending out ballots for the Nov. 3 midterms. The Trump administration is asking the justices to override U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani of Massachusetts, who blocked a U.S. Postal Service “Final Rule” that would refuse to deliver mail ballots unless state and local officials submit ballot-envelope designs to a USPS portal and receive advance approval — a requirement she found threatens to disenfranchise millions of mail voters less than 70 days before the election, with no record evidence of mail-ballot fraud to justify the rushed rollout. The rule operationalizes an executive order Trump signed in March; the suit was brought by the League of Women Voters and a coalition of Democratic attorneys general.

By SCOTUSblog’s account, the administration’s new application is not materially different from one it filed days earlier, and the Court in late August had already lifted one of two injunctions against the underlying order — leaving the mail-delivery piece as the live battleground. The clock is the point: every day the rule stays blocked, the midterm vote proceeds under the old regime in states where ballots are now in the mail. (Legal/elections story weighted to Tier 1 wire desks and SCOTUSblog/court reporting; the justices had not ruled on the emergency application as of Monday morning, and the executive order’s ultimate merits remain undecided.)

The Left-Leaning Take

The directional left sees a naked attempt to make mail voting harder in the weeks before a contested midterm, repeatedly running to a sympathetic Supreme Court to override a district judge who found no evidence of the fraud the rule claims to fight. The through-line: when the courts say the rule is unlawful, the answer is to escalate rather than comply. (Directional.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Germany · State Election

The far-right AfD wins Saxony-Anhalt in a landslide — its first plurality in a German state — but falls just short of a majority

+

The Alternative for Germany (AfD) — classified as right-wing extremist by German domestic intelligence — won Sunday’s Landtag election in Saxony-Anhalt with roughly 43.8% of the vote, more than double the center-right CDU’s about 17.2%, per preliminary official results. It is the first time the party has finished as the strongest force in a German state parliament. But the AfD took 39 of the chamber’s 83 seats — three short of the 42 needed for a majority — with the CDU on 15, and the SPD, Greens and Die Linke on 8 apiece, and the BSW clearing the threshold at 5.

A commanding plurality does not by itself deliver power. Every other party maintains the “firewall” (Brandmauer) refusing to govern with the AfD, so a CDU-led coalition assembled from a distant second remains the likeliest path to a government — but the arithmetic is now far tighter than the pre-vote firewall assumed. The result lands as a national warning shot ahead of further votes in the coming weeks and as a rebuke to Chancellor Friedrich Merz’s wager that a firmer CDU would win voters back from the far right. (Figures are preliminary official results and may be revised; seat totals can shift slightly on final certification.)

Sources: NPR, Al Jazeera, Euronews, NBC News, Wikipedia (results)

Energy · Fusion / AI

A Princeton AI system learns to head off a fusion-plasma instability before it starts — in milliseconds

+

Researchers at the Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory and Princeton University reported an AI framework, dubbed PACMAN (Prediction And Control using MAchiNe learning), that makes split-second control decisions to keep a fusion reaction stable. In experiments on the DIII-D tokamak, a machine-learning model predicted a disruptive tearing-mode instability about 200 milliseconds before it appeared and adjusted the plasma to avoid it — where conventional controllers can only react after such a mode has already begun. The system also steered all six of DIII-D’s microwave heating beams in real time to hit complex targets, while, the team stresses, keeping humans in charge of the goals.

Controlling the violent instabilities of superheated plasma is one of the central obstacles to commercial fusion power, and doing it faster than a human can react is a meaningful step toward a reactor that could run steadily on the grid. The framework’s design and first results were published in the journal Nuclear Fusion, with the lab announcing them on Sept. 2. (Caveat: this is a control-system demonstration on an existing experimental tokamak, not net-energy-gain fusion or a power plant; the path from stability control to grid electricity remains long.)

Sources: PPPL, ScienceDaily, The Business Standard
Electrification · Grid Storage

California passes 21,000 MW of grid batteries as U.S. storage installs head for a record year

+

California’s fleet of grid-connected batteries has surpassed 21,000 megawatts, an increase of more than 2,500% in about seven and a half years, according to the California Energy Commission — enough stored power to soak up midday solar and discharge it into the evening peak. The state reported that solar generation outpaced natural gas over the first half of 2026, a shift the batteries are built to exploit. Nationally, the U.S. installed 20.2 gigawatt-hours of battery capacity in the second quarter and is on track to add roughly 71 GWh across 2026, an industry report says, with California and Texas leading utility-scale deployment.

The build-out is reshaping how the grid balances supply and demand as renewables’ share climbs and data-center load surges. Supply-chain moves underline the momentum: manufacturers continue reallocating capacity toward lower-cost lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) cells for stationary storage, and new domestic lithium-supply deals aim to shore up U.S. sourcing. (Figures come from the California Energy Commission and industry trackers; the “record year” and full-year totals are projections that depend on installs completing on schedule.)

Science · Astronomy

A total lunar eclipse turns the Moon blood-red this week — for most of the world, though not the Americas

+

A total lunar eclipse darkens and reddens the full Moon overnight Sunday into Monday, Sept. 7–8, as Earth slides directly between the Sun and Moon and the planet’s shadow bathes the lunar surface in the filtered red light of every sunrise and sunset at once — the “blood moon” effect. Astronomers put totality at more than 80 minutes, with the best views across Asia, western Australia and parts of Europe and Africa; by one estimate, the great majority of the world’s population falls within the viewing zone. The notable exception is the Americas, which are turned away from the Moon during the eclipse and miss it entirely.

Unlike a solar eclipse, a lunar eclipse is safe to watch with the naked eye and needs no equipment, though binoculars deepen the color. (This is a predictable astronomical event rather than a discovery; exact timing and visibility depend on the observer’s location, and clouds are the usual spoiler.)

Sources: Space.com, Scientific American, NBC News
