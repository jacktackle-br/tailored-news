Transportation · FAA

Two days after the nationwide ground stop, Duffy says “we don’t actually have the root cause,” and Trump calls on Congress to pay for a new air-traffic network

+

The FAA halted flights nationwide on Friday afternoon, Nov. 13, citing a telecommunications failure. It was the first full ground stop since 2023 and lasted more than four hours. Saturday cancellations topped 6,000, and the FAA expects operations to be back to normal by Sunday night. Up to 17 of the 22 air-route traffic control centers were affected, though FAA chief Bryan Bedford said none “actually went dark.”

On Sunday shows, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said investigators “don’t actually have the root cause” yet. The working theory is that contractor L3Harris was swapping copper telecom lines for fiber at a remote FAA site, and that a router then flooded the national network. Two routers, from sites in Texas and Arkansas, are being tested at L3Harris headquarters in Florida. Engineers from the FAA, Juniper and Cisco are helping, and the router maker says its equipment did not fail. Duffy and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there is no sign of a cyberattack. Duffy declined to apologize and blamed earlier administrations for under-investing.

Trump broke his public silence on the outage in a Sunday-morning Truth Social post. He blamed the Biden administration and urged Congress to fund the upgrade all at once rather than in “drips and drabs.” Duffy is now seeking $50 billion. Democrats replied that Republicans already approved $12.5 billion last year and that most of it remains unspent. The figures differ: Sen. Chris Van Hollen said about one-third has been spent, while Reps. Rick Larsen and André Carson put spending at about $1 billion. The controllers’ union says some communications with aircraft were disrupted, and an FAA technicians’ union says its members were not involved in the upgrade.

Senate · Democrats

Yemen · Red Sea

Yemen’s government forces pull back from the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, and the Houthis push to within 60–80 km of Aden, their deepest advance since 2015

+

Reuters reported Sunday that Yemen’s Southern Armed Forces, the main government-aligned force in the south, are “redeploying” from Dhubab airport and the Bab el-Mandeb area to defend Aden, the government’s interim capital. The move appears to end a six-week counteroffensive to retake the Red Sea coast that the Houthis seized in September. Military sources say Houthi gains around Taiz undercut the campaign. AFP’s sources put Houthi fighters about 60 km from Aden after they took the Al-Maqatirah and Al-Madaribah districts. Xinhua cites 70 km, and Reuters earlier reported 80 km. Government troops are building sandbag lines and digging tunnels around the city.

The retreat gives the Houthis more leverage over one of the world’s key shipping chokepoints at the mouth of the Red Sea. That matters more because Iran is already disrupting traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. Reuters also reports that the Houthis have stepped up attacks on Saudi Arabia, which backs the government, and that the Saudi-led coalition has answered with more airstrikes. If Aden comes under direct threat, Riyadh faces a hard choice between deeper intervention and a negotiated settlement on Houthi terms.

Israel · Gaza

Israel kills Hamas’s Rafah Brigade commander, two months after killing his predecessor, as the year-old Gaza ceasefire stalls

+

The Israeli military said an overnight strike in southern Gaza killed Samir Abu Younis, 53, who commanded Hamas’s Rafah Brigade. Hamas’s Al-Qassam Brigades confirmed his death on Sunday, saying he was killed Saturday, and said he had helped plan the Oct. 7 attack. Medics said the strike hit a tent sheltering displaced people in the Mawasi area of Khan Younis and wounded two others. Abu Younis had led the brigade since Israel killed his predecessor, Nael Abu Obeid, on Sept. 15. The strike came days after an Israeli attack on Nasser Hospital that the military said targeted another commander.

The October 2025 ceasefire stopped major fighting but not Israeli strikes, and Hamas has not disarmed. Gaza health authorities say at least 1,600 Palestinians have been killed since the truce began, while five Israeli soldiers have been killed in that time. Targeted killings like this one keep pressure on Hamas’s armed wing, but they also wear away at a ceasefire whose second phase, covering disarmament and reconstruction, has not moved forward. This morning’s EU–Israel dispute over flood aid adds to that strain.

Courts · Interior · Washington

A federal judge temporarily blocks the administration from closing East Potomac Park, a day before Trump’s golf-course redevelopment was set to begin

+

On Saturday, U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes issued a two-week temporary restraining order. It bars the federal government from closing East Potomac Park and its public golf links in Washington. The course had been due to close on Sunday for a planned 15-month rebuild into a championship venue designed by Tom Fazio. The order says the park “shall remain open and accessible to the public,” except for routine, temporary closures to deal with imminent safety threats. It does not require the government to keep running golf operations, but it says the administration “cannot use the absence of golf operations as a reason to exclude the public.” Outlets differ on how broadly to describe the order. AP called it a temporary halt to work at the course, while NBC and local outlets stressed that it covers the park’s regular operating hours.

The DC Preservation League and two local golfers sued in February. They argue the plan violates the 1897 act of Congress that created the park, and they also allege that debris from the East Wing demolition was dumped on the course. Reyes again cited Trump’s own social media posts as evidence that the plan was final, and she questioned the Justice Department’s last-minute safety rationale for the closure. In late October she had issued a 70-page opinion requiring 10 days’ notice before any closure or “irreversible” design changes. The Justice Department said that “nothing in the Court’s order precludes planning” for the course to continue. The Post reports that the government is also objecting to a special master reviewing its lawyers’ conduct in the case. Trump denies the plaintiffs’ claims.

Health · Medicaid

CMS lets states hold off for six months on cutting anyone from Medicaid over the new work requirement, though the rule still formally starts Jan. 1

+

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services announced Friday that states may opt into a “hold harmless” period running through June 30, 2027. During that period, states can check whether enrollees meet the new work-and-community-engagement requirement. However, they cannot deny applications or disenroll otherwise eligible people solely for failing to meet it. States that want the option must notify CMS by December 11. The statutory start date of January 1 cannot be moved by the agency, so what CMS has delayed is enforcement. It told states to keep working toward full implementation and to test their systems end to end.

The requirement comes from last year’s Republican budget law. The Congressional Budget Office projects it will leave about 5 million fewer people enrolled in Medicaid over a decade. Nebraska, which started early in May, is the main test case: state figures show more than 1,000 people lost coverage or were denied it through August. A coalition of 26 states had sued over the rollout. Sarah Lueck of the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities said the move “lets states delay the harm.” Democrats, who will control both chambers in January, have said that repealing the law’s Medicaid cuts, including the work rules, will be a priority.

Iran war · Strait of Hormuz

Since the midterms, Trump has backed away from both new strikes and new talks with Iran, AP reports, leaving the region unsure where the war is headed

+

An AP analysis published Saturday finds that Trump’s statements since the November 3 elections point in several directions at once. Days after threatening to “destroy” Iran, he called further strikes “quite unlikely.” He then said “we don’t need a deal with Iran,” while also stepping back from more rounds with Tehran’s negotiators, as the conflict nears its eighth month. The Post reports that the White House is presenting the “status quo” of a naval blockade and economic pressure as a victory. Vice President JD Vance, the lead negotiator, has publicly tied the war and the resulting energy shock to Republican midterm losses.

On the water, the stalemate is not quiet. On Saturday Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said two more oil tankers had exploded after hitting mines in the strait, and other Iranian reports put the total at three. They said the ships had switched off their transponders and were using an “unauthorised” route. The claims could not be independently verified. The U.S. military says it has cleared the main shipping lanes of mines, and a Guards spokesman challenged Washington to “prove” that with independent evidence. Tehran continues to require ships to obtain its permission to transit the strait.

Gaza · EU · Humanitarian

The EU demands that Israel let “immediate, unimpeded” aid into flood-hit Gaza, and Israel calls the statement “completely unbalanced”

+

On Saturday, the office of EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas called on Israel to reopen all Gaza crossings and let shelter materials, medical equipment and other aid flow without restriction after winter flooding. The statement said Israel “must enable the work of the UN and its agencies, and humanitarian organisations including NGOs.” It also called for Israel’s full withdrawal and for full implementation of UN Security Council Resolution 2803, including the disarmament of Hamas. Aid groups say stocks of tents, tarpaulins and blankets inside Gaza are nearly exhausted. UNICEF says heavy rain has directly affected at least 106,000 people, and the WHO says close to a million displaced people live in flood-exposed areas.

Israel’s Foreign Ministry said the statement “rewards Hamas’s refusal to disarm.” COGAT, the military body that handles aid, said more than 93,000 tents and over 8,600 trucks of winter supplies had entered Gaza. It blamed UN agencies and NGOs for leaving about half of approved requests uncollected. Neither side’s figures have been independently verified. The same day, Israel said a targeted strike had killed the commander of Hamas’s Rafah Brigade. The episode shows how strained the U.S.-backed ceasefire arrangement remains as the reconstruction-versus-disarmament standoff drags on.

France · Protest

France’s protest movement marches for a fifth straight Saturday against living costs and a police-protection bill, with smaller but still six-figure crowds

+

The Interior Ministry counted about 106,000 demonstrators nationwide on Saturday, 24,000 of them in Paris. That is down from 146,000 nationwide and 46,000 in Paris a week earlier. Unions put Saturday’s turnout at 200,000. The rallies were largely peaceful, but police fired tear gas in Paris, Bordeaux and Nantes after some protesters set off fireworks. Authorities reported 219 arrests by early evening, including 45 in Paris, and a few officers were lightly injured.

The movement, which some outlets describe as a new “Yellow Vest” wave, has grown over more than a month of high-school blockades, strikes and marches. It combines anger over the cost of living and strained public services with opposition to a bill that would give police more legal protection. The bill has passed the National Assembly and awaits the Senate. Lawyers for protesters say police force at earlier marches caused serious injuries. Officials blame far-left radicals for the violence, and coverage divides along those lines. More protests are planned.

Space · SpaceX

SpaceX catches a returning Starship upper stage with the launch tower’s arms for the first time, completing Flight 15 after the booster catch was aborted

+

On Saturday the Starship ship returned from orbit to Starbase in South Texas, and the tower’s “chopsticks” arms caught it. It was the first time SpaceX had recovered the upper stage this way, after years of booster catches. Flight 15 had already reached orbit and deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites, the heavier next-generation version that Starship was built to carry. The planned “double catch” fell short on the first stage. The Super Heavy booster hit a problem during descent, aborted its catch, diverted to the Gulf of Mexico and exploded before reaching the water. Space.com reports that SpaceX attributed the abort to a guidance issue and is still investigating.

Catching the ship is the key step toward the full and rapid reuse that Starship’s economics depend on. Until now, every upper stage had been expended or lost, and recovering it is what would let SpaceX turn ships around like aircraft for Starlink launches, orbital refueling tests and NASA’s Artemis lunar lander. Outlets differ on parts of the timeline because the launch came on Friday and the catch on Saturday.

Climate · COP31

As COP31 enters its final week, UN climate chief Simon Stiell urges negotiators to speed up while major producers resist firm fossil-fuel language

+

Stiell told delegates in Antalya on Saturday to take on the hardest issues without delay. He called the talks constructive but acknowledged that significant differences remain. The central divide is over fossil fuels. According to Reuters, major oil, gas and coal producers and consumers are resisting firm new targets or a clearly mapped transition away from fossil fuels. That makes it harder for host Türkiye and Australia, which leads the negotiations, to land an agreement that advocates would consider ambitious.

Türkiye is promoting an electrification proposal, but it does not require the additional power to come from renewable sources, which has disappointed delegations that want a firmer transition roadmap. Australia’s Chris Bowen, COP31’s President of Negotiations, has said the first draft of the overall agreement should arrive early this week. That text will show whether fossil-fuel language survives into the endgame.
