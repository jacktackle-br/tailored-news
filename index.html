Courts · Election 2026

A judge’s order limiting DHS door-to-door checks on suspected noncitizen voters in nine states was set to take effect at 5 p.m. Friday unless the administration won a stay

+

U.S. District Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín of the Northern District of California issued a preliminary injunction Wednesday. It bars the Department of Homeland Security from election-related field operations and interviews when agents know, suspect or reasonably should suspect that the person is a U.S. citizen. The order covers California, Connecticut, Georgia, Missouri, Nevada, New York, Pennsylvania, Washington and Wisconsin and runs until polls close on Nov. 3. She paused it until 5 p.m. Friday so the government could appeal. As of this edition, no report we could find confirmed that an appeal had been filed or decided.

The judge found that the government’s target lists had a “high likelihood of error.” No federal database records the citizenship of U.S. residents, and the lists drew on 48.2 million voter records of “questionable provenance.” She also found that sending armed agents to people’s homes likely violates the Voting Rights Act’s ban on voter intimidation. She did not rule that investigating noncitizen voting is itself unlawful, and DHS may still pursue people it suspects are noncitizens. CBS News reported that hundreds of Homeland Security Investigations agents had been reassigned to the effort. DHS says it is “laser focused” on noncitizens who vote and has “no plans to question or investigate people we believe to be U.S. citizens.” Separately, the Justice Department is pressing a suit, now also before the Ninth Circuit, to make California set aside ballots from about 20,000 voters it calls “likely noncitizens.”

The Left-Leaning Take

Voting-rights groups and left-leaning outlets call the operation federal voter intimidation timed for the midterms. In their view, armed agents knocking on citizens’ doors with error-ridden lists is meant to scare eligible voters, especially naturalized and Latino citizens, more than to catch the rare noncitizen voter. (No in-window Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion piece found; summary drawn from Democracy Docket, the plaintiffs and House Democrats as reported.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Defenders of the program say noncitizen voting is a federal crime that DHS has clear authority to investigate, and that an unelected judge has hobbled enforcement days before an election. Right-leaning coverage highlights that the judge is a Biden appointee and that the ruling leaves the government’s underlying power intact. (No in-window National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason opinion piece found; summary reflects DHS statements and Fox News coverage.)

Courts · Press

Judge orders Trump to sit for a deposition in his $10 billion BBC lawsuit by Dec. 11, after he canceled an Oct. 16 session, and warns the case could be dismissed if he doesn’t

+

Federal Magistrate Judge Enjoliqué Lett in Miami ruled Thursday that Trump must be deposed by Dec. 11 in his defamation suit over the BBC’s edit of his Jan. 6, 2021, speech. Discovery closes Dec. 18, and she warned that sanctions for missing the deadline could include dismissal. Trump’s lawyers had asked for dates between Jan. 11 and Jan. 22 and wanted only a court reporter. The judge allowed video, but only for the attorneys’ use. The footage will not be made public. CNN reported that she also pressed Trump’s team over overdue records.

BBC lawyer Charles Tobin told the court that Trump canceled an Oct. 16 deposition, citing a “matter of national importance,” and spent that day campaigning in Georgia. The BBC has apologized for the edit but denies defaming Trump, noting the documentary did not air in the U.S. A provisional trial date is set for Feb. 15, 2027.

The Left-Leaning Take

Critics on the left call the suit dubious and say the president now has to answer questions under oath about the harm he claims, including how a program that never aired in the U.S. damaged someone who went on to win the 2024 election. Former Trump lawyer Ty Cobb predicted he will skip or walk out of the deposition and let the case be thrown out. (No in-window Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion found; drawn from MS NOW’s Maddow Blog and Poynter commentary.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Trump’s side says the BBC admitted a misleading edit, and that the dispute is over scheduling, not the merits: a sitting president’s calendar two weeks before a national election justifies a January date. (No in-window National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason opinion piece found; summary reflects Trump’s lawyers’ filings as reported.)

Iran · Strait of Hormuz

Trump says Iran is “faking” tanker hits to raise oil prices before the midterms; Brent briefly tops $117, and the U.N. ambassador says the war could last another year

+

Iranian state TV, citing the Revolutionary Guard navy, said four tankers were struck and set on fire Wednesday in the northern Gulf and three more hit naval mines west of the Strait of Hormuz. The Guard also threatened U.S. bases if American forces keep escorting commercial ships. None of the claims had been independently confirmed, and UK Maritime Trade Operations had not issued matching incident reports. On Thursday Trump wrote on Truth Social that Iran was making up “fake hits, mines and everything else” to push oil prices up before Nov. 3. He gave no evidence and said more oil now passes through the strait than before the war. Kpler data shows Gulf flows close to prewar levels. Saudi Aramco says flows on its East-West pipeline, which bypasses the strait, have risen from 2 million to 7 million barrels a day.

Markets reacted anyway. Brent rose about 4% Thursday to roughly $113 and briefly passed $117 on Friday, its highest of the war. In a Financial Times interview published Friday, U.S. Ambassador to the U.N. Mike Waltz said the U.S. could still be at a stalemate a year from now, with no nuclear deal and shipping below prewar levels, and that the naval blockade of Iranian ports could stay in place indefinitely. Iran says indirect talks continue through Pakistan and Qatar. Trump says there are no negotiations. Analysis: The official line now has two tracks. Trump says the war will end “very soon,” while his U.N. envoy is preparing the public for a long blockade. Together with the reported post-election strike plans covered in this morning’s brief, that suggests the administration sees no quick diplomatic exit. The market is treating disputed and unverified attacks as real risk.

Russia · Ukraine

Russia hits Kyiv with ballistic missiles and Ukraine sends hundreds of drones toward Moscow, a day after Putin told Witkoff he would resume three-way talks after Nov. 10

+

U.S. envoy Steve Witkoff met Vladimir Putin for about three hours in St. Petersburg on Wednesday, a day after meeting Ukrainian and European officials in Norway. Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov said Putin agreed in principle to resume U.S.–Russia–Ukraine talks after Nov. 10, possibly in Abu Dhabi, with a postwar security framework as the main agenda item. He dismissed recent European and Ukrainian proposals as having little value. Ushakov said the two sides also discussed a possible U.S.–China–Russia leaders’ meeting at APEC in Shenzhen. Russia freed detained American teacher Stephen Hubbard before the meeting, and Witkoff drew criticism at home for calling Putin a “humanitarian.”

Within hours the fighting intensified. Reuters reported that Russia struck Kyiv with ballistic missiles Thursday while Ukraine launched hundreds of drones toward Moscow. Reuters put the toll from attacks across a dozen Ukrainian regions at 2 dead and 34 injured, including 5 children; Al Jazeera reported 4 killed in the capital. The figures likely cover different areas. Fresh strikes on Kyiv were reported Friday. Analysis: Moscow is again agreeing to a process while keeping up military pressure. A date after the U.S. midterms gives the Kremlin time to see whether Washington’s attention and leverage change. Zelensky says Russia has often agreed to meetings that were later canceled.

Justice Department · Virginia

Sen. L. Louise Lucas is released after her first court appearance, and Gov. Spanberger says charging her this close to an election erodes trust in the Justice Department

+

Lucas, 82, is the Democratic president pro tempore of the Virginia Senate. She turned herself in Thursday after a 45-page federal indictment was unsealed in Norfolk. She made her first court appearance the same day. Fox News reported that prosecutors did not seek detention and that she was released on her own recognizance; CNBC had earlier reported her in custody with her co-defendants. The five counts are racketeering conspiracy, conspiracy to distribute marijuana, money-laundering conspiracy, honest-services fraud conspiracy and maintaining a drug-involved premises. Six others are charged, including two of her daughters and a grandson. Prosecutors allege a five-year scheme worth about $4.5 million that distributed more than 100 kilograms of marijuana, mostly before Virginia legalized retail sales, and that Lucas used her post as Finance Committee chair to protect the family business. She denies the charges and calls them political.

The reaction split along party lines. Gov. Abigail Spanberger said Lucas deserves the chance to present a defense and that bringing the case less than two weeks before the Nov. 3 election “further erodes” trust in the Justice Department. Senate Majority Leader Scott Surovell called the allegations serious but said Lucas has his support. House Speaker Don Scott questioned whether the public display of the case was politically motivated. Republican leaders defended the prosecution. Attorney General Todd Blanche dismissed the Justice Department’s practice of avoiding charges against candidates close to an election: “It’s not even a rule, it’s a practice.” Lucas is not on the ballot this year. Her daughter Lisa Lucas-Burke, one of the co-defendants, is running for Portsmouth City Council.

The Left-Leaning Take

Critics on the left see a pattern of pre-election prosecutions of Trump opponents. Lucas led Virginia’s pro-Democratic redistricting push, and her indictment comes days after federal agents searched Rep. Ilhan Omar’s D.C. home. They also point out that Blanche, as Trump’s lawyer in 2024, cited the same election-season practice he now dismisses. (No in-window Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion piece found; summary drawn from Democratic officials and left-leaning outlets such as Truthout and theGrio.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Republicans say a grand jury found the evidence sufficient. In their view, a powerful legislator accused of using her office to shield a family drug business should not be exempt because an election is close, especially one she is not on the ballot for. (No in-window National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason opinion piece found; National Review ran a straight news report. Summary reflects Republican officials and DOJ statements as reported.)

Campaign 2026 · Iowa

In Sioux City, Trump says the Iran war will be “finished very soon” while rallying for Senate nominee Ashley Hinson, who has called for the war’s “immediate end”

+

President Trump held a rally Thursday in Sioux City, in Iowa’s conservative northwest corner. Republicans there are defending an open Senate seat, the governor’s office and three competitive House seats. He brought Rep. Ashley Hinson, the Republican Senate nominee, on stage. She is in a close race with Democratic state Rep. Josh Turek, and spending in the race has passed $200 million. Trump also appeared with Zach Lahn, the Republican candidate for governor, who trails Democrat Rob Sand in recent polls. Trump told the crowd that Iran would be “finished very soon” and that oil prices would fall once the war ends. He is due back in Iowa on Nov. 1.

The war is a source of tension for Hinson. She has written that it “needs to be brought to a successful and immediate end” and has said she would push back on the White House, including over its dealings with Putin. But she voted against a House resolution to limit Trump’s war powers that two other vulnerable Iowa Republicans supported. Turek’s campaign says she voted seven times to continue the war and is trying to “rewrite her record.” CNN interviewed Iowa Republican voters who are wary of Trump resuming the war after the midterms. That ties the race to the New York Times report, covered in Foreign Affairs below, that U.S. and Israeli planners are preparing for post-election strikes.

The Left-Leaning Take

Democrats say Hinson’s call for an “immediate end” contradicts her votes. In their view, Republicans in Trump-won states are trying to separate themselves from an unpopular war and $4-plus gasoline without breaking with the president who started it. (No in-window Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion piece found; summary drawn from the Turek campaign and Democratic statements as reported.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Hinson and Trump argue that the way to end the war is to win it. For them, a successful end means a guarantee that Iran never gets a nuclear program, after which oil prices can fall. On that view, the right conservative message to voters is to finish the job, not cut and run. (No in-window National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason piece found; summary reflects the candidates’ statements as reported.)

Supreme Court · Elections

A Reuters review finds this term’s Supreme Court election rulings have mostly helped Republicans heading into Nov. 3, and Justice Kagan says they make “a too-polarized country even more so”

+

Reuters’ John Kruzel lists the Court’s major election decisions over the past year. In April the justices weakened a key provision of the Voting Rights Act, making it harder for minority voters to challenge maps as racially discriminatory. In June they struck down long-standing limits on coordinated spending between parties and candidates. Reuters reports that Republican committees have since spent well beyond the old limits, and Democratic committees have too, to a lesser degree. The Court also allowed starkly partisan maps to be used this fall, including Texas’s new congressional lines and California’s Democratic-drawn response. Not every ruling went the GOP’s way: the Court rejected a Trump-backed effort to restrict mail voting, with only Justices Thomas and Alito dissenting publicly.

Justice Elena Kagan criticized the majority in public remarks this week, saying its refusal to police partisan gerrymandering is making “a too-polarized country even more so.” George Mason law professor Ilya Somin told Reuters that several of the decisions reflect “longstanding conservative jurisprudential priorities.” Democrats need a net gain of four seats in each chamber to take control of Congress. Separately, on Oct. 21 the Court declined to let the Bureau of Prisons enforce its new policy on transgender inmates, with Thomas and Alito saying they would have granted the request.

The Left-Leaning Take

Liberal critics echo Kagan. They argue that the Court has stopped overseeing gerrymanders, weakened the Voting Rights Act and opened the way for unlimited party coordination, and that the result is a playing field tilted before any votes are cast. (No in-window Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion piece found; summary drawn from Justice Kagan’s remarks and critics as reported.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Conservative legal scholars say the rulings apply principles they have held for decades: race-conscious districting is suspect, political speech including party spending is protected, and partisan line-drawing belongs to legislatures rather than courts. They add that the mail-voting decision shows the Court is not simply siding with Trump. (No in-window National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason piece found; summary reflects conservative scholars as reported by Reuters.)

Sources: Reuters via U.S. News, Oct 22, Reuters via WMBD, Oct 22, SCOTUSblog, NBC News

Iran · War Planning

U.S. and Israeli militaries have drawn up plans for a short, intense round of strikes on Iran after the Nov. 3 midterms, the Times reports; Trump has not approved them

+

According to the New York Times, as confirmed in part by NPR, the plans call for a campaign of perhaps three days. Targets would include Iran’s nuclear sites, its energy infrastructure, and its missile and drone stockpiles. Some officials told the Times that Trump would keep strikes away from oil facilities to avoid pushing gasoline prices higher; the national average is already about $4.27 a gallon. Some officials believe the damage could push Tehran to make concessions. NPR’s source said the buildup may also be meant as pressure for a deal while indirect talks continue through Pakistan and Qatar. The Jerusalem Post reported that the two countries came close to striking earlier this month before Trump called it off at the last minute.

The report comes as the war is spreading across the Gulf. The State Department has ordered non-emergency staff out of Saudi Arabia after Houthi attacks on Saudi airports and oil sites, and CNBC reports that Trump is weighing direct U.S. strikes on the Houthis. Tehran has warned that it would retaliate against Saudi refineries, data centers and undersea cables. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Iran is “just as prepared for diplomacy as we are for war.” He is pressing a seven-day plan under which Hormuz would reopen once the U.S. lifts its blockade of Iranian ports and its oil sanctions. Washington still wants strict limits on uranium enrichment, which Iran rejects. The timing of the plans gives the war a clear place in the midterm campaign.

Sources: NPR, Oct 22, NYT via Investing.com, Washington Times, Oct 22, Times of Israel, CNBC, Oct 22, Gulf News
Taiwan · China

Taiwan’s coast guard says China is pairing maritime militia boats with coast guard ships to extend “battlefield preparation” into the western Pacific

+

According to a Reuters report published Friday, Taiwan’s Coast Guard Administration says Beijing is using fishing-militia vessels alongside its coast guard to expand operations around Taiwan and farther into the western Pacific. Taipei says the activity is also meant to pressure Japan and the Philippines. It says the activity has increased since June in waters where the three countries’ economic zones overlap, after Tokyo and Manila announced talks on drawing their maritime boundary.

Militia boats are civilian-flagged but organized by the state. That lets Beijing keep up a constant presence and test its neighbors’ responses while staying below the level of a military confrontation. With U.S. attention and naval assets focused on the Iran war and the Gulf, Taipei is presenting the pattern as a regional problem rather than only a cross-strait one, and is drawing Tokyo and Manila into its account of the threat.

Sources: Reuters via U.S. News, Oct 23

Tech · AI Spending

Meta beats revenue forecasts but reports its first cash burn in more than a decade and raises its spending floor, and the stock posts its worst day in nearly three months

+

Meta reported third-quarter revenue of $64.6 billion after Wednesday’s close, ahead of analysts’ estimates of about $63.4 billion. But it raised the bottom of its 2026 capital-spending range to $135 billion to $145 billion, up from $130 billion to $145 billion, almost all of it for AI data centers. Free cash flow was negative for the first time in more than a decade: about −$1.2 billion by analysts’ usual measure, or −$2.1 billion by Meta’s own, which also counts finance-lease payments. A year earlier it was positive $11.2 billion. Earnings also absorbed a $9.89 billion legal charge for a settlement over teen mental health. The board approved a new class of non-voting shares, and Reuters reported that investors objected to the plan to sell stock to help fund the buildout. The cost of insuring Meta’s debt against default reached a record high.

The shares rose nearly 6% after hours, then reversed. They closed down about 7% on Thursday, by one tally, and helped pull the S&P 500 down roughly 1.2%. Tesla also fell after its own results. The question for investors is no longer whether AI demand exists but whether the companies building for it can pay for the buildout from their own cash flow. Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon report next.

Grid · Virtual Power Plants

Google expands its Voltus deal to a 1-gigawatt virtual power plant on the PJM grid, adding 1,070 megawatts to the 100 megawatts it signed in June

+

Under the deal announced Thursday, Google will pay Voltus to sign up homes and businesses in PJM, the 13-state grid that serves about 67 million people. Participants will run down batteries, adjust smart thermostats or cut industrial load when the grid is strained, through 2040. They get bill savings and direct payments, and Google gets capacity it can count against the demand from its data centers. The companies say it is one of the largest distributed-capacity portfolios any hyperscaler has paid for. Voltus already manages more than 9 gigawatts of demand response and raised $225 million this month.

PJM forecasts that data centers and other large loads will add 30 to 34 gigawatts of demand by the early 2030s. Capacity prices have risen sharply, and data centers have become an issue in this fall’s campaigns. Utility Dive and Latitude Media describe the deal as a test of whether demand-side resources can grow fast enough to absorb some of the AI load before new power plants come online.

Sources: Utility Dive, Canary Media, Google, Voltus, Latitude Media
Batteries · Europe

German home-battery pioneer sonnen files for insolvency two months after Shell sold it, even as its U.S. arm says sales have doubled

+

Handelsblatt reported Thursday that sonnen GmbH has filed for insolvency, and the company confirmed it to the paper. Shell bought sonnen in 2019 and sold it in August to Tiven, a family office linked to Aurelius co-founder Gert Purkert. Under the plan, some entities will be wound down and others restructured under self-administration, with only one production site kept. Service is expected to continue for more than 150,000 customers, and about 400 headquarters staff have been told. Other German home-storage makers have also failed, and EnBW’s Senec unit has stopped taking new orders.

The U.S. business is doing much better. sonnen USA says its sales have doubled and that it expects to deliver more than 180 megawatt-hours of home storage this year. Electrek notes that it is unclear how the German proceedings will affect U.S. customers and warranties. The split reflects a wider trend: Europe’s home-battery market is consolidating after the subsidy-driven boom, while U.S. demand is growing as power prices rise and utilities pay customers to share stored power.
