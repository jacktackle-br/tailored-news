The Supreme Court

Supreme Court upholds birthright citizenship, striking down Trump's Day-One order

+

On June 30, the final day of its term, the Court struck down President Trump's January 2025 executive order denying citizenship to children born in the U.S. to undocumented or temporarily present parents. Chief Justice Roberts, joined by Justice Barrett and the three liberal justices, held that the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause guarantees citizenship to nearly all children born on U.S. soil: "The Framers of the Fourteenth Amendment extended that promise to every free-born person in this land. We keep that promise today." Thomas, Alito and Gorsuch dissented.

Both "6–3" and "5–4" are being reported, and both are right: the judgment was 6–3, but only five justices grounded it in the Constitution. Justice Kavanaugh concurred on narrower statutory grounds — the order violated a 1950s citizenship law — and pointedly noted Congress could rewrite that statute.

The Left-Leaning Take

A real win, but fragile (Slate, Vox). The alarm is Kavanaugh's concurrence — read as "a road map for a right-wing Congress to complete Trump's failed job," one that could create "an underclass of noncitizens who, by accident of birth, are denied basic rights."

The Right-Leaning Take

The reaction split. Much MAGA anger fell on Barrett; Kavanaugh drew less fire because his concurrence hands Congress a lawful, statutory path. National Review's long-standing line: address noncitizen voting by legislation, don't "swat a fly with a sledgehammer."

Columnist — David French, NYT / The Dispatch (guest column, The Bulwark)

The ruling vindicates a settled guarantee; the case "should've been 9–0," and that three justices dissented at all is the genuinely worrying signal — not the result.

Sources: NPR, SCOTUSblog, CNN, Slate, The Bulwark
Capitol Hill

SAVE America Act revolt stalls the House, forcing an early July 4 recess

+

Speaker Mike Johnson sent the House into recess early after conservatives ground business to a halt over the SAVE America Act — Trump's bill requiring documentary proof of citizenship to register to vote and tightening mail-in rules. Roughly 14 Republicans voted down a procedural rule on June 30; a bloc led by Reps. Anna Paulina Luna and Chip Roy demanded the provisions be inserted into the annual defense bill, with Luna calling Johnson's plan "a procedural head fake." The House returns July 13.

Senate GOP leaders have repeatedly told Trump the votes are not there; the chamber has already failed to advance the measure.

The Left-Leaning Take

A "show your papers" voter-suppression bill (Brennan Center, CAP): more than 21 million citizens lack immediate access to citizenship documents. The chaos shows Trump prioritizing an unworkable mandate over housing and defense.

The Right-Leaning Take

Divided on tactics, not the goal. National Review warns the bill isn't "worth the cost"; institutionalists fault the holdouts for hostage-taking the defense bill, while populists say election integrity is the fight the base sent them to have.

Sources: Washington Post, CBS News, Brennan Center

Iran

U.S.–Iran talks in Doha end without breakthrough as Hormuz toll fight looms

+

Two days of indirect U.S.–Iran talks in Doha concluded July 1 with implementation steps but no lasting breakthrough, building on an interim deal that paused the 2026 conflict (now past its 125th day). The sides agreed a goods-purchase mechanism for a first tranche of frozen assets, a channel to flag violations, and a shared focus on restoring passage through the Strait of Hormuz. VP Vance said talks were "going well."

Core disputes remain: Washington rejects any Iranian-led toll mechanism while Tehran asserts joint sovereignty with Oman; no frozen funds have moved; and the nuclear-inspection question persists. Iran signals it will begin charging shipping tolls in mid-August once a toll-free window expires — a possible flashpoint.

Sources: Rappler, CNN, Al Jazeera
Sudan / Darfur

Amnesty: RSF committed crimes against humanity and ethnic cleansing in el-Fasher

+

A July 1 Amnesty International report concludes Sudan's paramilitary RSF committed crimes against humanity and ethnic cleansing while seizing el-Fasher in North Darfur. "City Under Siege, Children Under Fire" documents murder, forcible transfer, torture, rape, sexual slavery and extermination against civilians from early 2024 to October 2025 — many from the Zaghawa ethnic group — and says the acts "may be relevant to the crime of genocide." Findings rest on 247 interviews plus 89 open-source videos and satellite imagery.

The report sharpens pressure over a war that has produced one of the world's gravest humanitarian catastrophes with little Western attention. Amnesty says RSF commander Gen. Hemedti had not responded by publication.

Sources: Amnesty International, Al Jazeera

Astronomy

A potentially habitable super-Earth found just 25 light-years away

+

Astronomers identified Gliese 3378b, a rocky "super-Earth" about 25 light-years away orbiting in its star's habitable zone — roughly 2.3 Earth masses, a 21.45-day orbit, receiving about 90% of the stellar radiation Earth gets from the Sun. It was found via radial-velocity data from the Habitable-zone Planet Finder (Hobby-Eberly Telescope) and the NEID spectrometer (WIYN Telescope).

Whether it holds an atmosphere is unknown — it sits near the "cosmic shoreline," where stellar radiation can strip a planet's air away. Its proximity nonetheless makes it a prime near-term target for atmospheric follow-up.

Sources: Sci.News, Space.com, NASA

Climate & the Grid

The grid becomes the bottleneck as solar heads for the top of the power mix

+

Electricity demand is rising almost everywhere — data centers, rising incomes, electrified transport and heat — and analysts (IEA, BloombergNEF) project solar will become the world's single largest electricity source within about six years. But the grid is now the binding constraint: more than 2,500 gigawatts of projects sit stalled in interconnection queues, and roughly $5.8 trillion in grid upgrades is forecast for 2026–2035.

The transition's question is shifting from "enough clean generation?" to "enough wires, queues and storage to use it?" One stopgap: batteries increasingly bridge surging data-center demand and what the grid can deliver — a workaround that also shows how far grid investment lags.

Sources: IEA Electricity 2026, BloombergNEF
