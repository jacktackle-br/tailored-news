Supreme Court upholds birthright citizenship, striking down Trump's Day-One order

The Facts On June 30, the final day of its term, the Supreme Court struck down President Trump's January 2025 executive order seeking to deny citizenship to children born in the U.S. to undocumented or temporarily present parents. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote for the Court, joined by Justice Amy Coney Barrett and the three liberal justices, holding that the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause guarantees citizenship to nearly all children born on U.S. soil: "The Framers of the Fourteenth Amendment extended that promise to every free-born person in this land. We keep that promise today." Justices Thomas, Alito and Gorsuch dissented.

A note on the count Outlets have described the outcome two ways, and both are correct. The judgment — the order is invalid — was 6–3. But on the underlying constitutional question, only five justices held that the Fourteenth Amendment itself compels birthright citizenship: Justice Kavanaugh concurred in the result on narrower statutory grounds, finding the order violated a 1950s citizenship statute rather than the Constitution, and pointedly suggested Congress could rewrite that statute. That 5–4 split on the constitutional merits is the source of the disagreement in how the case is being characterized (per SCOTUSblog and NPR).

The Left-Leaning Take Commentators at Slate and Vox framed the win as real but fragile. Their alarm centers on Kavanaugh's concurrence, which they read as "a road map for a right-wing Congress to complete Trump's failed job" — inviting lawmakers to amend the statute and create, in Slate's words, "an underclass of noncitizens who, by accident of birth, are denied basic rights" and are "detainable and deportable to countries they've never set foot in." The dissenters' "revisionist" history of the Fourteenth Amendment is treated as the deeper long-term threat.

The Right-Leaning Take The mainstream-right reaction was split and largely subdued. Much MAGA anger fell on Justice Barrett for joining the majority, while Kavanaugh — whose vote also went against Trump's order — drew far less fire because conservatives read his concurrence as handing Congress a lawful path to restrict birthright citizenship by statute (a framing amplified across the right). On the underlying policy, National Review has consistently argued the administration overreached procedurally: better to legislate than to "swat a fly with a sledgehammer" by executive fiat.

Intra-Right (anti-Trump) The Bulwark's David French argued the case was so clear it "should've been 9–0," criticizing the three full dissenters for entertaining what he views as a fringe reading of the Citizenship Clause. (Labeled as the anti-Trump right; not the mainstream-right position.)

Columnist David French (The Bulwark): the ruling vindicates a settled constitutional guarantee, and the fact that three justices dissented at all is the genuinely worrying signal — not the result itself.

Sources: NPR · CNBC · SCOTUSblog · CNN · Slate · The Bulwark

SAVE America Act revolt stalls the House, forcing an early July 4 recess

The Facts Speaker Mike Johnson sent the House into its Fourth of July recess early after a bloc of conservatives ground legislative business to a halt over the SAVE America Act, President Trump's priority bill requiring documentary proof of citizenship to register to vote and tightening mail-in ballot rules. On June 30, roughly 14 Republicans voted down a procedural rule; a coalition led by Reps. Anna Paulina Luna (R-Fla.) and Chip Roy (R-Texas) demanded the voting provisions be inserted directly into the annual defense policy bill, with Luna calling Johnson's plan "a procedural head fake." Leaders canceled Wednesday and Thursday votes; the House is scheduled to return July 13.

Context The revolt caps weeks of Trump using leverage to force the bill — canceling a signing ceremony for a bipartisan housing package and, per earlier reporting, tying it to surveillance-authority renewal. Senate Republican leaders have repeatedly told Trump the votes are not there to pass the measure, which the chamber has already failed to advance.

The Left-Leaning Take Progressive analysts (echoing the Brennan Center and Center for American Progress) call SAVE a "show your papers" voter-suppression bill, citing an estimate that more than 21 million citizens lack immediate access to documents proving citizenship and would face burdens — in some cases traveling hours in person — to register or update a registration after moving. They frame the House chaos as Trump prioritizing an unpopular, unworkable mandate over housing and defense.

The Right-Leaning Take The right is divided on tactics, not the goal. National Review's editorial line has warned the bill is not "worth the cost" and that noncitizen voting shouldn't be addressed by "swat[ting] a fly with a sledgehammer." Institutionalist conservatives fault the Luna–Roy bloc for holding the defense bill hostage; populists counter that election integrity is exactly the fight the base sent them to have.

Sources: Washington Post · CBS News · Philadelphia Inquirer · Brennan Center

Massive Russian barrage hits Kyiv; NATO neighbors scramble as Ukraine strikes Russian oil

The Facts Russia launched one of its largest aerial assaults of the war overnight into July 2 — President Zelensky said more than 70 missiles and roughly 500 drones struck Kyiv and other cities. Death tolls reported across outlets ranged from at least 17 to 25 killed, with 85-plus injured; more than 20 sites in Kyiv were hit, including residential blocks, a hotel, a research institute and an ambulance station. The exchange marks an intensifying tit-for-tat: Ukraine degrading Russia's economically vital energy sector while Russia leans on mass drone-and-missile saturation against Ukrainian cities. The spillover jitters among NATO members underscore how little margin for miscalculation remains along the alliance's eastern flank.

U.S.–Iran talks in Doha end without breakthrough as Strait of Hormuz toll fight looms

The Facts Two days of indirect U.S.–Iran talks in Doha concluded July 1 with implementation steps but no lasting breakthrough, building on an interim agreement reached two weeks earlier that paused the 2026 conflict (now past its 125th day). Negotiators agreed a goods-purchase mechanism for a first tranche of frozen Iranian assets, a communications channel to flag violations, and a shared focus on restoring reliable passage through the Strait of Hormuz. Vice President Vance said the talks were "going well."

Analysis Beneath the optimism, core disputes are unresolved: Washington rejects any Iranian-led toll mechanism in the strait while Tehran asserts joint sovereignty over the corridor with Oman; no frozen funds have actually moved; and the nuclear-inspection question persists behind vaguer "denuclearization" language. Iranian officials have signaled they intend to begin charging shipping tolls in mid-August once a toll-free window expires — a deadline that could reignite the crisis if talks stall.

Amnesty: RSF committed crimes against humanity and ethnic cleansing in Sudan's el-Fasher

The Facts A new Amnesty International report released July 1 concludes that Sudan's paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF) committed crimes against humanity and ethnic cleansing during their campaign to seize el-Fasher in North Darfur. Titled "City Under Siege, Children Under Fire," the report documents murder, forcible transfer, torture, rape, sexual slavery, enslavement, extermination and persecution against civilians between early 2024 and October 2025 — many from the Zaghawa ethnic group, whose homes RSF fighters burned after residents had fled. Amnesty says the documented acts, alongside crimes under separate investigation, "may be relevant to the crime of genocide." Findings rest on 247 interviews plus analysis of 89 open-source videos and satellite imagery.

Analysis The report sharpens international pressure over a war that has produced one of the world's gravest humanitarian catastrophes with comparatively little Western attention. Amnesty says it wrote to RSF commander Gen. Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo ("Hemedti") on June 10 detailing the findings and had received no response by publication.

Sources: Amnesty International · Al Jazeera
