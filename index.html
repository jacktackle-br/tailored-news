Midterms · Campaign Trail

At a Kentucky rally, Trump calls his second term “much more powerful” and promises a $100 million drug-plant investment if Republicans win, as his tour keeps favoring red states over swing districts

+

On Saturday Trump held a rally at Northern Kentucky University’s Truist Arena in Highland Heights. He shared the stage with Ed Gallrein, the GOP House nominee who beat Rep. Thomas Massie in the May primary, and with Andy Barr, the Republican running for Mitch McConnell’s open Senate seat. AP reports that Trump argued his current term is “much more powerful” than his first. As proof, he pointed to renaming the Gulf of Mexico, and he joked about renaming Lake Ontario “Lake America.” He also made a conditional promise: if Republicans win, the Department of War will invest $100 million in the drugmaker Antheia to revive a manufacturing plant in Winchester, Kentucky. According to AP, about half the arena applauded when he attacked Massie, who had pushed for release of the Epstein files and opposed the Iran war, while the other half stayed quiet. AP also notes that prices have risen because of tariffs and the Iran war, and that more voters now see the president as serving his own interests.

The stop fits a pattern. On Saturday NPR reported that Trump promised a swing-district blitz but has campaigned with fewer than a third of those candidates, and that his schedule leans heavily toward Republican territory. CNN framed the day as Trump stumping in deep-red Kentucky “as GOP tries to hold its turf.” The RNC and Trump campaign veterans say the rallies are meant to recreate 2024-level turnout and to reach neighboring media markets. Critics read them as defense in states that are no longer safe.

Justice Department · Congress

Rep. Ilhan Omar’s lawyers say federal agents ignored a judge’s limits when they searched her home and demand the seized property back; DOJ calls the claims false

+

In a letter dated Friday, Omar’s attorneys, Amy Jeffress and Joshua Matz, say Homeland Security Investigations agents did not follow conditions a magistrate judge set for last week’s search of her Washington home. The search is part of a Justice Department inquiry into her immigration history. The Washington Post reports that, according to the lawyers, agents did not give them the warrant and did not notify the House Office of General Counsel beforehand, as the judge’s order required. They also say Omar was kept from reaching her attorney for more than an hour. The lawyers argue those steps were meant to let congressional attorneys protect legislative work that may be exempt from seizure. CNN had previously reported that the judge ordered prosecutors to set up a screening process for that material. The letter demands that the seized property be returned and that recordings of the search be preserved.

A Justice Department spokesperson called the letter “full of false allegations” and said the government followed all court orders. Omar has not been charged. Her lawyers call the probe politically motivated, and it comes weeks before the midterms. Commentators are also asking whether the timing conflicts with the department’s long-standing practice of avoiding overt steps against candidates close to an election.

No in-window piece from Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion turned up. On the left, The Nation calls the search racist political targeting of a Somali-American Democrat. It asks why the raid has drawn none of the opinion-page condemnation that the Bolton search did. (No listed opinion-outlet pieces found.)

Ukraine · Russia

Hours after Trump shelves the Graham Act sanctions, Russia hits more than 15 Ukrainian energy sites with 332 drones and four cruise missiles, and Zelensky urges supporters to speak up for the law

+

Overnight into Saturday, Russia launched 332 attack drones and four cruise missiles at Ukraine, according to Ukraine’s air force. It said all the missiles and 289 of the drones were shot down or suppressed. Zelensky said more than 15 power generation and transmission facilities were targeted. The worst damage was in the west and center of the country, including Lutsk, Rivne, Zhytomyr and Vinnytsia. He accused Moscow of trying to cause a blackout before winter. Regional authorities said Russian attacks killed seven people and injured about 90 over the past day. The strikes come days after a large October 13–14 barrage on the grid, and despite a U.S.-proposed energy ceasefire that Moscow has not confirmed.

Zelensky responded publicly on Saturday to Trump’s executive order waiving the new sanctions and tariffs in the Lindsey Graham Act. He called the pause unfortunate but stopped short of criticizing Trump directly, writing that “this Act could have helped enormously — and still can” and that “every voice in support of it matters.” The Washington Post reports bipartisan anger in Congress. Co-author Sen. Richard Blumenthal said the waivers are about “sucking up to Vladimir Putin.” Analysis: Kyiv is appealing past the White House to Congress and the public. The timing of the strike will strengthen the argument that relief from sanctions has encouraged Moscow rather than moved it toward talks.

Sources: AP (via NPR), Oct 17, Washington Post, Oct 17, Salon, Oct 17, Kyiv Independent, Ukrinform
West Bank

A day after the Post photographer’s beating, settlers attack a convoy of French, Belgian and Polish diplomats in Taybeh; Israel’s foreign minister condemns it while Netanyahu alleges a “smear”

+

On Saturday, European diplomats visiting the olive harvest in the West Bank village of Taybeh say settlers attacked their convoy. Vehicles from the French, Belgian and Polish missions were targeted, and one diplomatic car was stoned and had its window smashed. France 24 reports that a 78-year-old French mayor traveling with the delegation was beaten by masked settlers. The Israeli military called the attack “severe,” Foreign Minister Gideon Saar condemned “severe acts of violence by Israeli rioters,” and police opened an investigation. Netanyahu disputed the accounts, saying they need “serious evidence,” and described a smear campaign against settlers. Separately, Washington Post photojournalist Heidi Levine, who was beaten near Abu Falah on Friday, had surgery at Beilinson Hospital on Saturday and is in moderate condition. She says soldiers watched the attack and did not step in. Israeli police are investigating, and no arrests have been reported.

Analysis: Attacks on Western journalists and diplomats in consecutive days make this harvest season’s violence a diplomatic problem for Israel’s European partners. Germany’s foreign minister, Johann Wadephul, visited Taybeh earlier and called settler violence “terror.” Berlin backs more EU sanctions on violent settlers. The split between Saar’s condemnation and Netanyahu’s denial also shows a divide inside Israel’s government over how to respond.

Guns · Oregon

Police credit a teacher and an armed father with stopping the Bend school shooter, a 16-year-old former student who used a shotgun his mother bought, as Oregon’s gun debate reopens

+

On Thursday a gunman shot his way through the glass doors of Silver Rail Elementary School in Bend, Oregon. In a special-needs classroom he killed three children, ages 5, 8 and 11, and a 25-year-old teacher, Megan Lambert. A 7-year-old and a 54-year-old teacher were critically wounded. On Friday police identified the shooter as Griffin J. John II, 16, a former special-needs student at the school. Police say a teacher tackled him and tried to take the gun. Kaylub Giamanco, 36, the father of two students, has a concealed-carry permit; he followed the shooter inside and shot him dead. The shooter was dead when police arrived, about three minutes after the first 911 call. The Washington Post reports that police credit the teacher and the father with likely preventing more deaths. The school had no resource officer.

Police say the weapon was a Maverick 88 pump-action 12-gauge shotgun. Law-enforcement sources told CBS and NBC that his mother bought it around his birthday in August. Police say how he got the gun and ammunition is still under investigation, and no motive has been given. Bend-La Pine Schools bars guns on school grounds, a policy it adopted under a 2021 state law. Gov. Tina Kotek praised the staff, first responders and the armed bystander. Sen. Ron Wyden called for gun laws that would prevent more school shootings. Measure 114, the permit-to-purchase law voters passed in 2022, is still waiting on an Oregon Supreme Court ruling.

Midterms · Money

Democratic Senate candidates outraise Republicans about 4-to-1 in the battlegrounds, led by Talarico’s record $64 million, as Musk’s $158 million carries the GOP super PAC to its own record

+

Third-quarter FEC reports, filed Thursday night, show Democratic Senate candidates far ahead in direct fundraising. NBC News tallied $253 million raised from July through September by Democrats in more than a dozen competitive races, compared with $56 million for their Republican opponents. In Texas, James Talarico’s campaign reported $64 million, the most any Senate candidate has raised in a quarter. Ken Paxton reported $18 million, and Talarico ended September with $14.6 million on hand to Paxton’s $4.9 million. In Georgia, Jon Ossoff out-raised Rep. Mike Collins by about $33 million to $3 million. In Kansas, Democrat Adam Hamilton raised $9.9 million to Sen. Roger Marshall’s $866,000. Outlets disagree on the totals. Axios says Democrats out-raised Republicans in every race, while Newsweek counts Minnesota as an exception. Roll Call’s figure for Talarico’s campaign committee alone is $60.2 million.

Outside money is where Republicans lead. The Senate Leadership Fund raised a record $272.9 million in the quarter. That included $158 million from Elon Musk in two September checks, and filings show the fund passed matching amounts to Texas PAC, which is backing Paxton. Its Democratic counterpart, Senate Majority PAC, also set a record at $151 million, but Musk’s gift alone exceeded that. AdImpact said early this month that Republicans had outspent Democrats by $188 million on ads in Senate battlegrounds. NPR reports that total midterm ad spending passed $10 billion this week.

Russia · Sanctions

Two days before they take effect, Trump waives nearly all the new Russia sanctions and tariffs in the Graham Act, a law he signed last month, and Moscow calls it “historic”

+

Late on October 16, Trump signed an executive order waiving almost all of the new sanctions, tariffs and restrictions in the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026. The measures were due to take effect on October 18. The law would have allowed tariffs of up to 100% on goods from the largest buyers of Russian oil and gas. It also limited the president’s ability to lift existing Russia sanctions. Trump used the law’s own waiver clause, certifying to Congress that a waiver is “in the national interests of the United States.” He gave no reason, and the waiver stays in place until the administration decides “further action is appropriate.” Sanctions already imposed under earlier executive orders remain. Bloomberg reports the new measures had complicated Trump’s push to bring down diesel prices before the midterms. The waiver follows a deal he announced last week to let Russia resume diesel exports.

Kirill Dmitriev, Putin’s economic envoy, called the waiver “an important and truly historic moment” and urged closer U.S.-Russian business ties. Members of both parties objected. Sen. Richard Blumenthal condemned it, Sen. Elizabeth Warren called it an “abject and predictable failure in leadership,” and Republican Rep. Don Bacon said Trump “is prolonging the war, not ending it.” EU leaders said Europe’s sanctions will keep tightening until Moscow stops the war. Relations between Washington and Kyiv were already at a low after Ukraine resumed strikes on Russian refineries. Trump also told Newsmax that he finds Zelensky harder to deal with than Putin.

Sources: CNN, Oct 16, CNBC, Oct 17, Bloomberg, Oct 17, RFE/RL, The Hill, CBS News

AI · Export Controls

China proposes export controls on 23 AI technologies, including frontier model weights, agents and sub-14nm chip designs, with an interim registration rule already in force

+

On Friday, China’s commerce and science ministries proposed adding 40 technologies to the country’s export-control catalogue, covering AI, biomedicine and photovoltaics. Fourteen would be prohibited and 26 restricted. The 23 AI items include large models above performance thresholds, along with their weights and source code, as well as autonomous agents, computing clusters and chip designs below 14 nanometers. AI tools that can write or modify malware on their own to attack infrastructure, the military or government would be banned from export entirely. A temporary registration requirement covering all the listed technologies took effect immediately. Public comments close November 15.

Outlets describe this as Beijing’s first attempt to control AI software assets directly, not just hardware. API access and public open-weight releases are largely exempt, and open-weight releases reportedly get automatic approval on registration. Some outlets report thresholds of 2 trillion parameters, 1026 FLOPs of training compute, or a global top-10 ranking. Others say the thresholds have not been published, so those figures are unconfirmed. The commerce ministry said the measures are routine and “not directed at a particular country, region or company,” adding that it wants to ensure AI “remain[s] safe and controllable.”

Sources: Bloomberg, Oct 16, Taipei Times, Oct 17, Law360, The Decoder, OODA Loop
Offshore Wind

Administration formally asks a judge to send Maryland’s US Wind permit back for reconsideration, a week after moving to rescind approval for the nearly finished Revolution Wind

+

Justice Department lawyers asked U.S. District Judge Stephanie Gallagher in Baltimore to send US Wind’s construction permit, for its project off Ocean City, Maryland, back to the Interior Department for reconsideration. The government wants the remand “without vacatur,” so the permit would stay in place during the review. It cited possible harm to North Atlantic right whales, scenic views, commercial fisheries and scientific research, and argued the project was not adequately reviewed under the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act. The Town of Ocean City welcomed the filing. US Wind said it is confident the courts will uphold its permits.

On October 9, Interior made a similar request to rescind its three-year-old approval of Revolution Wind off Rhode Island. That 704-megawatt project installed its 65th and final turbine in September. Interior says the filings follow court briefing schedules and do not signal a change in offshore wind policy. Grist counts three Atlantic projects targeted in a week, and Heatmap and a Washington Post editorial warn the campaign could sink the bipartisan permitting-reform deal now in the Senate.

Oncology

European regulators recommend approval of RevMed’s daraxonrasib, the first drug to target the RAS mutations behind most pancreatic cancers, which roughly doubled survival in its Phase 3 trial

+

The European Medicines Agency’s drug committee (CHMP) has issued a positive opinion on Revolution Medicines’ Rasonque (daraxonrasib), a once-daily pill. It would be used by adults with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma who have already had at least one systemic therapy. In the Phase 3 RASolute 302 trial, median overall survival was 13.2 months on the drug versus 6.7 months on chemotherapy, a 60% lower risk of death (hazard ratio 0.40). The FDA approved the drug on August 26. The opinion came through an expedited, phased review under the EMA’s Cancer Medicines Pathfinder pilot.

The European Commission now decides on authorization. A yes would cover all EU member states plus Iceland, Norway and Liechtenstein. Pancreatic cancer is among the deadliest major cancers. More than 90% of cases carry a KRAS mutation, a target that resisted drug developers for decades. Daraxonrasib inhibits the active form of several RAS variants, which is why oncologists see it as the start of a new class of treatment, not just a new drug.
