Senate · Iran war · War powers

Senate rejects an Iran war-powers resolution 48–51 in its first vote on the war since the midterms

+

On Thursday the Senate voted 48–51 against a House-passed measure, H.Con.Res. 93. It would have invoked the 1973 War Powers Resolution to direct President Trump to withdraw U.S. forces from hostilities against Iran. Republican Sens. Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski and Rand Paul voted with Democrats. Sen. John Fetterman of Pennsylvania was the only Democrat to vote no. Sen. Chris Murphy, who is recovering from a stroke, did not vote. Sen. Thom Tillis, who backed earlier war-powers efforts, voted with his party this time. He called the resolution a “political exercise” but signaled he could support cutting war funding. Reports differ on the day: one version of The Hill’s story says Wednesday, while CBS and AP say Thursday.

This was the first war-powers vote in either chamber since Democrats won both houses on November 3. It was largely a signaling vote: Trump would almost certainly have vetoed the measure, and he ignored a similar resolution Congress cleared in June. Democrats have forced more than a dozen such votes since the war began in late February. Their real leverage over war funding starts when the new Congress is seated in January.

Senate · Defense budget · Shutdown watch

Senate Republicans shelve Trump’s push for a party-line $350 billion Pentagon bill and look to Democrats first

+

Senate Majority Leader John Thune said after meeting his conference that Republicans will first try the regular bipartisan appropriations route for President Trump’s $350 billion defense request. They are holding off on a party-line reconciliation bill that would need no Democratic votes. Thune said it is still an “open question” whether Republicans even have the votes to pass the package on their own. Appropriations Chair Susan Collins and Sen. Thom Tillis prefer the bipartisan route, and Tillis warned that another party-line fight could trigger a shutdown. The government funding deadline is December 11. The Washington Post described the move as Republicans breaking with Trump. Punchbowl News called the reconciliation effort all but abandoned, but other reports say it remains an option.

The request includes $47 billion for munitions production, $17 billion for the Golden Dome missile-defense system and more than $48 billion for critical minerals. A separate $87.6 billion supplemental request is tied to the Iran war. Democrats want a stopgap bill that pushes the fight into the new Congress, a substantially smaller figure, and guardrails on the war. Republicans’ 53–47 Senate margin also narrows on December 15, when Sen. Jon Husted’s term ends.

Courts · Immigration · TPS

Administration withdraws its Supreme Court bid after the Ninth Circuit keeps Venezuelans’ protected status frozen

+

On Wednesday, hours after Solicitor General D. John Sauer asked the Supreme Court to step in, the Ninth Circuit extended its administrative stay of U.S. District Judge Edward Chen’s order restoring Temporary Protected Status for Venezuelans. Chen’s order had been due to take effect Friday. The stay now lasts until seven days after the appeals court issues its decision. On Thursday Sauer withdrew the Supreme Court application (No. 26A714), telling the clerk the government would come back if it loses at the Ninth Circuit. Some outlets still describe the application as pending.

In practice, Venezuelan former TPS holders remain without work authorization and can still be detained and deported while the case goes on. The Justice Department had said restoring TPS could force the release of about 1,800 detained Venezuelans. The administration argues the Supreme Court settled the question in its June ruling in the Haiti and Syria TPS cases. This was the administration’s third trip to the justices over Venezuelan TPS.

International law · Myanmar · Rohingya

World Court finds Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya, the first time it has held a state responsible under the Genocide Convention

+

The International Court of Justice ruled on Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya during its 2017 “clearance operations” in northern Rakhine State. The court cited killings, serious bodily and mental harm, and measures to prevent births, carried out with intent to destroy the group in substantial part. It also found Myanmar failed in its duty to prevent genocide, and it rejected Myanmar’s counterinsurgency defense. One outlet reports the genocide finding was 9–6; this brief could not confirm that split in a Tier-1 source. The Gambia brought the case in 2019 on behalf of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation. The court ordered Myanmar to make reparations, including enabling the Rohingya to return safely and prosecuting those responsible. Compensation will be set in later proceedings, and Myanmar must report back on its compliance. Myanmar has consistently denied genocide.

Analysis: The ruling’s weight is mostly legal and diplomatic. The ICJ has no enforcement power and can only refer non-compliance to the UN Security Council, where China and Russia have shielded Myanmar’s military. The judgment’s standard for inferring genocidal intent will be read closely in other pending genocide cases before the court. Bangladesh, which hosts about a million Rohingya refugees, welcomed the ruling. Analysts caution, however, that the ruling does not by itself make Rakhine, now largely held by the Arakan Army, safe for returns.

Arctic · Denmark · Greenland · U.S.

Danish parliament ratifies the U.S.–Greenland security deal 109–0, aiming to end the standoff over Trump’s designs on the island

+

Denmark’s parliament on Thursday unanimously ratified the trilateral security agreement with the United States and Greenland, 109–0 among members present. Greenland’s legislature approved it earlier this month. The deal was signed in New York on September 22 and supplements the 1951 defense accord. It lets the U.S. build two new bases on Greenland. Only NATO members may set up military sites there, and any further locations need approval from both Copenhagen and Nuuk. It takes effect once Denmark’s foreign ministry notifies Washington. Greenland also plans a law to screen outside investors on security grounds.

Analysis: Copenhagen is trading expanded, treaty-bound U.S. access for an end to Trump’s pressure to take control of the island. Some experts argue the deal adds little to the access the U.S. already had. Its real value for Denmark is in limiting Washington’s role to a negotiated framework that requires Danish and Greenlandic consent.

Courts · Washington · Monuments

Judge Chutkan extends her block on Trump’s 250-foot Memorial Circle arch by two weeks while she weighs an injunction

+

U.S. District Judge Tanya Chutkan on Wednesday extended for 14 days her order barring the administration from breaking ground on a planned 250-foot arch at Memorial Circle, the traffic circle by the Arlington Memorial Bridge near Arlington National Cemetery. The order had been due to lapse on Thursday and now runs until at least December 3 while she decides on requests for a preliminary injunction. The challengers include D.C., Virginia, Vietnam War veterans and an architectural historian, who argue that the arch would harm both jurisdictions and needs congressional approval. The administration says Congress authorized a monument at Memorial Circle decades ago. A separate suit by the Arlington County Board goes to a federal judge in Virginia for arguments on Friday. Wednesday’s order does not cancel the project or resolve the lawsuits.

China · Russia · U.S. · Nuclear arms

Trump, Xi and Putin sign a four-point Shenzhen declaration saying “a nuclear war cannot be won,” with nothing on Ukraine

+

The first three-way summit of the American, Chinese and Russian leaders took place on Thursday on the sidelines of APEC in Shenzhen, and Trump has since left China. China and Russia published identical texts of a joint declaration with a preamble and four points, on UN reform, petroleum supplies, the International Criminal Court, and nuclear risk. The three reaffirmed that “a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought” and agreed to “engage in dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing nuclear proliferation.” The ICC passage stresses state consent and the immunity of officials, and says such courts should not abuse their jurisdiction. Commentators linked it to the court’s 2023 arrest warrant for Putin. Xi called the meeting “positive, constructive, fruitful.” Trump and Putin also met one-on-one for 42 minutes.

The declaration does not mention Ukraine, although Trump had said he wanted China and the U.S. to help end the war. On Iran, Trump said the U.S. and China share an interest in oil flowing through the Strait of Hormuz and that “we’re not going to let Iran have a nuclear weapon.” He called for nuclear arms reductions and remarked that countries “only need about 10” nuclear weapons, while acknowledging that others would be hard to persuade. Before the meeting, analysts quoted by France 24 had expected more symbolism than breakthroughs, and nothing reported so far goes beyond shared principles and a promise of further talks.

Tech · AI safety · Government audit

GAO investigators got ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok to produce “viable” terror attack plans in under 12 hours

+

The Government Accountability Office on Wednesday released a 61-page covert-testing report (GAO-27-108814) in which investigators posing as would-be attackers got all four leading chatbots to produce attack plans in under 12 hours. Two of the systems produced plans for a mass-casualty improvised explosive device aimed at a hotel parking garage and a convention center, and others produced plans against the power grid. By “viable,” the GAO means a person “would likely be able to carry out the actions.” It did not test every step, made no recommendations, and concluded that no current generative AI system is completely immune to such misuse. The report does not say which platform produced which plan.

Google, OpenAI and SpaceXAI said the GAO tested earlier models that have since been improved. Anthropic said it does not dispute that a determined tester who lies about their purpose can extract harmful content and that it takes the finding seriously, but that it could not verify the report’s findings. Sen. Maggie Hassan, who will chair the relevant committee in the new Congress, said AI risks will be the subject of one of her first hearings in January.

Climate · COP31 · Antalya

COP31 enters its last day awaiting a new draft, with “phase out fossil fuels” facing rejection by the Arab Group and Russia

+

Negotiators at the UN climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, were waiting on Thursday for a revised agreement text ahead of the scheduled close. Australia’s Chris Bowen, who leads the negotiations, has said at least one more draft will come. The 18-page first draft from November 16 has two fossil-fuel passages: one calls for “accelerated action to phase out fossil fuels, including through the immediate transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems,” and the other restates the 2023 Dubai pledge to “transition away.” The Arab Group and Russia rejected the draft outright, and Saudi Arabia objected to any reference to Brazil’s roadmap for moving away from fossil fuels. The EU’s Wopke Hoekstra said the draft “does not go in the right direction.”

The talks will show whether last year’s outcome repeats: the Belém summit ended with no mention of fossil fuels after producer countries blocked it. Other disputes still open include Turkey’s proposal to raise electricity to 35% of energy use by 2035, which several governments oppose as a new sectoral target, and how much adaptation finance rich countries should provide. Bowen has called the text something to be “whittled down,” and the reports differ on whether the deadline falls on Thursday or Friday.

Astronomy · Stellar evolution · Andromeda

Astronomers report the first direct observation of a star spiralling to its death inside a giant companion’s envelope

+

A paper in Nature this week describes AT 2025abao, a transient in the Andromeda Galaxy, as a “common envelope” event. In this stage of binary-star evolution, one star is swallowed by its swollen companion. Here a roughly 0.7-solar-mass star entered the outer layers of a roughly 7-solar-mass asymptotic-giant-branch star around 2018 and spiralled inward over seven years, which explains the system’s steady brightening. A bright eruption in late 2025 is read as the companion merging with the giant’s core, throwing off roughly the outer tenth of the envelope.

Common-envelope evolution is thought to produce many close binaries, including the progenitors of some gravitational-wave sources, but it has mostly been inferred from its aftermath rather than watched as it happens. The interpretation depends on modelling: a preprint version notes that the brightening could instead have been a brief pulse of nuclear energy. The authors expect new survey telescopes to find more such events.
