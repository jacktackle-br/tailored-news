Justice Department · Virginia

Federal prosecutors indict Virginia state Sen. L. Louise Lucas, a leading Trump critic, on marijuana racketeering charges 12 days before the midterms

+

An eight-count indictment unsealed Thursday in the Eastern District of Virginia charges Lucas, 82, and six others, including two of her daughters and a grandson. Lucas is a Portsmouth Democrat who chairs the state Senate Finance and Appropriations Committee and led the state’s redistricting push, which the Virginia Supreme Court later blocked. Lucas is named in five of the counts, which include racketeering conspiracy, conspiracy to distribute marijuana, money-laundering conspiracy, conspiracy to commit honest-services fraud and maintaining drug-involved premises. Prosecutors say that since at least 2019 a family “Lucas Enterprise” ran an illegal cannabis business, worth about $4.5 million over five years, through Portsmouth storefronts. They also say Lucas used her committee post to protect the business and steer legislation and regulation in its favor. She surrendered Thursday morning.

Lucas denies the charges and calls the case political: “I will not be intimidated. I will not be bullied. And I will not back down.” Attorney General Todd Blanche said the investigation began “many years ago,” before Trump returned to office, and that there was “nothing improper” about announcing charges this close to an election. Critics pointed to the department’s long-standing practice of avoiding overt steps against politicians close to an election. Lucas is not on the Nov. 3 ballot. These are charges, not a conviction.

The Left-Leaning Take

Democrats and Lucas’s lawyer see a Justice Department led by Trump allies pursuing one of the president’s most outspoken state-level opponents, the architect of a Democratic redistricting counter-push, and doing so publicly in the final stretch of a campaign. In their view, the attorney general’s dismissal of election-season norms is itself the warning sign. (No in-window Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion piece found; summary drawn from Lucas’s statement and Democratic reaction as reported.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Administration defenders say the case is a years-old public-corruption investigation built on a grand-jury indictment, and that a powerful appropriator allegedly protecting a family drug business should not get immunity because she criticizes the president. (No in-window National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason piece found; summary reflects the Justice Department’s statements as reported.)

Elections · Courts

A federal judge bars DHS from election-related door-knocking on likely citizens in nine states, finding its noncitizen-voter lists unreliable; the order takes effect Friday evening unless appealed

+

Late Wednesday, U.S. District Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín of San Francisco issued a preliminary injunction that bars the Department of Homeland Security from election-related field operations and interviews in California, Connecticut, Georgia, Missouri, Nevada, New York, Pennsylvania, Washington and Wisconsin. The order applies when agents know, suspect or reasonably should suspect that the person is a U.S. citizen. It also bars operations that rest entirely on records produced by DHS’s voter-matching process. She wrote that “there is a high likelihood of error” because “there is no federal database containing citizenship status of U.S. residents.” She also found that the plaintiffs are likely to show a violation of the Voting Rights Act’s ban on voter intimidation. The plaintiffs are National Nurses United, the California Nurses Association, Voto Latino Foundation, San Francisco and Santa Clara County.

DHS can still investigate people it suspects are noncitizens. The judge paused her order until 5 p.m. Friday so the administration can appeal, and it expires after Election Day. A DHS spokesperson said the agency does not plan to investigate voters it believes are citizens.

The Left-Leaning Take

Voting-rights groups say the ruling confirms that federal agents knocking on the doors of naturalized citizens on the basis of error-prone lists, weeks before an election, works as intimidation whatever the stated purpose. They tie it to a pattern of fraud claims that have not held up in court. (No in-window Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion piece found; summary drawn from plaintiffs and allied groups as reported.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Conservatives and the administration say that checking whether noncitizens are on voter rolls is basic election integrity. They argue that a Biden-appointed judge has hobbled the effort in battleground states days before the vote, and that the department wants to “guarantee” only eligible people vote. (No in-window National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason piece found; summary reflects government statements and conservative news coverage.)

Iran War · Oil

Trump accuses Iran of inventing “fake hits, mines” in Hormuz to push up oil before the midterms, as Brent settles about 4% higher near $113

+

On Thursday, after Iran’s Revolutionary Guard said seven tankers had been hit overnight, President Trump wrote on Truth Social that Tehran was making up “fake hits, mines, and everything else” to drive up oil prices ahead of the Nov. 3 vote. He also said more oil is now moving through the strait than before the war. Iran has given no ship names, flags or casualty figures, and the full claim has not been independently confirmed. The available data do not support Trump’s description of shipping volumes. The UK Maritime Trade Operations agency has logged more than two dozen attacks on ships in October, a monthly record, and says most targeted vessels do not report incidents. Daily transits remain far below prewar levels. The Washington Post reported that the strikes are raising fears of a return to all-out war.

Bloomberg reported that Brent settled up about 4% near $113, its biggest gain in about two weeks, though AP put the rise at 2.6% to $111.66. Traders also cited deadly Houthi attacks in Saudi Arabia (below). Analysis: The president is now openly disputing whether the attacks are happening, while markets price them in on Iran’s word alone. That gap makes independent maritime confirmation the key fact to watch, and it shows how directly the Hormuz standoff now bears on U.S. gasoline prices in the last days of the campaign.

Saudi Arabia · Houthis

The U.S. orders non-emergency diplomats and families out of Saudi Arabia after Houthi strikes on airports and oil sites, including an attack that killed two at Dammam’s airport

+

The State Department ordered non-emergency U.S. government employees and their families to leave Saudi Arabia. A spokesman cited attacks by the Iranian-backed Houthis on civilian airports and infrastructure. On Wednesday evening, an attack on King Fahd International Airport in Dammam killed two people and injured eight, according to Saudi civil-aviation authorities, and halted flights until Thursday morning. The energy ministry separately said debris from a drone intercepted over the Eastern Province killed two people. The Houthis claimed strikes the same day on the Defense Ministry headquarters in Riyadh and on Aramco facilities in Yanbu. Reports put this month’s Houthi strikes on Saudi airports at about 21 dead. Some Saudi officials blame Tehran, and Iran denies any role. CBS reported that the move also reduces the U.S. footprint in the region.

Analysis: A second front on the Arabian Peninsula, aimed at civilian aviation and energy, increases pressure on Gulf exports already cut by the Hormuz closure. It also places Riyadh, a key U.S. partner, directly in the line of fire as Washington and Israel weigh whether to resume major operations against Iran.

Elections · Intelligence

The top Intelligence Committee Democrats say they are planning for Trump to claim foreign meddling after Nov. 3, and the U.S. Embassy in Ottawa accuses Canada of “potential interference”

+

Sen. Mark Warner and Rep. Jim Himes, the top Democrats on the Senate and House intelligence committees, told NPR on Wednesday that they are working on the assumption that President Trump will not accept the midterm results and will cite foreign interference as his reason. Himes said such a claim could be used “as an excuse to seize ballot boxes” or to question the election’s validity. The two said they have told Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton and other intelligence leaders to be reachable by phone on and after Election Day. They also said the administration has not delivered the pre-election threat reports the law requires at 120, 60 and 30 days out. So far they have received one classified report. Warner said it shows Russia again working against Democrats, with Iran and China acting differently. Himes drew a “profound distinction” between influence campaigns and attacks on voting infrastructure. The ODNI did not immediately comment. The White House said Trump has done more than any other president to ensure free and fair elections. A day earlier, the Washington Post reported that a small group of national-security officials has been quietly advising governors, election officials and lawmakers of both parties on how to prepare for possible federal interference.

Also on Wednesday, the U.S. Embassy in Ottawa released a fact sheet listing what it called “potential” Canadian interference in the midterms. It cites statements by Ontario Premier Doug Ford and Industry Minister Mélanie Joly, meetings between Canadian ministers and U.S. Democrats, and calls for retaliatory tariffs aimed at politically sensitive states, CBC reports. Prime Minister Mark Carney denied any attempt to sway the U.S. vote. He said Canada’s trade measures respond to U.S. tariffs and are meant to protect Canadian workers. The fact sheet follows remarks Ambassador Pete Hoekstra made last week warning Canadian politicians to stay out of American politics. Election Day is Nov. 3.

The Left-Leaning Take

Democrats argue that the administration is setting up a pretext. In their view, skipping the required threat briefings while naming a close ally as a possible meddler leaves room to discredit an unfavorable result. They point to the Justice Department’s run of court losses over voter-roll demands as evidence that the fraud claims have not held up. The Right-Leaning Take

Administration allies say that naming foreign efforts to influence U.S. voters, including those of a friendly government campaigning against U.S. tariffs, is the government doing its job, and that Democrats are pre-litigating a loss they fear. (No in-window National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason piece found; summary reflects administration and embassy statements as reported. Supreme Court · Border

The Trump administration asks the Supreme Court to let it resume border-wall work in Texas’s Big Bend region, which a judge halted on Oct. 2

+

On Wednesday, Solicitor General D. John Sauer filed an emergency application asking the justices to stay an injunction from U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone of El Paso, a George W. Bush appointee. Her Oct. 2 order bars the Department of Homeland Security from building barriers or doing “any construction or ground disturbing activities” in the Big Bend Sector. Cardone found the challengers likely to succeed in arguing that the environmental-law waivers issued between February and July by then-Secretary Kristi Noem and current Secretary Markwayne Mullin exceed the secretary’s authority under the major-questions doctrine. She refused to pause her order while the government appealed.

Sauer called the injunction “baseless.” He argues that Congress clearly authorized both building barriers and waiving laws to speed them up, that the district court lacked power to hear the major-questions claim, and that the government’s interest in keeping “a quarter of the border” from becoming a magnet for cartels outweighs local objections. He also asks the Court to decide the merits now. The plaintiffs are the Center for Biological Diversity, a church-preservation group and a local river guide. They and other ranchers and residents say the area has historically low illegal crossings and that the project would damage protected landscapes. A separate federal judge ruled for the government in a nearby Presidio-area case. The Court has not acted.

The Left-Leaning Take

Environmental groups and local opponents say the waiver power is being stretched to pave over one of the country’s most remote landscapes, in an area that does not need a wall, and that the major-questions doctrine conservatives used against student-loan relief should apply just as much here. (No in-window Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion piece found; summary drawn from plaintiffs’ statements as reported.)

The Right-Leaning Take

The administration’s view is that Congress settled the question in 1996 by giving the DHS secretary broad waiver authority, and that a single district judge should not be able to stop border construction across hundreds of miles. (No in-window National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason piece found; summary reflects the government’s filing as reported.)

Campaign 2026 · Senate

Trump rallies at Clemson for Sen. Darline Graham, who is running even with Democrat Annie Andrews and has split with him over the Russia sanctions waiver

+

On Wednesday night President Trump held a rally at Clemson University’s arena in Pendleton, S.C., for Sen. Darline Graham, the sister of the late Sen. Lindsey Graham. The governor appointed her to finish her brother’s term, and Trump then endorsed her in a new primary. Less than two weeks before the election, polls show her about even with Democrat Annie Andrews in a state Republicans usually win easily. Protesters interrupted Trump’s speech several times, and he told the crowd that Lindsey Graham had thought highly of his sister.

The race has become tied up with Trump’s Oct. 16 executive order waiving the Russia penalties in the sanctions law named for Lindsey Graham, two days before they were to take effect. Those penalties included tariffs of up to 500% on Russian goods and up to 100% on major buyers of Russian oil. Darline Graham has said she would have preferred to keep the sanctions. Several Republican senators went further: Thom Tillis called the waiver “beyond irresponsible,” and Bill Cassidy said Reagan and Graham would be “ashamed.” Ted Cruz said it was a mistake, and John Cornyn wrote that “Lindsey would be appalled.” The White House says enforcing the sanctions would get in the way of peace talks with Russia.

The Left-Leaning Take

Slate argues that with the midterms 13 days away and polls pointing toward a Republican wipeout, Trump has chosen to fight his own party over Putin. In that reading, the Graham-sanctions waiver hands Democrats an issue in a seat that should be safe and forces endangered Republicans to pick between Trump and their own records on Russia.

The Right-Leaning Take

Much of the conservative opinion press has broken with Trump on this. The Wall Street Journal editorial board, writing after the waiver last week (outside this window), called him “Putin’s best friend in the world” and said he had “stuck a shiv in the back” of Graham’s legacy. A National Review Corner post said the waivers keep coming even as “scrutiny of his aid to Putin intensifies.” Administration defenders, including Energy Secretary Chris Wright, say the waiver is leverage to bring Moscow to the table. (No in-window Dispatch or Reason piece found.)

Russia · Ukraine

Hours after agreeing to new talks, Russia fires 21 ballistic missiles at Kyiv in three waves, and Zelenskyy says there are “no real changes” in Moscow

+

Overnight into Thursday, Russia hit Kyiv with ballistic missiles and drones. Ukraine’s air force reported 21 ballistic missiles in three waves, none intercepted, along with about 210 drones. Ukrainian authorities first put the toll in the capital at one dead and 22 wounded, AP reported. Zelenskyy later said 34 people were injured in Kyiv and the regions, including five children, with two deaths known. Russia’s Defense Ministry said it downed 808 Ukrainian drones aimed at Moscow and other cities overnight.

The barrage came less than a day after the Kremlin said Vladimir Putin, following talks with U.S. envoy Steve Witkoff in St. Petersburg, had agreed to restart technical-level three-way talks on or after Nov. 10, possibly in Abu Dhabi. It would be the first round since February. Zelenskyy backed the talks but said the strike was a deliberate show of force and that Moscow’s stance had not changed. Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov said some details of the U.S. framework “do not entirely satisfy us,” and Russia is still demanding that Ukraine shrink its military and renounce NATO membership. Analysis: The timing looks like Moscow’s usual approach of talking while escalating, which raises the stakes for Washington as it tries to get both sides to the table without new leverage over Russia.

Sources: AP via KSAT, Oct 22, NBC News (Reuters), ABC News, CNN, Oct 21, AP via West Hawaii Today, Oct 22
Iran War · Strait of Hormuz

Iranian state TV says seven tankers were set on fire or disabled overnight near Hormuz, which would be the most in one day since the war began, and Brent jumps about 3% to above $112

+

Iranian state television said Thursday that seven oil tankers were hit overnight in the Strait of Hormuz, AP reported. According to the Revolutionary Guard’s navy, four caught fire after being targeted while transferring oil, and three were disabled by naval mines. If confirmed, it would be the largest number of vessels damaged in a single day since the war began on Feb. 28. Iran gave no information on casualties or the ships’ flags, and the claim had not been independently confirmed. The Guard also threatened U.S. bases in the region if Washington tries to escort tankers through other parts of the strait. On Wednesday, UK maritime monitors reported a tanker hit by multiple projectiles, and Iran said mines had stopped three tankers using routes it considers unauthorized. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said the strait will not fully reopen until the U.S. accepts Tehran’s terms, and that indirect talks continue through Pakistan and Qatar.

Brent settled Wednesday at $108.82 and rose about 3% to roughly $112.27 on Thursday after the report, while Asian stock markets fell. Kpler data put oil flows out of the Gulf at about 8.5 million barrels a day, down from more than 20 million two weeks ago. Goldman Sachs says average crude freight rates are above $20 a barrel, compared with $3 a year ago, and attributes about $7 of that to war-risk premiums for crossing Hormuz. Analysis: With Gulf exports down by more than half, each new attack on a tanker now moves prices sharply, and the Hormuz standoff is increasingly where the war is fought.

Sources: AP via WRAL, AP via Durango Herald, CNBC, Oct 21, AP via ABC News, The National, Oct 21
Israel · Election

In the final week before Israel’s Oct. 27 election, Eisenkot’s Yashar! leads Likud in the polls, but neither bloc is close to a majority and neither has a clear plan to end Hamas rule in Gaza

+

AP describes a tight race between Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s Likud and former army chief Gadi Eisenkot’s centrist Yashar! party. Eisenkot is campaigning in outlying towns and traditional Likud strongholds to make Yashar! the largest party, and Netanyahu is touring his base, running on security, the economy and President Trump’s endorsement. A Walla poll published Oct. 20 gave Yashar! 23 seats to Likud’s 19, with Netanyahu’s camp at 48 seats, well short of the 61 needed for a majority. Both leaders promise to end Hamas control of Gaza, but AP reports that neither has a clear way to do it. Eisenkot says he could do it within 18 months, through Trump’s ceasefire plan or military force.

Security issues are part of the closing days of the campaign. Haaretz and the Times of Israel report that Israeli officials have told U.S. counterparts Hamas has no intention of disarming and has repaired tunnels and rebuilt weapons stocks in the areas it still controls. Defense Minister Israel Katz has ordered expanded intelligence-gathering ahead of the vote. Israeli strikes on Tuesday killed seven people in Gaza, according to the territory’s civil-defense service. Analysis: With both blocs short of 61, small parties clearing the electoral threshold could decide the result, and any coalition talks will open with the Gaza ceasefire under strain.

Tech · EVs & Autonomy

Tesla’s third-quarter profit falls about 43% on flat $28 billion revenue, and Musk says robotaxi revenue probably won’t show up until the second quarter of 2027

+

Tesla reported third-quarter results Wednesday. Revenue was about $28.05 billion, flat from a year earlier and close to analysts’ $27.92 billion estimate. Adjusted earnings of 48 cents a share beat the expected 45 cents, and free cash flow was about $1.53 billion. Net profit still fell sharply, to about $787 million (down roughly 43%), as spending rose, though auto margins improved. Active Full Self-Driving subscriptions reached 1.74 million, up 67%, a figure that includes upfront purchases. Musk said self-driving is now the biggest driver of demand.

Musk said the robotaxi service would not “move the needle” this quarter and would probably start showing up in revenue in the second quarter of 2027. Tesla says the service has logged nearly 2 million cumulative unsupervised miles and will expand from Austin to Dallas next month. Executives said they still need key regulatory approvals to scale robotaxis and FSD, and they were cautious about robotics and chipmaking plans. The stock rose at first after the report, then fell about 3% after hours to roughly $374.

Energy Policy · Offshore Wind

The administration asks a judge to let it reconsider the federal permit for the 2-gigawatt New England Wind project, the fourth wind farm it has targeted, putting the Senate’s permitting deal at risk

+

On Tuesday, federal defendants in a case over New England Wind, a 2-gigawatt project off Massachusetts approved in 2024, asked a federal judge to send the permit back to the Interior Department for a new decision, AP reports. It is the fourth offshore wind project the administration has gone after this way in recent months. The same approach was used against the nearly finished Revolution Wind and against SouthCoast Wind’s construction and operations plan, along with other Biden-era approvals that have not begun construction.

The move complicates a bipartisan Senate permitting agreement reached in September, which has not yet come to a vote. Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse, one of its authors, said: “This is how you blow up a permitting deal.” Erik Milito, president of the National Ocean Industries Association, said the actions “underscore why Congress needs to get this done.” Developers have argued for months that permits that can be revoked after the fact are making U.S. offshore wind hard to finance, at a time when electricity demand from data centers is rising.
