Russia · Sanctions · Congress

Four GOP senators join Democrats in demanding Trump “immediately reverse course” on his Russian diesel deal, while Bessent and Barrasso defend it

+

On Sunday, a bipartisan group of senators released a statement urging the administration to “immediately reverse course” on Friday’s deal with Moscow to import Russian diesel. The Republicans were Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski, Thom Tillis and John Curtis; the Democrats included Jeanne Shaheen and Richard Blumenthal. Outlets disagree on the count: AP says six senators, while CNN and The Hill say seven and include Ron Wyden. The senators say the deal runs counter to a 2022 law barring Russian energy imports until Moscow ends its aggression against Ukraine. They also say the administration did not consult Congress or submit the justification the law requires. Tillis, who is retiring, said “there’s a legitimate case for saying the president’s not following the law.” Murkowski said Trump should use the sanctions law against Russia, not around it.

Trump says Russia will supply more than 300,000 tons of diesel immediately, 500,000 tons in November and 1 million tons after that. A Treasury general license exempts Russian diesel loaded onto tankers as of Friday from US sanctions until April 2027. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent defended the deal on Fox News on Sunday, saying the administration has the legal authority and the deal is good for Americans because it will lower fuel prices. Senate Majority Whip John Barrasso told CBS’s “Face the Nation” that Trump is focused on costs for consumers, farmers and truckers. The fight comes three weeks before the Nov. 3 midterms and a day after Trump said Ukraine needs “a new leader who can make a deal” and that Zelensky “better damn well stop” hitting Russian refineries. Energy analysts quoted by Fortune and Al Jazeera doubt the volumes will move pump prices much.

The Left-Leaning Take

Mother Jones argues that Trump is blaming Ukraine for a fuel-price spike driven mainly by his own Iran war and the closure of Hormuz. On this view, relief for Russian diesel is a pre-midterm political patch paid for by Kyiv. (Mother Jones, this month; no in-window Slate, Vox or Guardian opinion piece on the senators’ statement found.)

The Right-Leaning Take

No National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason piece on the deal turned up in-window. The pro-administration case, from Bessent and Barrasso, is that cheaper diesel for farmers and truckers comes first and existing law allows it. The opposing case comes from the GOP senators themselves and Rep. Don Bacon, who called Russian fuel “the wrong answer.” Intra-right disagreement: The Bulwark, from the anti-Trump right, calls the deal disgraceful and says Zelensky is “striking Putin where it hurts most”; this is not the mainstream-right position.

Pentagon · Death Penalty

Trump says he’ll “make a decision” on Hegseth’s plan to livestream the Fort Hood shooter’s firing-squad execution, as GOP and church critics pile on

+

On Saturday, Trump stepped back from fully endorsing Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s announcement that the execution of Nidal Hasan will be livestreamed. Hasan is the former Army major who killed 13 people at Fort Hood in 2009. “There are two thoughts: don’t show it and show it. So I’ll make a decision ultimately,” Trump said. He added that he “wasn’t involved in that decision” but understood it. The Pentagon says Trump approved the use of an Army firing squad. Hegseth set the execution for 1 p.m. on Dec. 3 at Fort Hood, the same day of the week and hour as the attack. If it is livestreamed, it would be the first public execution in the US in nearly a century and the first ever streamed.

CNN reports that Army and Pentagon officials were caught off guard by the announcement. Republican critics include Sen. Thom Tillis (“beyond disgusting”), Sen. John Curtis and Rep. Austin Scott, who said livestreamed executions are “what jihadists do.” Vice President Vance said he would not watch. Sen. Mark Kelly called for Hegseth’s firing. Archbishop Paul Coakley, president of the US Conference of Catholic Bishops, called the plan “a barbaric promotion of a culture of death.”

The Left-Leaning Take

Mother Jones calls it a “snuff film,” and Slate argues Hegseth chose a uniquely unsympathetic prisoner to make a publicity stunt hard to oppose. Both say the spectacle risks making Hasan a martyr while doing nothing for deterrence. (Pieces ran earlier this week, after Hegseth’s announcement.)

The Right-Leaning Take

Unusually, the mainstream right is largely opposed. National Review’s Corner calls the plan disgusting, arguing that the death of a human being, even a deserved one, is not for show. The Dispatch’s “Showtime in Pete Hegseth’s America” frames it as postliberal performance politics. Reason questions what a state-run stream is for. Intra-right disagreement: The Bulwark, from the anti-Trump right, calls it Hegseth’s “pathetic tough-guy act.” (Pieces ran Oct. 9–10.)

Sources: Washington Post, Oct 10, CNN, Oct 10, NPR, Oct 9, NBC News, Mother Jones, Slate, National Review, The Dispatch, Reason, Oct 9, The Bulwark

Ukraine · Russia

Trump declares a Russia–Ukraine “energy ceasefire” Kyiv learned of on social media, and within hours Russia hits Kharkiv’s power plant

+

On Sunday, Trump posted on Truth Social that an “ENERGY CEASEFIRE” between Russia and Ukraine was “effective immediately” and that both sides had agreed. He gave no terms, the White House did not clarify the post, and Moscow did not immediately comment. Zelensky said he learned of it through social media and was waiting for details. Hours later, Russia struck Kharkiv’s combined heat-and-power plant (CHP-5) at least twice, setting it on fire; Kyiv’s presidential office confirmed the timing. Euronews also reported a drone hit on a power plant in Kyiv, causing outages, though accounts differ on that strike. Zelensky said Ukraine backs an energy truce and would stop hitting Russian refineries if Russia stops attacking Ukraine’s grid. He said Russia carried out more than 200 strikes on Ukrainian power generation and transmission in the past week.

Analysis: The announcement follows Trump’s diesel deal with Moscow and his Saturday demand that Kyiv stop hitting refineries. That makes the “ceasefire” look like it is mainly about protecting Russian fuel output, which matters to US pump prices before the midterms. Trump made a similar claim in mid-September that neither side confirmed. Until the Kremlin signs on, Ukraine bears the risk: if it stops hitting refineries, Russia keeps a free hand against Ukraine’s grid going into winter. CNN reports that European capitals see the diesel deal as driving a wedge through the Western alliance.

Iran War · Diplomacy

Iran calls Washington’s reply to its seven conditions “insufficient” and says Hormuz stays shut until they are met

+

On Sunday, Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council, said the US had accepted some of Tehran’s seven conditions but its response was “insufficient,” with other points left unclear. He said the Strait of Hormuz would not reopen to the US, and there would be no negotiations, until all the conditions are met. He said Washington had sent proposals through Pakistan, Qatar and Iraq, and that Tehran would not return to the previous format of talks. The conditions include lifting the US blockade, releasing frozen Iranian funds and ending attacks on all fronts, including Israel’s campaign in Lebanon. Together they amount roughly to a return to June’s memorandum of understanding. Trump had earlier called Iran’s seven-day plan “not acceptable.”

Analysis: Hormuz is still closed, and that keeps the fuel-price squeeze on, which explains much of the week’s other news, including the Russian diesel deal. A growing Houthi threat to the Red Sea, after the strike on Riyadh’s airport that the State Department blamed on the group, gives Tehran another source of leverage. This search found no official US response to Rezaei, and most quotes come through Iranian state media. Treat Tehran’s description of what Washington accepted as unverified.

Press Freedom · Justice Department

The Justice Department opens an antitrust probe of ABC, CBS, NBC, CNN and Fox over the White House TV pool’s coverage boycott

+

On Saturday night, the Justice Department confirmed it is investigating the five networks that make up the White House television pool (ABC, CBS, NBC, CNN and Fox News) under the Sherman Act. The New York Times reported it first. A DOJ official said civil investigative demands went out on Friday. According to the Times, they seek “communications and materials related to the White House TV pool.” A department spokesperson said: “Group boycotts among commercial competitors can violate the Sherman Act… The Antitrust Division is simply investigating to determine whether these news organizations have violated the antitrust laws.”

The probe stems from last month’s press-access fight. After the White House barred CNN, MS NOW and Politico in mid-September, the TV pool stopped covering pool events. A federal judge then found the ban likely unconstitutional and temporarily halted it. Credentials were restored on Sept. 24, and pool coverage has resumed. NBC, CBS and CNN declined to comment. ABC said it had not yet been notified. Jonathan Kanter, the Biden-era antitrust chief, said the pool’s coordination is “no more an antitrust violation than the reporters coming together to form the pool in the first place.” Bruce Brown of the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press called the probe “an even more brazen attempt to try to intimidate journalists” and a pretext for seeking records.

The Left-Leaning Take

No in-window Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion piece turned up yet; the story broke late Saturday. Press-freedom groups quoted in news coverage make the argument the left is likely to press. They say antitrust law is being turned into a tool to punish coverage decisions, and that the real goal is discovery into newsroom communications. (No listed opinion-outlet pieces found.)

The Right-Leaning Take

No in-window National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason piece turned up. The administration’s case, in the DOJ statement, is that competitors jointly refusing to cover events is a textbook “group boycott” and deserves the same scrutiny as any other industry. International Law · The ICC

Allies and the UN close ranks around the International Criminal Court after Washington sanctions the court itself

+

On Friday the State Department designated the International Criminal Court itself under Executive Order 14203. Until now US sanctions had targeted only individual judges and prosecutors. The designation blocks court assets under US jurisdiction and generally bars Americans from dealing with it. A Treasury general license gives about 180 days to wind down business, with carve-outs for operations, IT and cloud services, pensions and detainee matters. State says the window gives the court’s 125 member states time to show “meaningful progress” on US concerns. Those concerns are the Afghanistan probe of US personnel and the warrants for Israeli leaders; neither the US nor Israel is a party to the Rome Statute. Rubio said the US would keep working to “dismantle the ICC piece by piece”; some outlets render it “brick by brick.”

Reactions came through Saturday. UN Secretary-General Guterres called the move “a serious blow” to international criminal justice. The ICC called it “unprecedented.” The European Commission called it “deeply regrettable,” and Council President Costa pledged political, financial and practical support. Belgium urged the EU to activate its Blocking Statute, and the Dutch government, as host country, pledged to defend the court. Foreign ministers of the UK, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Denmark, Canada and Japan said they “strongly disagree.”

Analysis: Sanctioning the institution makes the court’s banks, cloud providers and IT contractors the real pressure point. The test over the next six months is whether European firms follow US sanctions law or an EU Blocking Statute that forbids them to. The timing, hours after a former ICC judge won the Nobel Peace Prize, makes the confrontation more pointed (see Politics).

Israel & Palestine

Abbas postpones the first Palestinian elections in two decades to 2027, as Gaza’s ceasefire turns one with the killing still not over

+

Palestinian Authority President Mahmoud Abbas issued a decree, reported Saturday by the official Wafa agency, moving Legislative Council elections from Nov. 28, 2026 to Sept. 11, 2027. The new date is combined with a presidential vote. AP says the decree was signed Friday. The PA cited Israeli military operations, settlement expansion and settler violence, restrictions in the West Bank and East Jerusalem, and Israel’s withholding of tax revenues. The PLO Executive Committee backed the delay. Critics note that Abbas, 90, was elected in 2005 to a four-year term and now rules by decree. Israel’s foreign minister accused him of “fooling the international community.”

The decree came on the first anniversary of the US-brokered Gaza ceasefire. Gaza’s Health Ministry reported 9 people killed in the previous 24 hours, including a child, and says more than 1,480 have been killed since the truce began; Israel says it targeted militants. Jared Kushner called the situation “far from perfect,” pointing to the return of all hostages and a rise in aid, but said “there is still hard work ahead.” Hamas disarmament, agreed in principle in July, has not happened, and Israel still holds roughly two-thirds of the Strip behind the “Yellow Line.” Little movement is expected before Israel’s Oct. 27 election.

Tech · AI Safety

Microsoft’s Satya Nadella calls for an “emergency brake” on advanced AI: assume the model is compromised, and keep the controls outside it

+

In a post on X on Saturday, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella laid out what he called the need for an emergency brake on AI models. “We must assume a model is compromised and contain it from the start,” he wrote. He listed four principles. An authorized human should always be able to pause or shut down a model mid-task. The model should be kept separate from the “harness” that orchestrates its work. Controls and safeguards should sit outside the model. And every meaningful action should leave “tamper-proof human readable evidence.”

TechCrunch notes the post comes as leading labs acknowledge more incidents in which they appeared to lose control of model behavior. It is a statement of principle, not a product or policy change. Still, it is notable for coming from the head of the company that is perhaps the biggest distributor of frontier models.

Sources: TechCrunch, Oct 10, Business Standard, Oct 11
Energy · The Grid

After Isaias, Panhandle utilities race to restore power, with outages near 90% in some counties and FPL aiming for 95% restored by Monday

+

The grid recovery from Hurricane Isaias began in earnest Saturday. Outages peaked near 950,000 customers, and Gov. DeSantis said about 448,000 Florida customers were still dark at his latest update. At 7 a.m. Saturday the share of customers without power was roughly 90% in Santa Rosa County, 89% in Holmes, 86% in Washington, 85% in Walton and 75% in Escambia. The CHELCO electric cooperative lost power to about 68,900 members, around 90% of its system. At least four deaths have been reported.

Florida Power & Light had restored more than 43,000 customers by early Saturday afternoon. It is prioritizing hospitals, 911 centers and first responders, and is targeting 95% restoration by the end of Monday. Municipal utilities, including Kissimmee Utility Authority and Orlando Utilities Commission, sent mutual-aid crews west. Distribution networks in pine-heavy, rural counties are slow to rebuild after a Category 2 storm. Co-ops serving them face the longest restoration times, which is why such storms keep testing proposals for undergrounding and microgrids.

Space · Launch

SpaceX lofts 21 more data-relay satellites for the Pentagon’s low-orbit mesh network after a five-day scrub

+

A Falcon 9 lifted off from Vandenberg Space Force Base at 12:39 a.m. PDT Saturday. It carried 21 data-transport satellites for the Space Development Agency’s Proliferated Warfighter Space Architecture, a mesh of optically linked satellites in low Earth orbit meant to move targeting and sensor data between US forces. It was the fourth of nine Tranche 1 launches and brings 84 of the planned 126 transport satellites into orbit. A first attempt on Oct. 5 was halted 58 seconds before liftoff.

The booster landed back at Vandenberg, and SpaceX cut its webcast after touchdown at the SDA’s request before confirming that all 21 satellites had deployed. Reports differ on which contractor built this batch; some credit Northrop Grumman.

MLB · ALDS Game 5

Guardians complete a comeback from 0–2 down, beating the White Sox 2–0 behind Gavin Williams’ 12 strikeouts and a Ramírez homer

+

Cleveland won the deciding Game 5 of the AL Division Series 2–0 on Saturday to take the series 3–2 after losing the first two games. José Ramírez supplied the only runs with a two-run homer in the fourth off Sean Burke, on a curveball just 0.74 feet off the ground. Per ESPN, that is the lowest pitch hit for a postseason home run in the pitch-tracking era. Gavin Williams, who earned the save in Game 4, struck out 12 in a scoreless start, tying Charles Nagy’s franchise postseason record. Guardians pitchers struck out 21 in all, the most ever in a winner-take-all postseason game.

Cleveland is the seventh team in the Wild Card era to come back from 0–2 in a best-of-five. It now faces Tampa Bay, which swept the Yankees, in the ALCS starting Monday in St. Petersburg.

Sources: Boston Globe (AP), Oct 10, ESPN recap, Oct 10

Arsenal

Arsenal 2–1 Leeds: Calafiori levels and substitute Bruno Guimarães strikes from distance as the champions draw level with City

+

Arsenal came from behind at the Emirates on Saturday. James Justin gave Leeds the lead on 55 minutes, and Riccardo Calafiori equalized from close range seven minutes later after a Ben White header.
