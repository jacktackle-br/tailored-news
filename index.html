Election 2026 · Final Weekend

On the last weekend before the midterms, the administration presses an Alaska criminal probe and says it will likely take the California ballot fight to the Supreme Court

+

The administration said Saturday it would likely ask the Supreme Court to make California set aside mail ballots from more than 20,000 registered voters it says are likely noncitizens, Reuters reported. A district judge and then the 9th Circuit have already refused. In Alaska, U.S. Attorney Michael Heyman confirmed “both a civil investigation and a criminal investigation” into how a second candidate named Dan Sullivan got onto the Senate ballot. The Justice Department has asked U.S. District Judge Sharon Gleason to order state officials to “preserve and segregate” every ballot that ranks Dan J. Sullivan before Monday. State lawyers say that cannot be done without breaking ballot secrecy. The department has issued dozens of subpoenas, including to the campaigns of Democrat Mary Peltola and Dan J. Sullivan. The New York Times reported this week that Peltola’s campaign helped recruit him; Peltola says she knew nothing about it. No one has been charged.

The Washington Post reports that the administration is reshaping its election lawsuits and investigations so they can continue after Tuesday. On Friday a federal judge in San Francisco barred federal pressure on election workers, and NPR notes how unusually prominent the Justice Department has been this close to a vote. The department says it is “following the facts and the law, not anyone’s political timetable.” Nearly 50 million ballots have been cast early. All 435 House seats and 35 Senate seats are on the ballot, and Republicans hold the Senate 53–47. The Post says the competitive map is wider than either party expected. Trump spent Saturday at Mar-a-Lago, reinstated rallies he had cancelled, and is due in Kansas and Iowa on Sunday.

Washington · Courts

D.C. and Virginia sue to stop Trump’s 250-foot triumphal arch days before construction is due to begin

+

On Saturday the District of Columbia and Virginia filed a federal suit in Washington to block President Trump’s planned triumphal arch near the Potomac. The defendants are the National Park Service and the Interior Department. Attorneys general Brian Schwalb (D.C.) and Jay Jones (Virginia) argue that the project lacks the congressional authorization required under the Commemorative Works Act. They also say the 250-foot arch would far exceed the 130-foot limit in the Height of Buildings Act. The suit says construction would snarl traffic, add pollution and interfere with emergency services. The administration had planned to break ground on Nov. 5.

A separate suit by a Vietnam War veteran and others is already pending, and the two jurisdictions had raised objections earlier in October. A White House spokesperson defended the arch as a tribute to American service members. The case now turns on whether a judge will pause work before Thursday.

Iran · Hormuz

Trump says remaining Iranian sites could be hit “in 10 minutes” as Tehran floats a two-day Hormuz reopening, on its own terms

+

Speaking to reporters at a Halloween event at Mar-a-Lago on Saturday, Trump said the U.S. still has to “take care” of one or two Iranian sites, naming the suspected underground nuclear complex at Pickaxe Mountain near Natanz. “We can do that in 10 minutes,” he said. He also said Washington is in no hurry, and he called a question about whether fighting resumes next week “stupid.” Secretary of State Marco Rubio said no decision has been made. Reuters reported that Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei told commanders any threat must be met with an immediate response that has no geographic limits.

Meanwhile, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said talks with Qatari mediators had cut Iran’s proposed timeline for reopening the Strait of Hormuz from five days to two. The offer applies only once Washington accepts Tehran’s demands, which include lifting the blockade of its ports, releasing frozen assets and waiving oil sanctions. He called a letter from Vice President Vance a “positive step” but said Iran sees “no need to meet with the Americans at this stage.” Trump said the letter was only about getting medical supplies into Iran. At sea, Qatar’s Nakilat said its LNG carrier Al Mayeda was hit by “unidentified objects” in the Gulf on Friday evening. No one was hurt and no one has claimed the attack. That a ship belonging to the mediator was hit shows how exposed Gulf shipping remains.

G20 · Diplomacy

G20 foreign ministers leave Atlanta without a joint communiqué, split over Russia’s war in Ukraine

+

The U.S.-hosted G20 foreign ministers’ meeting ended in Atlanta on Saturday without a joint statement. Differences over Russia’s war in Ukraine were the main obstacle. Secretary of State Marco Rubio instead issued a chair’s statement summarizing the talks and the areas of shared concern. At his closing press conference he stressed freedom of navigation in the Strait of Hormuz and the Black Sea, critical-mineral supply chains and food security. Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov said Moscow would not accept Ukraine becoming a “military threat” to Russia’s security interests. Polish Foreign Minister Radosław Sikorski said the lack of consensus was no surprise.

The meeting had opened with the group photo cancelled after European diplomats refused to pose with Lavrov. It closed with a document only the host signed, a sign of how hard the Miami leaders’ summit in December will be. Washington’s decision not to invite South Africa added to the friction, and it is still unclear whether Vladimir Putin will attend in Miami.

Tech · AI & the Web

OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and Anthropic agree to honor one robots.txt signal for opting out of AI training

+

Four of the biggest AI developers say they will respect a single, shared way for websites to refuse AI training. A site adds a robots.txt group under the policy token __usage_ai-training (for example, “User-Agent: __usage_ai-training” and “Disallow: /”). That one entry then tells all four companies the content may not be used to train models, instead of the site blocking each company’s crawler separately. The token comes from a voluntary IETF Internet-Draft written by Anthropic’s Kevin Kelley. It covers training only. Search indexing and crawling continue, and a site with no such entry is treated as having expressed no preference.

Rollout dates differ. Anthropic says the signal is already live, OpenAI says “in the coming months,” Microsoft says Feb. 1, 2027, and Google says early 2027. Google will treat the token as equivalent to blocking its Google-Extended crawler unless a site says otherwise. Microsoft is adding a separate control for “grounding,” meaning live retrieval for answers. For publishers fighting over training data, it is the first common opt-out the major labs have agreed on, though it does not cover content that has already been collected.

Sources: Santage AI, Oct 31, PPC Land, Gagadget
Tech · Space Computing

Google’s Suncatcher satellite runs an AI model on a data-center TPU in orbit for the first time, and the first answer is a haiku

+

Google says its Project Suncatcher prototype, launched on a Falcon 9 on Oct. 1, has produced its first AI output from orbit. Engineers sent up a prompt during a ground-station pass asking the onboard Gemma 4 model to write a haiku about being in space. The answer came down as the satellite passed over Svalbard. The spacecraft carries four data-center-class TPUs powered by roughly a kilowatt of solar panels. Only one chip runs at a time, for about 15 to 20 minutes per session, because the satellite has limited ability to shed heat.

The test matters less for the poem than for what it proves about the hardware: data-center chips can survive launch and radiation and still compute in orbit. That is the first step toward Google’s idea of solar-powered AI data centers in space. Two more satellites, with more power and optical links between them, are planned for 2027. Skeptics note that one working inference run says little about the cost, cooling and bandwidth problems that orbital data centers would face at scale.

Energy · Oil

Brent ends October up about 13%, and Big Oil’s war-driven profits nearly triple from a year ago

+

Crude rose for a fourth straight week. On Friday the expiring December Brent contract settled at $117.64 a barrel, and the new front-month January contract at $113.43. Brent gained about 13.6% in October, and WTI closed at $104.12. Traders cited the tanker attacks near Hormuz, Houthi threats against Saudi energy sites, and the risk of renewed U.S. strikes on Iran after Tuesday’s election.

NPR reports the war has produced a profit windfall. Third-quarter earnings were $15.4 billion for ExxonMobil, about double a year earlier, $10.8 billion for Shell and $9.9 billion for Chevron, up 180%. Executives said much of it will go to buybacks and dividends, and Exxon declared its largest-ever quarterly dividend. Diesel set a record in September and remains near it. Fuel prices are already a midterm issue. Exxon’s CEO has previously called windfall taxes a “misguided policy.”

Space · Launch

SpaceX closes October with the 700th Falcon 9 launch, as the company shifts its focus to Starship

+

A Falcon 9 lifted off from Space Launch Complex 4 East at Vandenberg Space Force Base at 9:04 p.m. local time on Friday carrying Starlink satellites. It was the rocket’s 700th launch and SpaceX’s 11th orbital flight of October. Spaceflight Now notes that SpaceX is beginning to wind down the Falcon program as it moves to Starship.

The next Starship test, Flight 15, has a reported target of Nov. 12 from Starbase, pending FAA approval. SpaceX has not announced a date. The 700-flight milestone, and SpaceX’s stated plan to retire Falcon, make the handoff to Starship the main story in commercial launch.

Arsenal · Liverpool Preview

Dowman’s red-card appeal fails, leaving Arsenal short on the right for today’s game at Anfield

+

An FA independent regulatory commission has rejected Arsenal’s appeal against Max Dowman’s straight red card in the Carabao Cup tie at Fleetwood. It dismissed both the wrongful-dismissal and excessive-punishment claims, so the teenager serves a three-match ban starting with today’s game at Liverpool (4:30 p.m. GMT). Arteta said the club was “very disappointed.” With Bukayo Saka also out with a thigh injury, Arsenal have no first-choice option on the right. Declan Rice, Riccardo Calafiori and Christos Tzolis are back in training. Arteta also said a new contract for Rice is “a matter of time.”

Arseblog says the forward line “must step up” if Arsenal are to win a league game at Anfield for the first time in 14 years. It notes that Arteta presented the game as an opportunity rather than a burden, and the bookmakers have Arsenal as slight favourites. Liverpool list Victor Muñoz and Dominik Szoboszlai as doubts. With City losing again on Saturday, a win would make Arsenal’s weekend a very good one. (The Athletic’s Arsenal desk was unreachable this run; corroborated via Yahoo Sports, Goal and Liverpool FC.)

College Football · SEC

No. 3 Georgia turns four Florida turnovers into a 27–8 win, played indoors in Atlanta this year

+

With Jacksonville’s EverBank Stadium under renovation, the Georgia–Florida rivalry moved to Mercedes-Benz Stadium in Atlanta. No. 3 Georgia led 24–0 at halftime and beat No. 17 Florida 27–8. The Bulldogs scored 17 points off four turnovers, outrushed the Gators 217–48, and recorded 11 tackles for loss and four sacks. Florida’s star back Jadan Baugh was held to 38 yards on 17 carries. The Gators scored only late, on a short Tramell Jones Jr. run and a two-point conversion.

It was Georgia’s sixth straight win in the series, and it puts the Bulldogs in a strong position to return to the SEC Championship Game.
