Trade · Tariffs · New Since This Morning

USTR formally proposes “excess capacity” tariffs on 14 economies, with 20% on China and about 15% for the EU and Japan; Beijing and Brussels reject the premise

+

This morning’s report was based on sources. On Wednesday the administration made the plan official. U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer published proposed Section 301 tariffs on 14 trading partners: China, the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, India, Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Cambodia, Bangladesh and Switzerland. The proposal follows an eight-month investigation of 16 economies into “structural excess capacity” in manufacturing. Mexico and Norway were investigated but left off the list for now. China faces the highest rate, a proposed 20% on most imports on top of the Section 301 duties imposed in 2018. Partners that already have deals with Washington, including the EU, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Switzerland, would generally keep the roughly 15% all-in rates they negotiated. Reported figures vary, with some outlets citing up to 20% for the EU and up to 25% for India (17% if it signs a trade deal). Greer said the rates would mostly work as a top-up to existing duties rather than being added in full. None of the tariffs is in effect yet. A notice-and-comment period and a public hearing on Jan. 25 come first, and outlets disagree on whether duties could start in January or only after the hearing.

Outlets widely describe the move as rebuilding the global tariffs the Supreme Court struck down earlier this year. Unlike the emergency-powers statute behind those tariffs, Section 301 explicitly authorizes tariffs. China’s Commerce Ministry called “overcapacity” “a false proposition” and warned against using it “as a pretext for political manipulation.” The timing matters because the U.S.-China trade truce expires on Jan. 10. European Commission spokesman Olof Gill said the bloc “strongly rejects” the idea that Europe is a source of structural overcapacity. The EU also warned that extra duties would undercut its existing trade deal with the U.S.

White House · Health Care

Trump calls Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed a “smart guy” and says he will meet him about “TrumpCare,” a rebranded single-payer plan

+

In an interview with the New York Post published Tuesday, President Trump said “I know I’ll be meeting with him” about Michigan Democratic Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed’s offer to name his universal health care plan “TrumpCare.” Trump called El-Sayed a “smart guy” and suggested there might be middle ground. No date has been set. The two clashed during the campaign. El-Sayed has said he “really doesn’t care” what the program is called if it passes, and he told The Hill “I’m happy to chat.” Over the weekend Trump reposted El-Sayed’s image of gold-plated “Trumpcare” cards, and Vice President Vance’s office had already contacted El-Sayed’s office about a meeting.

The administration has not committed to any policy. A White House spokesperson called the proposal a “government takeover” and suggested the name “Medicare for None,” which echoes the line Trump used against single-payer in 2018. Trump said as recently as August that Medicare for All “doesn’t work.” Coverage notes that El-Sayed’s version would keep a role for private insurance, unlike the Sanders bill.

No in-window piece from this brief’s usual left-leaning outlets turned up. Opinion writers at MS NOW, which is outside that usual list, call the “TrumpCare” offer “a smart idea”: a way to tie a progressive goal to Trump’s appetite for credit and to test whether his populism on health care is real.

Iran · Strait of Hormuz · Gulf

Iran says more tankers hit mines in Hormuz and tells ships to coordinate passage with its forces, while U.S. Central Command says the main lanes are clear

+

Iran’s Revolutionary Guards Navy said in a statement carried by state media that two oil tankers struck naval mines, exploded and caught fire in the Strait of Hormuz on Wednesday while using an “unauthorised route,” according to Reuters. This follows similar Iranian claims on Tuesday. Iranian Army spokesperson Mohammad Akraminia said the military had deployed new surveillance and control systems in the strait. He warned that ships passing without coordinating with Iranian forces had faced greater risk and had struck mines in recent weeks. Iranian outlets give higher and inconsistent counts, including a third tanker and an LNG carrier. None of the strikes has been independently verified. U.S. Central Command has rejected the mine claims and says American forces have cleared all internationally recognized primary shipping routes. Tehran says it will not restore normal shipping until its demands are met, including lifting the U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports and ending the war “on all fronts.” Under a Qatari-mediated proposal, Iran would reopen the strait within 48 hours of those conditions being met.

Analysis. Disputed mine claims serve Tehran whether or not they are true. They raise insurers’ risk estimates and push shippers toward Iran’s preferred “coordinated” corridor, which would give Iran a form of control over the strait without a declared closure. The fighting along the Gulf’s other edge is also widening. Al Jazeera and AFP report that a Houthi drone hit a tower in Riyadh’s King Abdullah Financial District on Tuesday, injuring 11. The Saudi-led coalition then struck Yemen’s Hodeidah port, and the Houthis threatened Jeddah and other Saudi Red Sea ports. That Yemen thread is reported here only from regional outlets and AFP-based copy; no Tier-1 desk version was available.

White House · Nvidia · Congress

Trump says a pardon for Jensen Huang is “always an option” as a Post analysis counts 44 Nvidia trades in his portfolio and GOP senators join the criticism

+

In a New York Post interview published Tuesday, President Trump said a pardon for Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang is “always an option, I guess, but I don’t think it’ll be necessary.” He also said Huang should not need to invoke the Fifth Amendment when he testifies to Congress. No criminal charges against Huang have been publicly announced, and Trump did not say what offense a pardon might cover. Also on Tuesday, a Washington Post review of Trump’s financial disclosures counted 44 trades of Nvidia stock in his accounts between January and September 2026: roughly $6 million to $17 million in purchases and $4 million to $12 million in sales. Disclosures report only ranges and run only through Sept. 30. The White House says third-party institutions manage the president’s portfolio independently.

Criticism from Republicans grew. Coverage syndicated on Wednesday reported that at least six GOP senators had objected to Huang’s reinstatement, which is unusual for Senate Republicans. Most aimed at Nvidia or at the administration’s assurances rather than at Trump himself. Senate Intelligence Committee Chairman Tom Cotton said “Congress has much to investigate,” and Sen. Pete Ricketts said Huang’s return does not settle questions about national security or chips that may be unaccounted for. Huang is due for closed-door questioning by three House committees on Dec. 1 and for a public Senate Banking Committee hearing. Democrats, who take control of the House in January, have said they will look into possible corruption involving Nvidia and senior administration officials.

The Left-Leaning Take

Mother Jones asked whether this is “the most important scandal ever.” Its reporting this month has focused on Trump’s repeated Nvidia trades and on Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick’s former firm, Cantor Fitzgerald, which reportedly held large Nvidia positions. Slate treats it mainly as a national-security scandal. It reports that the administration knew last year about the woman’s alleged ties to Chinese intelligence and still let Nvidia sell chips to her company, and it suggests Trump’s holdings may have shaped his intervention. Both pieces ran earlier this month, before Tuesday’s developments.

The Right-Leaning Take

The mainstream right is mostly critical. Reason (Nov. 23) argues that Trump “barges into Nvidia’s internal decisions” and opens the door to more federal meddling in the chip business. National Review argues that political cover does not remove Nvidia’s legal exposure or the board’s duty to shareholders. In The Dispatch, Jack Kubinec argues that Huang’s return will cost the company. The Wall Street Journal editorial board, as quoted by The Hill, warned that the intervention “will have legal and political costs.” The Bulwark, representing the anti-Trump right, goes further and argues that the episode plants “the seeds of impeachment.” That is an intra-right dissent, not the mainstream conservative view.

Trade · Tariffs · Section 301

Administration readies new tariffs on China, the EU and 14 other economies over subsidized “excess” production, AP sources say

+

According to people familiar with the plans who spoke to the Associated Press, the Trump administration is expected soon to announce higher tariffs on China, the European Union and 14 other economies. Its argument is that subsidies in those countries drive excess manufacturing output that floods the U.S. with cheap goods. The U.S. Trade Representative’s office is expected to release the findings of its Section 301 investigation into industrial subsidies in the coming days. Rates and timing had not been set. The Wall Street Journal, as cited in other coverage, reported that Japan, India, Vietnam and South Korea are among the 16 economies under investigation.

The move is the administration’s second attempt to replace the emergency-powers tariffs the Supreme Court struck down in February. Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 sets no statutory cap on tariff rates. Any new duties would come on top of the 10% to 12.5% Section 301 tariffs imposed earlier this year, which are being challenged at the Court of International Trade.

The Left-Leaning Take

Search turned up no in-window piece on the planned tariffs from Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion, so none is summarized here.

The Right-Leaning Take

Free-market conservatives and libertarians have opposed this approach for months, though search turned up no response to Tuesday’s report. In Reason and at its Volokh Conspiracy blog, legal scholars have argued since summer that Section 301 covers specific discriminatory practices and does not authorize broad tariffs, calling earlier rounds “massively harmful and illegal.” The Dispatch’s markets newsletter called an earlier round “sham tariffs” built on a pretext. These are earlier pieces, not reactions to this week’s report.

Pacific · Australia · China

Solomon Islands names Australia its “primary security partner” in a new treaty that bars foreign bases, and is reported to be letting its China pact lapse

+

Australia and Solomon Islands signed a security and economic treaty in Canberra on Wednesday. It names Australia the Pacific nation’s “primary security partner” and states that “there will be no foreign military or security base” on Solomon Islands soil. Australia commits about A$740 million over a decade, including money for free education. Honiara has reportedly told Beijing it will not renew the divisive 2022 security pact with China, which expires in April. Opposition leader Manasseh Sogavare, who signed that pact, criticized how fast the new treaty was pushed through and said the status of the China arrangement remained “unclear.”

Analysis: the 2022 deal set off alarms in Canberra and Washington that China could gain a military foothold about 2,000 km from Australia, and this treaty is the clearest reversal yet. China’s foreign ministry said its cooperation with Pacific island countries involves “no element of geopolitical rivalry or bloc confrontation.” Reports differ on how final the break with China is: Australia’s ABC says the pact has been scrapped, while AP and others describe a decision not to renew it.

Alliances · Canada · NATO

Pentagon accuses Canada of putting “parochial” commercial interests ahead of defense; Ottawa says it is on track for NATO’s 5% target

+

In a fact sheet released Tuesday, the Pentagon sharply criticized Canada. It said Ottawa “has yet to make the hard decisions and tradeoffs needed” to meet its NATO spending pledge and to be a credible partner in defending North America. It accused Canada of favoring “parochial economic and commercial interests,” including in its review of the F-35 purchase, over military effectiveness. It also said only one of Canada’s four submarines is seaworthy and that Ottawa is putting off its biggest spending increases until after the 2029 election. The Pentagon threatened further, unspecified measures. Spokesperson Sean Parnell said intelligence sharing, NATO work and North American air defense were not affected.

Canadian defense ministry spokesman Simon Lafortune said Canada is “on track” to reach NATO’s new 5%-of-GDP target by 2035 and has committed C$81.8 billion over five years to rebuild its forces. Defence Minister David McGuinty said military cooperation has not changed. Analysis: Kerry Buck, Canada’s former ambassador to NATO, told the CBC that there is “obviously more at play” than spending. Coverage ties the dispute to the wider U.S.–Canada trade war and to Trump’s talk of making Canada the 51st state.

Space · China · Pakistan

Shenzhou-24 docks with Tiangong carrying Pakistan’s Muhammad Zeeshan Ali, the first foreign astronaut aboard China’s space station

+

China’s Shenzhou-24 lifted off from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center on Wednesday morning, Beijing time. Its crew is commander Tang Hongbo, flight engineer Wu Yi and Pakistani astronaut Muhammad Zeeshan Ali, and they entered the Tiangong station at 2:35 p.m., about six hours after launch. Ali is the first non-Chinese astronaut to visit Tiangong. He is on a short stay, reported as roughly one week to 10 days depending on the source, to run experiments before returning on the outgoing Shenzhou-23 capsule. His Chinese crewmates take over the station from the Shenzhou-23 crew.

Pakistan’s prime minister called the flight a national milestone. For Beijing, it is the first step in turning Tiangong into a platform for international partners at a time when the International Space Station is heading toward retirement and the U.S. and China are competing to return crews to the Moon.

Tech · Regulation · Australia

Australia passes a “digital duty of care” law that exposes Big Tech to multibillion-dollar fines for foreseeable online harms

+

Australia’s Parliament passed online safety legislation on Wednesday that requires large platforms to take reasonable steps to prevent foreseeable harm to users. Companies that fail face fines reported at up to 5% of global revenue. The Senate vote was 36 to 23. Because no party holds a Senate majority, the government needed a deal with the Greens. The law replaces the current industry codes with a broader duty-of-care obligation after a 12-month transition period, and Google and Meta are among the companies expected to be covered.

Opposition leader Angus Taylor called the law “authoritarian” and pledged to repeal it if his coalition wins the 2028 election. The U.S. ambassador had warned that it would “impose disproportionate compliance burdens on American companies.” This is Canberra’s second attempt: a 2024 plan to fine platforms over misinformation was dropped after it ran into opposition in the Senate.

Sources: AP via WSLS, Nov 25, AP via KSAT, Nov 25, Al Jazeera, Nov 24
Electrification · EVs · Grid

Volkswagen’s Elli will pay German EV owners up to €720 a year to stay plugged in, in a mass-market vehicle-to-grid launch

+

Elli, Volkswagen’s charging subsidiary, will launch a vehicle-to-grid package for German customers in December. It pairs an 11 kW bidirectional DC wallbox, priced at €1,995 before installation, with two green-power tariffs. Owners earn €0.24 for each eligible hour their car stays connected, with sessions of at least three hours, whether or not power actually flows. That works out to about €60 a month, or up to €720 in the first contract year. Electrek reports that Elli expects to add payments of about €0.40 per kWh for power fed back to the grid later on; other outlets describe that feed-in tariff as part of the launch package.

Volkswagen says about 360,000 of its cars in Germany already have the hardware groundwork for bidirectional charging, across VW, Škoda and Cupra models. The Mobility House will pool the cars and sell their flexibility into power markets. A 20-household pilot logged more than 6,500 charge and discharge sessions. BMW and E.ON already sell a rival home V2G package for the iX3. Together these mark a shift from pilots to products that let parked EV batteries help balance the grid.

Sources: Electrek, Nov 24, Volkswagen Group press release

Arsenal 1–1 Dortmund: Gyökeres’s long-range strike is cancelled out by Beier despite 30 Arsenal shots, and Arteta calls it a “brilliant performance”

+

Arsenal drew 1–1 with Borussia Dortmund at the Emirates on Tuesday in the fifth round of the Champions League league phase. Arsenal stay unbeaten in Europe with 11 points from five games, inside the top eight, and play Real Madrid next in the competition. Next up in the league is Manchester City. After City’s draw on Tuesday, Rayan Cherki said “we go to London to win the game.” The Athletic’s Arsenal coverage could not be reached for corroboration; the facts are backed by ESPN, Sky Sports and Arsenal.com.
