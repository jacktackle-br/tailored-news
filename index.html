White House · Diplomacy

Kimberly Guilfoyle resigns as ambassador to Greece after the White House asks her to go. The news came at new press secretary Katie Zacharia’s first briefing

+

New White House press secretary Katie Zacharia held her first briefing Tuesday and said the administration had asked Kimberly Guilfoyle, the U.S. ambassador to Greece, to resign. Guilfoyle sent her formal resignation later in the day. She calls the allegations against her false and says she will defend her reputation. The reports against her say she sought money for access to administration officials before she was confirmed, took lavish gifts as ambassador, and brought a lobbyist for a Greek construction company into official meetings. One donor, Eric Deters, says she asked him to pay a $100,000 credit-card bill days before her July 2025 confirmation hearing. The Wall Street Journal first reported that claim, and her lawyers deny it. On Monday Trump said he was “looking at” firing her. On Tuesday he said he “didn’t like what I read.” House Foreign Affairs Democrats said resigning “does not shield her from accountability.” A source told reporters the State Department inspector general is investigating.

Zacharia replaced Karoline Leavitt and previously advised Trump Media & Technology Group. Her debut briefing ran under 15 minutes. NBC and PBS reported that she took questions from only three outlets: Breitbart, Fox News and the Daily Mail.

Courts · Washington

Judge Reyes extends the East Potomac tree-cutting ban to Oct. 26 and orders the Interior aide behind the cuts to testify Monday

+

On Tuesday, U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes extended her temporary restraining order on tree removal at East Potomac Golf Links through Oct. 26. Before that, it had been due to expire at 2 p.m. Tuesday. The government must now give the court and the plaintiffs three business days’ written notice before making any permanent changes to the course. Frank Lands, the Park Service’s deputy director for operations, testified that he learned of the cutting plan on Oct. 5 at the direction of the Interior Department. He said the order came from Gregory Wischer, deputy chief of staff to Interior Secretary Doug Burgum.

Wischer could not be reached to appear Tuesday, so Reyes ordered him and the Park Service’s Jessica Bowron to give sworn testimony on Monday, Oct. 19. Bowron signed off on the tree plan a day after declaring under oath that the renovation plans were still conceptual. Reyes called that “double speak.” The government argued that Wischer’s testimony was irrelevant. Reyes answered that it goes to “whether or not I’ve been lied to.”

The Left-Leaning Take

No in-window Mother Jones, Vox, Slate or Guardian opinion piece found. Left-leaning outlets such as Raw Story and Crooks and Liars cast the hearing as a judge catching the administration in apparent defiance and regretting that no one has been punished yet. (Not listed outlets.)

The Right-Leaning Take

No in-window National Review, Dispatch, WSJ editorial or Reason piece found. In court, the government says this is a lawful renovation of federal parkland and that questioning a senior Interior aide is unnecessary. (Government’s argument only.)

Saudi Arabia · Yemen

Saudi coalition bombs the Houthis’ biggest fuel hub; more missiles and drones fly at Riyadh as the U.S. limits its staff’s use of Saudi airports

+

The Saudi-led coalition said Tuesday that it struck Al-Sabaha Central Station, the Houthis’ largest fuel-distribution hub in northern Yemen. The coalition says the site holds about 58,000 tonnes and supported attacks on the kingdom and on Red Sea shipping. The Houthi-run Yemen Oil Company said the depot held no fuel. The Houthis say two civilians were killed, and the coalition says it warned civilians to leave. Hours later the coalition said it destroyed three cruise missiles and two drones aimed at the Riyadh region and the south. Saudi authorities said falling debris injured at least four people. The State Department now requires U.S. government staff to get chief-of-mission approval before using any Saudi airport. Riyadh’s King Khalid International stays closed through Oct. 17.

Analysis: The Washington Post reports that Mohammed bin Salman is trapped between security risks and his economic ambitions. The U.S. is sharing intelligence and helping plan targets but has not struck the Houthis itself, and Trump seems reluctant to commit U.S. air power. That could push Riyadh toward talks with the Houthis rather than an open-ended air war. The Houthis have threatened the U.S. with a “swift and painful” response if it joins in.

Russia · Ukraine

Russia’s overnight strike report lists no energy targets for the first time since Trump’s “truce”; the Kremlin says only that contacts continue

+

Russia’s Defence Ministry published its daily list of overnight strikes on Tuesday, and it named no Ukrainian energy facilities. United Kingdom · Iran

MI5 chief says Iran is moving its UK plots toward military sites, and the suspected RAF Fairford plot “is not unique”

+

In his annual threat update on Tuesday, MI5 Director General Ken McCallum said Iran’s ambitions had made a “major shift this year” toward military sites, intelligence facilities and other well-guarded targets. He linked this to the suspected plot against RAF Fairford, the British base used by U.S. bombers. Iran-linked operations led to more than 50 arrests in the past year, many of them aimed at Jewish communities. McCallum said Tehran still relies mostly on low-level criminal proxies in Britain that cannot mount a sophisticated, large-scale attack. He warned that the Revolutionary Guard is more capable closer to home. He again called Russia “aggressor number one.”

Analysis: The speech ties the Iran war directly to the safety of allied bases. The UK government says the Fairford plot was almost certainly directed by the IRGC. Tehran denies it and has asked for evidence, and officials have not publicly laid out the link.

Justice Department · Midterms

Justice Department says the Omar search didn’t break its pre-election rule because it “was not meant to be public”; Raskin says it plainly did

+

The day after Homeland Security Investigations agents searched Rep. Ilhan Omar’s Washington-area home, the dispute has moved to DOJ’s own election-year rules. Department practice has long been to avoid overt investigative steps against candidates within about 60 days of an election, and the Justice Manual bars choosing the timing of an action to affect one. A person familiar with the matter told CNBC the department does not think the search violated that policy because it “was not meant to be public.” Rep. Jamie Raskin, the top Democrat on House Judiciary, said DOJ broke its own rule, which he described as requiring it to abstain from overt actions in politically sensitive matters in the 60 days before an election. CBS reported, citing sources, that Attorney General Todd Blanche knew of the search in advance and that the Public Integrity Section was consulted.

DOJ has said only that a judge authorized the warrant as part of an “ongoing federal criminal investigation.” People familiar with the probe say it concerns immigration paperwork filed by Omar’s late father when she was a child; no charges have been filed. Trump said he had “nothing to do with it.” Republican reaction has split. Minnesota Rep. Tom Emmer posted “Nobody is above the law… ACCOUNTABILITY IS COMING!” Top GOP statewide candidates in Minnesota either declined to comment or kept their statements guarded. Omar is on the ballot Nov. 3.

The left’s argument, voiced by House Democratic leaders and Raskin, is that the search is “corrupt weaponization” of federal law enforcement against a Trump critic, timed weeks before an election. It also holds that DOJ’s “not meant to be public” defense ignores the obvious fact that searching a sitting member’s home becomes public. (No listed opinion-outlet pieces found.)

Tech · AI Chips

Microsoft unveils Maia 300, a 2nm in-house AI chip it plans to make the main engine for Copilot and its other AI services from 2027

+

Microsoft’s third-generation accelerator is built for inference: running trained models, not training them. The company says it will scale the Maia family to “multi-gigawatt deployments” starting next year and make it “the primary inference system for our own products and services.” Reported specs include TSMC’s N2P 2nm-class process, a multi-chiplet design and eight HBM4 memory stacks. Microsoft claims about 40% more tokens per watt than leading GPU systems and up to seven times the throughput of a Maia 200 rack. The chip will run OpenAI models, Microsoft’s own MAI models and open-source models, and Techzine reports Anthropic plans to run Claude on it too.

The performance figures are Microsoft’s own. Analysts note that Maia 200 was delayed and deployed only narrowly, that 2027 volumes are plans rather than shipments, and that Nvidia keeps an edge on some workloads. Axios notes that every major Nvidia customer (Google, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, and now OpenAI with its Broadcom-built chip) is now also a chip rival.

Science · Economics Nobel

Economics Nobel goes to Robert Hall and Michael Woodford for building the micro foundations of modern central banking

+

On Monday the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences gave the prize to Stanford’s Robert E. Hall and Columbia’s Michael Woodford “for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation.” Their models start from the decisions of individual households and firms rather than aggregate statistics alone. The committee said that approach has improved how policymakers smooth booms and busts and how central banks set and communicate interest rates. Hall’s 1978 paper showed that consumer spending tracks expectations of future income. Hall also chaired the panel that dates U.S. recessions for 46 years.

Woodford’s 2003 book Interest and Prices linked firms’ pricing decisions to inflation and monetary policy. It became the framework behind the forward guidance that central banks now use routinely. The prize is worth 12 million Swedish kronor (about $1.2 million), split between them. It closes this year’s Nobel announcements.
